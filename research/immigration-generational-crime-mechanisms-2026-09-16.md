**Verdict:** The 2018–2026 literature changes the question. In every within-family, survival and full-population design read this session, the first-to-second-generation "rise" is a first-generation floor, not a second-generation ceiling: adult-arrival immigrants offend far below everyone, their US-born children land at, not above, the native-born level in US surveys, and in European registers the ordering of "first" and "second" generation flips with age at arrival (Bersani & Pittman 2019; Inkpen 2024; Moehling & Piehl 1900–1930; Hällsten et al. 2013; Statistics Norway 2017). Two register facts come before any mechanism: sex-and-age standardization alone removes two-thirds of the Norwegian second generation's excess over natives but only two-fifths of the first generation's, and the residual after parental socioeconomic controls has been widening in Denmark and Norway since the mid-2000s. The mechanisms with A-grade causal identification are all *ceiling* mechanisms that act on children through their environment: permanent legal status from birth (German jus soli reform, treated youth crime −70%, 95% CI about −16% to −125% on 24 birth-month clusters; see the 2026-09-23 revision), parental resources and integration (Danish language-training and benefit-cut reforms move sons' convictions in opposite directions), and exposure to convicted peers (Danish and Swedish refugee dispersal), while neighbourhood deprivation itself does nothing within families. Selection, the default explanation for the floor, has never been tested on a crime-relevant trait. Cultural transmission is a live contradiction (Larsen et al. 2025 and Couttenier et al. 2019 versus Hällsten et al. 2013). The measurement channel is large and runs both ways: in linked Dutch data, self-reported offending explains only 9–16% of minority youths' overrepresentation as police suspects, yet US self-report is unbiased by generation and official records treat undocumented youth more harshly than their self-reports warrant. Twenty angles are listed in §6; the cheapest with the highest value are the ethnic-attrition correction applied to crime, the self-report-versus-official wedge as an estimand, age-standardizing every generational comparison before theorizing, and a sibling age-at-arrival design on Nordic conviction registers.

Three things follow. First, the gap is produced at the bottom: adult-arrival immigrants sit far below everyone, and their children sit at the native-born level in the two US designs that can hold the family constant or follow one cohort. The right question is what suppresses adult-arrival offending and why it does not transmit. Second, "the second generation exceeds native whites" is not supported by US individual-level data on arrest or self-reported offending; where the repo has documented a second-generation excess it is in European registers (Sweden, Denmark, Netherlands; [crime memo](immigration-crime-rates-unauthorized-vs-native-born.md) claim 8) or in US-born-by-ethnicity figures that pool second and third-plus generations and inherit ethnic-attrition bias (§6, angle 3). Third, the floor is an age-at-arrival floor. Every dataset that splits arrival age finds child arrivals near the second generation and adult arrivals far below (Moehling & Piehl; Rumbaut's decimal generations; Bersani & Pittman's footnote), and in Stockholm the childhood immigrants out-offend the Swedish-born second generation in every model (§4.1) while in Norway the Norwegian-born out-offend the immigrants (§4.2): "generation" is a proxy for years of host-country childhood, and it inverts depending on who is in the first-generation cell. Any floor mechanism therefore has to be something adults bring and children raised here do not acquire: selection on the migration decision, an origin-country adolescence, or a legal position that children born here never hold.

## 3. Evidence by mechanism, with the numbers verified

### 3.1 Permanent status from birth: the German jus soli reform

Andres, Bauernschuster, Dahl, Rainer & Schüller, "Birthright Citizenship and Youth Crime", IZA DP 18374 (2026) [SOURCE: https://docs.iza.org/dp18374.pdf, full text read this session]; the lane reports an AEA Papers & Proceedings version, DOI 10.1257/pandp.20261097 [UNVERIFIED]. The 1999 law gave children born in Germany from 1 January 2000 automatic citizenship if a parent had eight years' legal residence; parents could not opt out. Citizenship at birth among second- and third-generation migrant children rose from 22% (born July–December 1999) to 89% (born January–June 2000), a 67-point first stage; in the full population the rise was 7.1 points. Data: every police-recorded crime in Baden-Württemberg, Hesse and Berlin (about 25% of Germany), offences at ages 14 to 19 years 8 months, recorded July 2012 to February 2020, 120,441 criminal cases. Identification: the reform reclassifies the same children from "non-German" to "German" suspects, so a double difference-in-differences summed across the two nationality series recovers the behavioural change. Treatment effect −4.941 crimes per county × birth-month × year cell (SE 1.943, clustered at birth month × year), against an estimated counterfactual of 7.013: TOT −70.45%, ITT −47.2%. A Poisson model with youth population as exposure gives −0.083 (SE 0.031), about −8% of all youth crime in the population. Boys −6.225 (SE 1.974); girls +1.284 (SE 0.527), which the authors link to Dahl et al. 2022, who found the same reform lowered girls' integration and educational attainment "arguably because treated girls were pushed by parents to conform to traditional culture". By offence: property −3.077 (SE 1.424) and drug −0.901 (SE 0.333) significant; violent −0.127 (SE 0.479) not. Citizenship did not change welfare access.

What it means for the generational question. In a jus sanguinis country the second generation is the group that lacks citizenship, and giving it to them cut their offending by more than half. Part of the European second-generation excess is therefore a legal-status effect, which no jus soli country can have. This morning's answer in this session treated legal status as a falsification of the generational rise ("the second generation has full status and still offends more"); that holds for the United States and fails for Europe. The offence pattern (property and drug, not violence) matches Pinotti's Italian legalization and Gunadi's DACA property-crime result in the repo, which points to opportunity cost rather than fear. *[Qualified 2026-09-23, study audit B: Gunadi's property-crime figure is a 51-state panel association whose same design gives murder −6.8% and rape +7.0% per DACA approval per 1,000 residents, implying about 340 murders averted per 100,000 recipients a year; it is not evidence on recipients' behaviour and does not corroborate the mechanism. Pinotti's effect sits entirely in applicants with likely fictitious domestic-worker sponsorship and cannot be separated from expulsion.]*

**Precision and scope, re-read 2026-09-23.** The design handles the relabeling of suspects from
"non-German" to "German" by adding the two nationality series, and the effect sits in property and
drug crime, so it is not an artifact of nationality coding or of offences only foreigners can
commit. Its precision is weak. Standard errors are clustered on the 24 birth months of the sample,
too few clusters for conventional cluster-robust inference, and the identifying contrast is one
difference-in-differences of half-year birth blocks. The 95% interval on the treatment effect,
−4.941 ± 1.96 × 1.943, runs from −8.75 to −1.13 crimes per cell, or −125% to −16% of the
counterfactual; the ±4-month window gives −5.394 (SE 2.792), not significant at 5%. The TOT is a
ratio of two estimates (the effect and the mechanically displaced count). The police data delete
month of birth two years after a single minor incident, so the counts over-weight repeat and
serious offending. The treated children are those of parents with eight years' legal residence;
the paper says nothing about children of unauthorized parents. Read it as: citizenship at birth
lowered recorded youth property and drug crime among boys by an imprecise amount, with girls'
offending rising. [SOURCE: IZA DP 18374, pp. 3–6 and Appendix Tables 1–2, re-read 2026-09-23]

### 3.2 Legal precarity as a floor: deterrence and perceived sanction risk

Bersani, Fine, Piquero, Steinberg, Frick & Cauffman 2018, *Migration Letters* 15(2):147–166, DOI 10.33182/ml.v15i2.366 [SOURCE: open PDF, results passages read]. Crossroads Study, seven waves, first-time male offenders in three US jurisdictions; groups: undocumented, documented, second generation, US-born white, US-born Latino. Self-report: undocumented youth report the least offending before first arrest and after it (three-year cumulative prevalence 65% vs 70% documented, 77% second generation and US-born white, 78% US-born Latino; variety IRR .58 vs second generation, p = .003). Official records: undocumented youth are re-arrested more often than documented youth (IRR 2.73, 95% CI 1.05–7.11) and, the authors write, "official records reflect a marginally higher level of re-arrest among undocumented immigrants, particularly in the months immediately following the first arrest." Note that NIJ 310356's literature review, which the repo cites elsewhere, paraphrases only the self-report half of this paper.

Han, Piquero & Bersani 2024, *J. Research in Crime & Delinquency* 61(6):815–859 (Pathways to Desistance): rational-choice variables, especially perceived risk of arrest, "help to explain, in part, why first-generation immigrants are less likely to offend"; first-generation youth with a higher perceived risk of arrest report lower offending than second- and third-plus-generation youth [abstract, agent-read and parent-checked]. Piquero, Bersani, Loughran & Fagan 2016 (same sample): first-generation youth hold more positive views of the law and report more social costs of punishment [abstract].

Gunadi 2020, *J. Economic Behavior & Organization* 178:327–353 [SOURCE: RePEc abstract]: "I fail to find evidence that DACA statistically significantly affected the incarceration rate of undocumented youth"; one DACA approval per 1,000 population is associated with a 1.6% fall in property crime.

Reading: legal precarity is a floor mechanism that switches off mechanically between generation one and generation two in the United States. The evidence that it lowers offending, as distinct from lowering police contact, is B-grade (self-report in offender samples plus the perceived-risk mediation), and the same paper that supports it shows the official record moving the other way.

### 3.3 Parental resources: two Danish reforms with opposite signs

Foged, Hasager, Peri, Arendt & Bolvig 2023, *J. Public Economics* 220:104840 [SOURCE: open-access PDF, Tables 1, 4 and 5 read]. Regression discontinuity at 1 January 1999 in the admission date of refugee parents; children born 1990–2001 observed at 15–18. Baseline: 8% of refugee children convicted of property crime at 15–18 vs 3% of native children; violent 4% vs 1%; nearly one in five refugee boys convicted of something. Table 5, boys born 1996–2001 (n = 1,438), RD estimates with the sign as stated in the text ("a very large reduction in crime rates for the younger boys"; the extracted table cells lost their minus glyph): any conviction −0.160 (SE 0.080) on a base of 0.220; violent conviction −0.146 (SE 0.057) on 0.162; any charge −0.315 (SE 0.106) on 0.390; property charge −0.395 (SE 0.089) on 0.376; violent charge −0.228 (SE 0.058) on 0.229. Older boys and all girls: no significant crime effects. The point estimates approach elimination of the outcome at the cutoff and come from subsamples using the full-sample bandwidth; the authors report stability from bandwidths above two to three months and rare significance at placebo cutoffs (footnote 20). Treat the sign as A-grade and the size as unreliable.

Andersen, Dustmann & Landersø 2019, "Lowering Welfare Benefits: Intended and Unintended Consequences for Migrants and their Families", CReAM DP 05/19 and ROCKWOOL Study Paper 138 [SOURCE: open PDFs, abstract and result passages read]. Denmark's 2002 Start Aid cut benefits by about 50% for refugees granted residency after 1 July 2002. Adults: employment 10% → 19% in year one but disposable income −40%; convictions roughly double in the first year, driven by shoplifting. Children: childcare enrolment −15 points; the share scoring in the bottom tail of language tests +5 points from a 3% base; boys aged 10–13 at residency complete almost a year less schooling; for teenagers at least 14 at residency "the number of crime convictions double for basically every crime type"; by sex, shoplifting for females and both property and violent crime for males.

Reading: the parental channel is real and manipulable in both directions. What moves is family resources and integration, which is not the same thing as "family control" or "dissonant acculturation", the constructs the US survey literature uses. Both reforms act on boys.

### 3.4 Neighbourhood: peers transmit, deprivation does not

Damm & Dustmann 2014, *AER* 104(6):1806–1832 [abstract, agent-read]: Danish refugee dispersal 1986–1998; "the share of young people convicted for crimes, in particular violent crimes, in the neighborhood increases convictions of male assignees later in life. No such effects are found for other measures of neighborhood crime including the rate of committed crimes." Dustmann, Mertz & Okatenko 2023, *Economic Journal* [IZA DP 16158 abstract verified; journal DOI per lane, UNVERIFIED]: gang crime in the assigned neighbourhood raises boys' crime before 19 and girls' teenage motherhood. Grönqvist, Niknami & Robling, SOFI WP 1/2015 [SOURCE: LSE conference PDF, abstract and intro read; no published version found]: Whole-of-Sweden placement 1985–1994, boys placed at 7–14 followed to 26; one within-municipality SD of immigrant-share segregation raises drug convictions and custodial sentences 11–13%, "about one-fifth of the gap in crime between immigrants and natives for these types of offenses"; no effect on violent or property crime; concentrated among children of low-educated parents; IV for long-run exposure gives +28%.

Sariaslan, Långström, D'Onofrio, Hallqvist, Franck & Lichtenstein 2013, *Int. J. Epidemiology* 42(4):1057–1066 [SOURCE: PMC full text]. All children born in Sweden 1975–1989 living in the three largest cities at 15 (N = 297,752; 172,525 siblings in 78,570 families). Violent conviction at 15–20 per 1 SD of neighbourhood deprivation: OR 1.57 (1.52–1.63) crude; 1.09 (1.06–1.12) with observed covariates; 0.96 (0.83–1.11) within siblings; 1.03 (0.93–1.13) within cousins. Neighbourhood context accounts for 12.2% of the variance crude and 1.8% adjusted; the family context for 30.1%.

Reading: these are not in conflict once the treatment is named. Assignment to a place with more convicted young men raises a boy's later convictions; living in a poorer area, holding the family constant, does not. The deprivation index (welfare receipt, unemployment, immigrant share, education) is a composition measure; the dispersal papers identify exposure to specific people. For the generational question the peer channel is a ceiling mechanism that can only act on children raised in the host country, which is exactly the group that rises. Chen & Jiang's Add Health mediation (network peer deviance, share of second-generation friends) is the US survey analogue.

### 3.5 Selection: assumed, not measured

Bütikofer & Peri, NBER w23877 (Norwegian conscription records, cohorts born 1932–33): adaptability and cognitive ability raise the probability of migrating, "implying a strong positive selection of less educated migrants with respect to the (previously unobserved) adaptability skill" [abstract, agent-read]. Knudsen, Univ. of Copenhagen DP 19-01: 1,253,317 Scandinavian emigrants 1850–1920 linked to childhood households; individualistic households emigrated more, lowering origin-country individualism by 3.9 (Denmark) to 13.1 (Norway) points [abstract, agent-read]. Borjas, Kauppinen & Poutvaara (Danish registers): emigrants positively selected on education and earnings out of a compressed wage distribution; the Roy logic predicts reversal for high-inequality origins [summary, agent-read].

None of these measures a crime-relevant trait, and no paper tests whether second-generation means on such a trait sit between the first-generation and host means at the rate imperfect transmission predicts. Two within-family facts bound the story: mother–child offending correlations in immigrant dyads are below .20 (Bersani & Pittman), and the adjusted crime correlation between two random people from the same origin country is about .01 in Stockholm (Hällsten et al.). Selection remains the best available explanation for the adult-arrival floor because the alternatives (deterrence, an origin-country adolescence) are also only B-grade, not because it has been shown.

### 3.6 Culture: a contradiction, not a settled question

Larsen, Lenz & Mikkelborg 2025, *PNAS* 122(48) e2500535122 [SOURCE: PMC full text, Table 1 read]. Log homicide victimization of white internal US migrants regressed on the log 1933–42 homicide rate of their birth state: coefficient 0.60 for migrants vs 0.96 for non-migrants in 1959–61 (62% persistence), 0.37 vs 0.69 in 2000–17 (54%); with state-of-residence × age-group fixed effects 0.47 and 0.21 (50% and 31%); within county of residence 0.39 and 0.18. Similar for gun and non-gun homicide. A preregistered survey finds migrants from historically violent states score higher on honour ideology, aggressive responses to scripted provocations and distrust of police. The authors: "any interpretation should be made cautiously." Outcome is victimization, migrants are internal and white, and no second generation is observed.

Hällsten, Szulkin & Sarnecki 2013, *British J. Criminology* 53(3):456–481 [SOURCE: OUP abstract and DiVA preprint passages]. Stockholm cohorts completing compulsory school 1990–93 (N = 63,462) followed to ages 28–31; parental resources and neighbourhood segregation explain half to three-quarters of the male gap in registered crime and sometimes all of the female gap; the adjusted Spearman correlation in crime between random pairs from the same origin country is at most about .01 for suspicions and convictions and lower for prison, violence and incarceration, "miniscule" once family and neighbourhood are removed. van der Gaag 2019, *J. Contemporary Criminal Justice* [abstract, agent-read]: across five Western European countries, 70–80% of the migrant–native difference in self-reported offending is structural; the Western-origin group with the highest cultural alignment offends most.

Couttenier, Petrencu, Rohner & Thoenig 2019, *AER* 109(12), DOI 10.1257/aer.20170263 [SOURCE: AEA abstract page]: Swiss crime data 2009–2016; asylum-seeker cohorts exposed to civil conflict or mass killing during childhood "are 35 percent more prone to violent crime than the average cohort", strongest for early-childhood exposure and concentrated on co-nationals; "offering labor market access to asylum seekers eliminates two-thirds of the effect". First generation only.

Reading: one design says norms about violence travel with people and survive relocation, and the Swiss result says childhood conflict exposure does too; the Stockholm design says shared origin explains almost none of the variance in total registered offending among children of immigrants. All three can be right if origin experience shapes responses to conflict (and victimization) while contributing little to the volume of recorded property and drug crime that dominates registers, and if opportunity moderates it (two-thirds of the Swiss effect disappears with work rights). Nobody has tested both outcomes in one dataset (§6, angle 14).

### 3.7 Measurement and the system: the wedge runs both ways

Bersani & Piquero 2016, *J. Quantitative Criminology* 33(4):835–857, DOI 10.1007/s10940-016-9314-9 [SOURCE: RePEc abstract]: Pathways to Desistance, nearly 1,300 adjudicated youth, self-reported and official arrests compared across ten waves; "a very high degree of similarity regardless of immigrant generation. We found no evidence of systematic crime reporting bias among foreign-born, first generation immigrants compared to their US-born peers." So generational gaps in self-reported *arrest* are not an under-reporting artefact.

Bersani & Wy 2025, *Justice Quarterly*, DOI 10.1080/07418825.2025.2534405 [abstract only; paywalled on tandfonline, Sci-Hub, Exa and author pages]: NLSY97 decomposition; the explained share "is largely attributed to assimilation processes linked to family, school, and contextual factors. Notably, models explain much less of the disparity in arrests implicating potential system-generated disparities." The direction of the unexplained arrest residual is not recoverable from the abstract. [GAP: full text.]

Muchow 2024, *Criminology*, DOI 10.1111/1745-9125.12367 [SOURCE: results passages via publisher page]: California counties 1980–2004, staggered adoption of intergovernmental service agreements leasing jail beds for immigrant detention; Hispanic arrest rates +11.5% (β = .115, p = .023; about 7.6 arrests per 1,000 Hispanic residents a year), White and Black arrests unchanged; violent and property felonies unchanged, misdemeanours +11.6%; strongest where Hispanics are 11–22% of the population; fiscal-distress moderators not significant.

Together with the [bias mechanisms memo](immigration-crime-statistics-bias-mechanisms-2026-09-16.md): status misclassification and enforcement-chilled reporting understate first-generation rates; discretionary policing of Hispanics and harsher official processing of the undocumented overstate them. The generational gradient measured in official data is therefore biased in an unknown direction, and the one direct within-sample comparison (Bersani et al. 2018) has the official record *compressing* the first-generation advantage, not creating it.

### 3.8 Age at arrival and the register sibling design

Beckley 2015, *Social Science Research*, DOI 10.1016/j.ssresearch.2015.06.006 [PubMed abstract]: Stockholm register data on foreign-background males; with covariates, arrival around age 4 has the highest suspicion risk; with family fixed effects "a later age at immigration was tied to a lower likelihood of crime", not statistically significant. Andersen & Skardhamar 2012, Statistics Norway DP [abstract, agent-read]: the negative arrival-age gradient "seems to be exaggerated by the residual effect of the omitted duration of residence variable". Hermansen 2017, *Demography* 54(1) [abstract]: the sibling fixed-effects arrival-age design is validated in Norwegian registers for education, employment, earnings and welfare; it has not been run on convictions with power. Melchor-Ayala 2019 (Portland State dissertation) [abstract, agent-read]: no arrival-age difference in offending among the foreign-born except lower drug offending for childhood arrivals.

## 4. European register evidence

### 4.1 Sweden: the ordering runs on age at arrival, and the explained share falls with severity

Hällsten, Szulkin & Sarnecki 2013 [SOURCE: corpus full text, Tables 1–2 read by the parent]. Stockholm cohorts completing compulsory school 1990–93, N = 63,462, followed to ages 28–31; three groups: children of native Swedes (reference), children of immigrants (born in Sweden, n = 2,532 males) and childhood immigrants (arrived as children, n = 2,072 males). Male risk ratios relative to the reference, raw → with family resources → with neighbourhood fixed effects: suspected, children of immigrants 0.396 → 0.146 → 0.087 (78% explained), childhood immigrants 0.552 → 0.213 → 0.138 (75%); convictions 0.378 → 0.147 → 0.107 (72%) and 0.519 → 0.205 → 0.158 (69%); violent conviction 0.614 → 0.262 → 0.17 (72%; the final estimate is at the edge of significance, t = 1.98) and 0.912 → 0.431 → 0.32 (65%); incarceration 0.989 → 0.597 → 0.508 (49%) and 1.227 → 0.647 → 0.527 (57%). Table 2: the raw ratio rises with the offence count (convictions ≥15: 1.75 for childhood immigrants, 1.17 for children of immigrants, against 0.52 and 0.38 at ≥1) while the explained share falls from about 70% to about 55%. "Male children of immigrants manifest lower levels of overrepresentation than male childhood immigrants in all of the models." For females the second-generation conviction gap turns negative with full controls. Culture test: the co-national intra-class correlation in crime is at most about .03, an ordinary correlation of about .17 by the authors' square-root conversion, against brother ICCs of .41 (Frisell et al. 2011, Swedish registers) and .47 (Rowe & Farrington 1997); the authors call culture "unlikely to be a strong cause of crime among immigrants, even though the effect is not negligible" [agent-read] and note the co-national ICC is biased upward by shared unmeasured SES.

### 4.2 Norway: standardize first; then a gap that is widening

Statistics Norway Rapporter 2017/36 (Andersen, Holtsmark & Mohn) [SOURCE: SSB landing page read this session; PDF not read]. Residents aged 15+ in 2010 registered as offenders: immigrants 6.7%, Norwegian-born to immigrant parents 11.3%, rest of the population 4.5%. Adjusted for sex and age composition: 5.8% and 6.8%. The excess over the rest falls from 2.2 to 1.3 points for immigrants (41% removed) and from 6.8 to 2.3 points for the Norwegian-born (66% removed); after standardization the second generation's excess is about 1.8 times the first generation's, not three times. Overrepresentation "decreased for all groups since the previous report" on 2001 residents, most among refugees and African-origin immigrants.

Statistics Norway 2024, "Siktelser og siktede personer etter innvandringsbakgrunn" [SOURCE: SSB article read this session]. Charges 2020–2023 per 1,000 residents: all ages, immigrants ~130, Norwegian-born to immigrant parents ~130, rest ~80; males 15–24, ~550, ~630, ~280; males 15–24 in Oslo, ~820, ~910, ~210. Charged persons per 1,000 males 15–24 (annual average 2021–23): ~59, ~64, ~35. Overrepresentation among males 15–24 rose from 53% to 96% (immigrants) and from 59% to 124% (Norwegian-born) between 2015–18 and 2020–23. The article states that demographic differences are not accounted for in these rates. Charges give ratios of 1.96 and 2.25 to the rest; charged persons give 1.69 and 1.83, so charges per charged person inflate the gap (the events-versus-persons mechanism in the bias memo).

Andersen & Skardhamar 2012 (SSB DP 728) [abstract, agent-read]: arriving younger means more registered crime among male immigrants, an "immigrant paradox" given their better integration, with the caveat that age, age at arrival and duration of residence are exactly collinear and the gradient "may be exaggerated by the residual effect of the omitted duration of residence variable".

### 4.3 Denmark: peers transmit, the residual widened, and the first generation is modelled with fewer controls

Damm & Dustmann 2014 [SOURCE: corpus full text, key passages verified]. Refugee children assigned 1986–1998; 38% charged and 31% convicted at least once by 21, against about 13% and 11% for a 10% sample of Danes born in 1980; male refugee youths aged 15–21 have a 46% conviction rate (1.48 convictions). One SD more youth criminals in the assignment neighbourhood raises the probability of a conviction by 5–9% and the number of convictions by 7–11%; the effect runs through youth conviction rates, not adult ones or crime rates; co-national youth convictions matter over and above the overall share; susceptibility is highest at assignment ages 10–14; no effect for females. The lane also reports a 29% charge-to-conviction ratio for refugee children against 28% for Danes [agent-read], which argues against differential attrition at the prosecution stage.

ROCKWOOL Foundation Research Unit 2023, "Overrepræsentation af dømte blandt ikke-vestlige efterkommere" [SOURCE: article read this session; underlying chapter not read]. Danish register, males 15–65, 1993–2019, outcome at least one conviction in the year, indexed to ethnic Danes. Age-adjusted, non-Western male descendants stood at about 3 times ethnic Danes in the early 1990s, fell until 2006, then rose to nearly 4 by 2019; with own and parental socioeconomic conditions the ratio is under 2 for most of the period, and that residual has risen over the last 15 years. ROCKWOOL's candidate explanations: descendants get less crime-preventive return from education; low-resource Danish men reduced crime faster; the financial crisis hit young non-Western men hardest. They flag that child-rearing and cultural indicators were not available.

Andersen & Tranæs 2011 (ROCKWOOL), reported through Klement 2020, *Nordic J. Criminology* 21(1) [agent-read through the review]: 94,193 male immigrants, 11,064 male descendants and a 10% native sample, prevalence and frequency modelled separately, with up to 23 controls for descendants but 10 for immigrants because registers hold descendants' parents and not immigrants' parents; Western-origin groups at or below the majority; non-Western higher on property, violence and total; Asian-origin males not significantly different from the majority. The control asymmetry is itself a finding (§6, angle 5). Skardhamar, Thorsen & Henriksen 2011 (SSB 2011/21), via the same review: age and sex composition "may account for up to 25%" of the overrepresentation.

### 4.4 Netherlands: the self-report–register wedge, quantified twice

Leerkes, Martinez & Groeneveld 2019, *British J. Criminology* 59(1):166–185, DOI 10.1093/bjc/azy021 [SOURCE: corpus full text, key passages verified]. NELLS survey 2010, ages 15–45, Turkish and Moroccan first and second generation vs native Dutch, self-report with social-desirability items, set against Statistics Netherlands suspect rates for the same ages. Official suspect rates run from 3.9% (first-generation Turks) to 11.1% (second-generation Moroccans) against 2.0% for the native Dutch, 2.0–5.6×; without social-desirability control, self-reported crime is lower for the first generation than for natives and 1.1–1.5× for the second; after the adjustment, official suspect shares are still 1.6–3.6× what self-reports imply, most for the second-generation Moroccans. Blom & Jennissen 2014, cited there: raw suspect odds 2.2 (first-generation Turkish) to 8.1 (Dutch-born with two Moroccan-born parents), 1.6 to 4.0 after age, sex, urbanization, household and SES. In the models, income and education absorb most of the ethnic coefficients, perceived discrimination predicts self-reported crime, neighbourhood disadvantage adds nothing, and family bonds and religiosity are protective and heavier in the first generation, which the authors call a "righteous migrant effect".

Bezemer, Leerkes & Dykstra 2026, *European J. on Criminal Policy and Research*, DOI 10.1007/s10610-026-09684-x [SOURCE: publisher full-text passages via search; PDF not retrieved]. Dutch juveniles 12–18, N = 5,078, self-reported offending (with social-desirability score replacement) linked to police suspect registrations; negative binomial models. Differences in self-reported offending account for 9–16% of minority overrepresentation in registrations; socioeconomic status for 27–39% (12% for the residual migrant group); urban residence for a further 14–19%; social-deviance measures do not systematically explain differences; minority youth remain 1.6–2.6 times as likely to be registered after all covariates, which the authors put at 35–46% of the gap unexplained. Minority youth reported more serious violence and drug offences, so involvement contributes, "but only to a limited degree".

### 4.5 Finland: heterogeneous sufficiency and institutional reach

Ansala, Hämäläinen & Sarvimäki 2016, "Slipping through the Cracks of a Welfare State", VATT WP 72 / RFBerlin DP 1606 [SOURCE: RePEc abstract]: among children of immigrants from OECD countries and the former Soviet Union, "differences in criminal sentences disappear once we condition on parental income and location of residence"; "large gaps remain for the children of immigrants from other regions, even conditional on background characteristics"; and children of immigrants from all origins are "substantially less likely to use psychotropic medication than the offspring of natives despite their higher self-reported mental health problems", which the authors read as institutions "not fully reach[ing] the children of immigrants". Salmi, Kivivuori & Aaltonen 2015, *European J. Criminology* 12(3) [abstract, agent-read]: Finnish self-report study 2012 (N = 8,914); immigrant youth report more delinquency; routine activities and parental control are the correlates; the excess "remained significant" after controls, and the authors say social disadvantage alone does not explain it.

Reading across the five countries: the second-generation excess in European registers is, in order, age structure, parental resources and neighbourhood, then a residual of roughly 1.5–2× that is largest for serious offences, grows over time in two countries, is heterogeneous by origin, and is not separable from system response with register data alone. The Dutch linked studies put the behavioural share of the *suspect* gap at a fraction of the whole; the Finnish medication result shows the same children reaching the justice end of the pipeline and not the clinical one.

## 5. Disconfirmation: what the new evidence kills or weakens

- **"The second generation offends more than native whites" (US).** Killed for NLSY97 arrest (Inkpen: no significant difference from third/fourth-plus non-Hispanic whites) and for NLSY79-CYA self-report (children statistically similar across generations). Survives only in unadjusted adolescent delinquency (Chen & Jiang) and in US-born-by-ethnicity incarceration, which is not a second-generation measure.
- **"Legal status cannot explain the rise because the second generation already has it."** Holds for jus soli countries; fails for Europe, where citizenship at birth cut second- and third-generation youth crime by 70%. And in the United States the *deterrence* half of legal status is a floor mechanism that switches off at birth, so it is a candidate for part of the rise even though the *opportunity* half is not.
- **"Neighbourhoods make the second generation criminal."** Weakened as a deprivation story (Sariaslan within-sibling null) and sharpened as a peer story (Damm & Dustmann; Grönqvist).
- **"Culture is imported."** Not supported for total registered offending among children of immigrants (Hällsten ≈ .01), supported for violence-relevant norms among internal migrants (Larsen). Unresolved.
- **"Immigrants' low rates are a reporting artefact."** Self-report is unbiased by generation (Bersani & Piquero); the one direct offender-sample comparison shows the official record harsher on the undocumented than their self-reports (Bersani et al. 2018).
- **"Selection explains the floor."** Nothing directly tested; the within-family correlations are low enough that a transmitted trait cannot be the main story of the *rise*, which is consistent with selection on the parent that does not transmit.
- **"The European second generation offends two to three times as much as natives."** Raw, yes. Sex-and-age standardization removes two-thirds of the Norwegian excess; parental resources and neighbourhood take the Swedish and Danish ratios to under 2; and in the Netherlands self-reported offending explains 9–16% of the suspect gap. What survives is a residual near 1.5–2× for serious offences whose behavioural share is unknown.
- **"The second generation converges over time."** Not in Denmark or Norway since about 2006: the residual is widening. Whether descendants rose or low-resource natives fell faster is untested.
- **The male-only regularity.** Four independent designs find the second-generation crime effect only in boys (Andres: girls' estimate positive; Foged: girls null; Dustmann–Mertz–Okatenko: girls' margin is teenage motherhood; Hällsten: the female gap is fully explained by resources and segregation). Gender-blind mechanisms (cultural distance, SES, selection) fit this worse than peer, street and policing mechanisms.

## 6. New angles, ranked by value over cost

1. **Reframe the estimand as a floor.** Publish the generational gap as "adult-arrival deficit" plus "child-of-immigrant excess over natives", separately. The first is large everywhere; the second is about zero in US surveys and positive in European registers. The repo's crime memo should carry both numbers, not one gradient. Cost: editorial.
2. **Age at arrival is the variable; "generation" is a proxy that inverts.** Stockholm's childhood immigrants out-offend its Swedish-born second generation; Norway's Norwegian-born out-offend its immigrants; Rumbaut's decimal generations rise monotonically with years of US childhood. A sibling age-at-arrival design on Nordic conviction registers (Hermansen's validated design; Beckley's Stockholm attempt was underpowered) with duration of residence handled explicitly would separate host-country childhood exposure from family selection in one step. Cost: register access.
3. **Ethnic attrition applied to crime.** Duncan & Trejo report Hispanic self-identification falling from 99% (first generation) to 94% (second) to 83% (third-generation children), about 30% of third-generation Mexican youth not identifying, and positive selection of the leavers; Antman, Duncan & Trejo 2016/2020 show the same machinery overstates Mexican-American health disparities [agent-read]. No paper applies it to arrest or incarceration. Predicted sign: measured later-generation Mexican-American crime rates are biased upward. NLSY97 records grandparent birthplace, so the objective-versus-subjective contrast is constructible in the dataset the field already uses. Cost: one analysis.
4. **The self-report-versus-official wedge as an estimand.** The Dutch linked studies put behaviour at 9–16% of the juvenile suspect gap and leave 1.6–2.6× unexplained; Bersani & Wy report the US arrest gap resists explanation; Bersani et al. 2018 show the official record harsher on the undocumented; Muchow quantifies discretionary-arrest inflation. Nobody has estimated how much the first-to-second-generation gradient itself changes between self-report and official record in the same people. Pathways and Crossroads can do it now. Cost: one re-analysis.
5. **Asymmetric control sets across generations.** Registers hold the parents of the second generation but not of the first, so Danish models use 23 controls for descendants and 10 for immigrants [agent-read via Klement 2020]. Every adjusted first-versus-second comparison compares an under-adjusted first generation with a fully adjusted second one, overstating the second generation's residual relative to the first. Not flagged in the literature found. Cost: re-estimate with a common control set.
6. **Standardize before theorizing.** Sex-and-age adjustment removes 66% of the Norwegian second generation's excess and 41% of the first generation's. Because descendants are concentrated in peak-offending ages by the timing of their parents' migration, any raw generational comparison overstates the second generation's excess relative to the first by construction. Cost: none; a rule.
7. **The comparison group is moving.** Ratios in Denmark and Norway widened after 2006 while native conviction rates fell. A differential-desistance question ("why did descendants not join the native crime drop?") is not the same as "why do descendants offend more?", and no paper found adopts it. Cost: register series already published.
8. **Differential returns, not differential levels.** ROCKWOOL's own reading of its widening residual is that education and employment buy less crime reduction for descendants. Every decomposition reviewed estimates SES main effects; the interaction is untested and would mean the standard decomposition understates what SES could do. Cost: one specification.
9. **Legalization dose-response.** Order the identified effects by permanence and age at grant: birthright citizenship from birth −70% (Germany); Italian click-day legalization halves serious crime among winners (repo; point estimate, 95% interval reaching zero — audit B 2026-09-23); IRCA −3 to −5% (repo; county-level, imputed UCR); DACA, temporary and granted in adolescence, null on incarceration. A meta-regression on permanence × age at grant × breadth of rights would separate opportunity cost from deterrence. Cost: a paper, inputs exist.
10. **Parental-integration shocks as a family of quasi-experiments.** Language training (down), benefit cuts (up); untried: labour-market access dates, family-reunification waiting times, dispersal to labour markets. Couttenier's two-thirds reduction from work rights suggests the same lever moderates origin effects.
11. **Institutional interception.** Finnish children of immigrants use far less psychotropic medication despite worse self-reported mental health, while being over-represented in sentences. If troubled native youth are routed to clinical services and troubled immigrant-origin youth to the police, the generational gap is partly a pipeline allocation. Linked health, social-services and crime registers in all four Nordic countries can test it.
12. **Regression-to-the-mean test on a crime-relevant trait.** Measure self-control, impulsivity or risk tolerance in first and second generation (Nordic conscription batteries; NLSY97 non-cognitive items) and test whether the second-generation mean sits where imperfect transmission predicts. Nobody has run it.
13. **Male-only mechanism.** Whatever acts on the second generation acts on boys. Test the peer and street-exposure channel against the honour-norm channel by sex; the Andres girls' result (integration reduced by parental response to citizenship) is a mechanism lead.
14. **Culture versus resources in one dataset.** Test origin-country violence exposure on (a) violence and victimization and (b) total registered offending for the same second-generation sample. The Larsen–Couttenier–Hällsten contradiction dissolves if (a) is positive and (b) is near zero.
15. **Inter-country adoptees as a control arm.** Webb et al. 2015 (PLOS ONE, Danish cohort of 2.07 million) distinguish adoptees, first and second generation [agent-located, not read]. Adoptees carry origin ancestry and visible-minority status without immigrant-family socialization, language or parental-SES channels, which separates discrimination from socialization better than any covariate.
16. **Urbanicity decomposition on modern data.** Moehling & Piehl's whole 1.5/second-generation excess was place composition. Re-run with ACS/NLSY97 place controls.
17. **A meta-analysis of the individual-level generational literature does not exist.** Ousey & Kubrin asked for one in 2018; none found through 2026 [agent-read; provisional negative].
18. **Cohort and period are confounded with generation in every US estimate** (single birth cohorts: NLSY97 1980–84; Add Health one school cohort; CILS 1991). A multi-cohort design is the fix.
19. **Return-migration selection on criminality** is unexamined in any register; it would mechanically produce a first-generation advantage in host-country data.
20. **Cross-level contradiction.** Kubrin's NIJ 309968 finds neighbourhood English proficiency predicts *lower* crime while individual-level assimilation predicts *more* offending [agent-read]. A joint multilevel model is the test.
