**Verdict:** Recent primary sources strengthen the case that portable work rights and skilled migration can improve economic outcomes, while supporting a positive housing-demand effect in constrained places. They do **not** establish a universal net benefit, a universal capacity threshold, or gains for every native worker. Important qualifications come from the papers themselves: employment shares are not levels; average wages can mask composition; macroeconomic prediction is not automatically causal; and a new publication can analyze an old policy. [INFERENCE, based on the sources below.]

## Material updates from primary texts

### Skill, time horizon and heterogeneous outcomes

The H-1B study uses industry's exposure to the cap expansion. Treatment is **expected exposure to additional authorized visa slots**, allocated using historical industry shares, not a measured increase of the same number of employed migrants; some caps were unfilled. Continuous-treatment synthetic-control adjustment addresses differing pretrends. Reported income gains concentrate in non-STEM occupations and downstream industries. The paper also reproduces short-run native STEM losses and finds no detectable patenting response. [SOURCE: NBER 35560, introduction and empirical design.]

**Implication:** This challenges a fixed-demand account in which immigrants simply replace native workers. But adjustment for pretrends does not rule out later industry shocks. Patent nonsignificance cannot establish the absence of innovation, and the supply-chain pattern does not uniquely identify productivity as the mechanism. Subgroup averages cannot show every worker gains; historical skilled migration does not identify an unlimited inflow of all skill levels. [INFERENCE]

### Legal rights, formalization and employment-share arithmetic

The Italian study instruments the 2006–2007 increase in EU07 formal workers, scaled by **fixed 1991 local population**, with the similarly scaled 2001–2002 amnesty change. Its reported first-stage F statistic is **181.52**. The principal sample is a balanced panel of formal firms. EU07 shares increase and native shares fall, without detected changes in native hiring or separation rates. Firm-average wages show no significant change, while migrant incumbents gain wages and move toward higher-paying employers; permanent-contract access improves. [SOURCE: NBER 35493, introduction and Sections 4–5.]

**Implication:** A smaller native share can result from immigrant growth without native employment falling. Average wages can mask incumbent/entrant composition. The authors explicitly say the instrument does not separately identify existing residents' legalization from new arrivals. A native-wage null alone cannot establish that the arrival margin was unimportant. The exclusion restriction still requires historical amnesty exposure not to predict relevant later local changes through other channels. First-stage strength and nonsignificant pretrend tests do not prove that assumption. [INFERENCE]

### Macro productivity and capital adjustment: association with a causal gap

The OECD paper harmonizes migration stocks and analyzes subsequent productivity and capital accumulation. Positive associations are strongest for high-skill migration; low-skill estimates are close to zero and imprecise. It abandons national push-factor instruments because they have little predictive power, studies policy openness mainly in reduced form, examines unusually large migration episodes and separately uses European regional shift-share variation. [SOURCE: NBER 35523, Sections 3, 6.1 and 7.]

**Implication:** The results challenge fixed-capital extrapolations as a universal long-run account. They do not supply a clean national causal multiplier. The authors explicitly call conditional exogeneity unlikely because of omitted variables and reverse causality. A large, surprising inflow is not thereby exogenous. Related specifications can share confounding; their agreement is corroboration, not identification by accumulation. Nonsignificant low-skill estimates do not prove zero effects, and no detected saturation is not evidence of unlimited capacity. [INFERENCE]

### Enforcement changes work through several margins

The ICE revision's principal CPS outcome requires being **employed and at work in the reference week**. Its sample includes those working, searching or having worked within five years so a sector can be observed. Legal status is inferred from demographic proxies. It reports reductions of **1.3 percentage points overall** and **1.8 points in more exposed sectors** for the likely-undocumented group. These are unweighted averages across areas for the eligible CPS sample observed there, not population-weighted national changes or effects on a fixed panel of all original residents. Native results vary by sex. Robustness includes Callaway–Sant'Anna estimates alongside the main specification. [SOURCE: NBER 35129, sample, results and robustness sections.]

**Implication:** This challenges an automatic native-employment gain from this enforcement margin. It is not a measured national deportation effect or a 1.3% employment change for all undocumented residents. Absence, production disruption, permanent job loss and sample change are different outcomes. The selected sample and inferred status limit interpretation; they are not by themselves a refutation. The publisher's revision field establishes an in-window version, but without the previous PDF we cannot attribute particular findings to that revision rather than the original paper. [INFERENCE]

### Fiscal incidence: an accounting choice can change the sign

ESRI's review emphasizes cohort characteristics, employment integration, the business cycle and expenditure allocation. Its discussion of an older OECD accounting exercise puts foreign-born residents' Irish contribution at **+0.62% of GDP** after congestible public goods and **−0.21%** after pure public goods are also allocated. These refer to **2006–2018**, not present-day marginal entrants. [SOURCE: ESRI report 141, executive summary.]

**Implication:** The **0.83-percentage-point difference** changes assigned budget categories; it is not a causal estimate of a newly imposed immigrant cost. Neither allocation automatically equals the marginal counterfactual. Irish cohort composition and budget conditions do not establish the US unauthorized-migrant or municipal-shelter result. Economic services can coexist with a negative narrowly measured fiscal contribution; favorable fiscal averages can coexist with local transition costs. [INFERENCE; arithmetic: 0.62 − (−0.21) = 0.83.]

### Housing pressure survives; a universal ceiling does not follow

The UK review finds mostly positive, modest housing-price effects, varying with geography, method, supply responsiveness and migrant composition. It selects preferred study specifications and harmonizes units where possible. Among institutionally similar settings, the median is **0.65% higher house prices per one-percentage-point increase in immigrant population share**. Its **4–6% of the UK's total house-price rise over three decades** is a literature-based calibration, not a new UK causal estimate. [SOURCE: primary full report, summary and Sections 3–4.]

**Implication:** Housing costs should not be dismissed. Neither does this review establish immigration as every market's dominant driver. One percentage point of population share is not a 1% proportional increase in immigrant population. Study averages are not universally transportable structural elasticities. Higher rent redistributes income between renters and owners, so the rent bill is not itself a net world-welfare loss. Crowding, moving, construction and distributional welfare require separate measurement. [INFERENCE]
