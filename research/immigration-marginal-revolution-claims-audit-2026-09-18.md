# Marginal Revolution on immigration, 2003–2026: Cowen and Tabarrok graded against the repo

**Verdict:** The blog is not a target in the way Smith, Decker and Nowrasteh are. Of 23 graded author-voice claims, Cowen's grade AGREE 10, COORDINATE 5, HARD 0; Tabarrok's grade AGREE 3, COORDINATE 4, HARD 1. Cowen has said in his own voice most of what the repo measures: California's fiscal burden (2004), state and local policy as the real immigration policy (2019), welfare and immigration as complements (2024), backlash as the binding constraint (2015 onward), and that natives run a negative fiscal balance too under average cost (2025). His COORDINATE rows are the CBO and Colas–Sachs fiscal citations (federal, model output, first generation) and the 2012 forecast that Mexican assimilation would now run faster than critics predicted, which holds on language and attitudes and fails on the fiscal unit. Tabarrok's one HARD row is the 2015 claim that schooling immigrant children is a loan repaid through their later taxes: on the Mexican-origin second generation the repo's microdata says it is not, while on the all-origin second generation it is. His 2024 fiscal post names the NAS caveat against his own side before arguing it away with model output. The single most useful sentence in the archive is Cowen's 2024 complaint that immigration critics never send him a cost-benefit study; the resident account under 144 conventions is that study. ## 3. What the archive shows that the four-target scorecard did not

1. **Cowen's record is the repo's frame, twenty years early.** The California burden (2004), the state-local unit (2019), welfare-immigration complementarity (2024), natives negative under average cost (2025): each is a sentence in his own voice. The scorecard's "coordinate truths" charge does not fit him; the fit is that he cites the federal number when a federal number is what is on offer, and says the local thing when he is writing about localities. [INFERENCE from rows C1–C15]
2. **The cost-benefit challenge.** "immigration critics do not send me cost-benefit studies, nor do they seem to commission them... You could have a good one for a few hundred thousand dollars, right?" (2024-07-01). The resident account is that study: a complete annual balance for one origin population, all ages, 144 conventions, against a same-age benchmark, with the second-order channels tested and null. It is not a cost-benefit study of admission, which the repo's limit 1 says it cannot be; it is the account of the population that admission produced. [SOURCE: archive; ladder 130, 137–142]
3. **Tabarrok's rows are Caplan's rows.** Open borders, the welfare-state keyhole, jails convergence, Mexican cooks: each maps to a Caplan row already graded (C1, C3, C4, C2). The one new sentence is the schooling loan, and it is the cleanest HARD in the whole exercise because it names the mechanism (later taxes) that the second-generation result measures directly. [SOURCE: rows T1–T8]
4. **The three limits still bind.** Residents, not admission; the sign is a convention and the gap is not; wages and first-generation crime are theirs. Cowen's own 2025 sentence is limit 2 stated by the other side. [SOURCE: scorecard §3]
