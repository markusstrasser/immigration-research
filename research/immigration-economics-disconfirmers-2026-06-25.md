# Immigration Economics Disconfirmers — Execution (2026-06-25; repaired 2026-09-05)

**Verdict:** These sources challenge specific large-wage-loss and fiscal-cost claims. They do not create a common empirical experiment, a universal upper bound on fiscal cost, or a measured realized benefit from the 2021–2026 surge. Publication status and ideological surprise do not establish causal validity. [INFERENCE]

## Mariel: a disputed magnitude, not a proved zero

Borjas reports wages falling **"dramatically, by 10 to 30 percent"** in a narrowly defined Miami sample: non-Hispanic men aged 25–59 with less than high school. Clemens & Hunt identify sensitivity to sample definition and composition, including a large change in the sampled Black share. They report effects **"indistinguishable from zero"** under broader definitions and discuss results "compatible with a modest fall of roughly **−2% to −8%**" in the narrow sample. These are estimates and uncertainty statements, not a proof that every real effect is zero or bounded by 8%. [SOURCE: https://www.nber.org/papers/w21588; https://www.nber.org/papers/w23433]

The June memo’s list of annual sample sizes for 1983–1987 (27, 18, 16, 15, 16) averages **18.4**, not 17. That internally inconsistent “exact” transcription is withdrawn pending reconciliation of sample definitions. The small-sample and changing-composition concern does not require that mistaken average. [RECALCULATION; primary table reconciliation UNVERIFIED]

A schooling-composition placebo and attenuation after a race indicator support the critique, but do not by themselves settle the causal effect: specification choices, sampling error and the counterfactual remain. Borjas’s 2019 response, “The Role of Race,” is a live counterargument; the June pass had only its abstract. Peri & Yasenov’s synthetic-control analysis reports no significant negative wage/employment effect for its low-skill population; non-significance is not equivalence. [SOURCE: https://doi.org/10.1177/0019793919825753; https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.54.2.0217.8561R1; INFERENCE]

**Scope:** Mariel’s large-loss interpretation is contested and sensitive to the estimand. That does not logically refute every national labor-demand model, nor does it establish the effect of a different surge or enforcement policy. [INFERENCE]

## Texas convictions: an additional outcome using overlapping data

Cato’s Texas series reports lower unauthorized than native homicide-conviction rates in its covered vintages. The 2013–2022 analysis reports approximately 2.2 versus 3.0 convictions per 100,000 and a 26.2% difference using unrounded rates. The earlier 2015, 2017 and 2019 publications use overlapping Texas DPS administrative sources; they are not four independent datasets or four independent replications. [SOURCE: https://www.cato.org/policy-analysis/illegal-immigrant-murderers-texas-2013-2022; https://www.cato.org/sites/cato.org/files/pubs/pdf/irpb-4-updated.pdf; https://www.cato.org/publications/immigration-research-policy-brief/criminal-immigrants-texas-2017-illegal-immigrant; https://www.cato.org/immigration-research-policy-brief/criminal-immigrants-texas-2019]

Convictions are a later legal outcome than arrests, but remain conditional on reporting, detection, clearance, charging, adjudication and status classification. Cato itself flags some non-homicide data as "suspect". Neither changing outcomes nor repeating a data vintage proves the latent offending ratio. The lower observed conviction rate is useful evidence, with population-denominator and retrospective status-update checks still relevant. [SOURCE: same primary Cato study; INFERENCE]

## UK fiscal accounting: match cohort and horizon

Dustmann & Frattini (2014) estimate historical fiscal contributions, not lifetime NPV. Their 2001–2011 recent-arrival account reports positive contributions for both EEA and non-EEA arrivals since 2000: the exact totals depend on the accounting scenario and are not re-certified here. Their longer-resident/all-cohort comparison is different; non-EEA balances there are negative. The June memo incorrectly used that older/all-cohort negative result to characterize the recent non-EEA cohort. [SOURCE: https://www.cream-migration.org/files/FiscalEJ.pdf; https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12181]

Assigning dependent children’s education costs to parents is an explicit accounting choice, not automatically a bias. Consistent costs, taxes, descendant attribution, horizon and fiscal-policy assumptions are needed before comparing this UK account with a US NAS age/education NPV. These results show cohort and institutional dependence; they do not independently refute a different population’s projected balance. Fiorio et al. (2023) is a newer source lead whose full design was not assessed in the June pass. [SOURCE: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10797-023-09787-9; INFERENCE]

## US indirect fiscal channels and the CBO projection

**Colas & Sachs (2024)** model indirect fiscal gains from changes in native wages and labor supply; their preferred estimate is about $750 per low-skilled immigrant per year. Their paper describes an offset "of the same order of magnitude
  as the direct fiscal [cost] and of the opposite sign" and says it "turns the total fiscal effect
  from a fiscal burden to a fiscal surplus" in more favorable cases and "significantly reduces the burden" in others. Those are scenario-dependent model results. A positive omitted channel does not make a static estimate a rigorous upper bound on the real-world cost, since other omitted effects and parameter uncertainty remain. [SOURCE: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20220176; https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/93967/1/352.pdf; INFERENCE]

**Clemens, “The Fiscal Effect of Immigration: Reducing Bias in Influential Estimates,”** develops a capital-income-tax correction to influential fiscal estimates under stated economic assumptions. The June title/abstract-only inference that this result was principally a public-goods/descendant reallocation was unsupported and is withdrawn. Model-derived sign changes require matching the exact scenario and population; they cannot simply be added to Colas–Sachs or CBO. See [the repaired Clemens analysis](immigration-dismantle-clemens-2026-06-25.md). [SOURCE: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3982027; INFERENCE]

**CBO July 2024, publication 60165,** projects the incremental effects of its 2021–2026 immigration surge over 2024–2034: about **$897B lower covered federal deficits**, approximately **$1.2T additional federal revenue** and **$8.9T additional nominal GDP**. The fiscal score includes revenues, mandatory spending and net interest; it excludes discretionary appropriations and state/local budgets. CBO illustrates approximately **$0.2T** higher discretionary funding under a proportional-spending assumption. This makes the coverage incomplete; it does not establish that $897B is a proven ceiling or a mere accounting artifact. [SOURCE: https://www.cbo.gov/publication/60165]

CBO’s 8.7M excess “other foreign nationals” is an accounting-category net flow relative to baseline, not a count of all unique border entrants. The report is a projection, not realized 2024–2034 evidence. Its GDP total is output, not fiscal revenue or additional welfare to add to the budget score. The projected federal gain does not determine each state/local balance or each incumbent group’s welfare. [SOURCE: same CBO report; INFERENCE]

## What conclusions change

| Claim | Defensible update |
|---|---|
| Mariel establishes a large, precise low-skill wage loss | Magnitude is contested and sample-sensitive; neither a causal zero nor a universal bound has been established here |
| A static NAS cell gives the complete fiscal effect | Modelled indirect channels can materially change that cell’s balance under specified assumptions |
| The static cost is a mathematical upper bound | Unsupported; one positive omitted channel is insufficient to bound all omitted effects |
| CBO establishes a realized federal gain and universal local loss | Incorrect; it projects covered federal effects for a particular surge and does not settle the local or full welfare ledger |
| UK recent non-EEA immigrants were fiscally negative in D&F | Incorrect for the cited recent-cohort account; distinguish it from the all-cohort account |
| Conviction evidence settles actual offending | Too strong; it is a distinct observed justice-system outcome with its own selection/classification limits |

The coherent synthesis is conditional: fiscal signs vary with age, skill, cohort, taxes, benefits, horizon and behavioral adjustments. A fiscal surplus is neither necessary nor sufficient for a positive total welfare effect, and aggregate gains do not imply every incumbent group gains. [INFERENCE]
