# The Cato Institute's immigration case — corrected assessment

**Date:** 2026-06-25; materially revised 2026-09-05. **Section of:** consolidated dismantling memo.

**Verdict:** The June fiscal refutation was wrong as written. Cato's historical accounting deserves sensitivity analysis, but its descendant treatment is internally consistent and the alleged state/local sign reversal was not demonstrated. The crime and welfare results answer narrower descriptive questions; the policy conclusions require additional evidence. [INFERENCE]

**Frame and scope:** Fiscal accounting, incumbent welfare, worldwide output and crime incidence are distinct outcomes. Cato's advocacy affiliation is relevant context, not a calibrated probability that a result is false. Findings favorable to immigration are consistent with Cato's preferences, so the June description of its crime result as against-interest evidence was incorrect. This review is also LLM-assisted; see [instrument caveat](../notes/llm-bias-caveat.md).

## C1–C2 — Crime rates and enforcement policy

The Texas report finds lower homicide-conviction rates for unauthorized and legal immigrants than for all native-born residents in its 2013–2022 population: approximately 2.2, 1.2 and 3.0 per 100,000, respectively. Its percentage differences use unrounded underlying rates. Convictions provide a different outcome from arrests; they are not a complete measure of offending or an adjustment for all differences in detection and processing. [SOURCE: source 1]

**What follows:** the reported Texas first-generation comparison is evidence against a blanket claim that unauthorized immigrants have unusually high homicide-conviction rates. It does not establish a nationwide causal effect of immigration status or the optimal enforcement policy. In particular, a low group-average rate cannot evaluate enforcement targeted at people with individual evidence of serious offending. That requires the intervention's marginal public-safety effects and costs. [INFERENCE]

The NIJ/RTI county-level result discussed in the [confidence ladder](immigration-confidence-ladder.md) is a different estimand: a null association with county unauthorized share cannot erase an individual-level nativity/status rate difference. Nor does second-generation convergence by itself reverse the first-generation comparison or prove that the native-born comparator is inflated. [INFERENCE; conceptual audit §6]

## C3 — Historical fiscal surplus: an allocation-sensitive estimate, not a refuted sum

Cato reports $24.2T revenue, $13.6T allocated costs and $3.9T interest savings: approximately **+$14.5T** for first-generation immigrants over 1994–2023, in 2024 dollars. The state/local **+$6.6T** includes roughly $4.9T direct surplus (`9.6 − 4.7`) and $1.7T interest savings. The June presentation omitted that final term in its state/local arithmetic. [SOURCE: source 2, Tables 1–4; RECALC]

**Descendants:** Cato says "those children are natural-born **Americans**, not immigrants". Its first-generation headline excludes both their taxes and their costs. The separate first-plus-second-generation result includes both and reports **+$7.9T including interest**, approximately +$6T before interest, across all government levels. Neither is a forecast of descendants' future taxes. The 2023 paper's future-NPV section separately explains why its historical dependent reallocation does not apply to that projection. The accusation that Cato banks future descendant taxes while assigning their schooling to natives is withdrawn. [SOURCE: source 2, Table 13; source 5, forecasts methodology]

**Public goods:** the model assigns zero marginal costs for pure public goods, while charging congestible services such as transport and fire protection on a per-capita basis. NAS warns that zero marginal cost becomes "less tenable" for large multiyear inflows; it also cautions that average allocation can overstate the incremental cost. This supports sensitivity analysis, not the June assertion that one allocation is uniquely correct. [SOURCE: source 2, Appendix; NAS [Summary, pp.7–8](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/23550/chapter/2) and [chapter 8, pp.360–364](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/23550/chapter/13)]

**Comparison discipline:** an all-immigrant 30-year historical total, a lifetime forecast for a low-education subgroup and a recent-surge budget estimate need not have the same sign. They are not direct contradictions. The claim that removing either dependent allocation or zero-public-goods costs makes this state/local result negative is **unverified and withdrawn**; no matching rerun was performed. A positive federal result also does not independently validate Cato's much broader historical total. [INFERENCE]

**The remaining substantive concern:** calling the estimate a strict lower bound requires more than naming omitted positive spillovers. Uncertain allocations and any omitted adverse effects also need bounds. A descriptive first-generation total cannot by itself settle a descendant-inclusive future policy counterfactual. These are limits on interpretation, not a demonstrated negative replacement total. [INFERENCE]

## C4 — Lower welfare consumption is a sub-account, not a full fiscal result

The SIPP brief reports 21% lower per-capita welfare/entitlement use by immigrants and 54% lower use by noncitizens in its sample. Its author explicitly states: "This new brief is an analysis of the consumption of welfare benefits; it is **not a net-fiscal impact analysis** that considers the consumption of other government services or tax revenue." That caveat should be credited, not repackaged as an admission of a hidden error. [SOURCE: source 3; Nowrasteh, [February 19, 2025](https://alexnowrasteh.com/)]

This comparison does not include every expenditure or revenue channel, and demographic composition and eligibility matter. Lower average use also does not alone test whether benefit access influences migration decisions. The June claim that it settles the welfare-magnet hypothesis was too strong. [INFERENCE]

## C5 — Welfare exclusions: partial mechanism with policy tradeoffs

The retained description of Figure 21 in the 2023 paper says it "does not show actual divergent net fiscal impacts". Treat it as a counterfactual, with its own assumptions. [SOURCE: source 5]

Restricting a set of benefits does not remove schooling, shelter, policing or every other public expense. But it need not eliminate every cost to improve a net fiscal balance. Nor is it circular to observe below-native benefit use and consider further restrictions: a lower relative rate can still represent a large absolute amount. The relevant quantity is the marginal savings after behavioral, eligibility, administrative and humanitarian effects. Their political durability is an empirical policy question, not something this memo can declare impossible. [INFERENCE]

## C6–C7 — Backlogs and the proposed inflow target

Bier's administrative evidence supports serious delays and distortions from existing quotas and country caps. Backlog counts and waiting times should retain their original dates. A queue alone does not identify an optimal admission rate: excess demand for a constrained benefit is not sufficient evidence that any particular cap is too low. Evaluating the case for expansion also requires the costs and benefits of the applicants and alternatives. [SOURCE: source 4; INFERENCE]

Likewise, a historical inflow near 1% of population or a contemporary high employment rate cannot by itself establish an optimal modern target near that size. Skill mix, housing supply and adjustment time matter; available capacity can also expand. These are unresolved policy comparisons, not proof that a 1% target fails. The case for high-skill expansion may be strong without being literally costless or pure deadweight-loss elimination. [INFERENCE]

## C8 — Open-borders magnitude and absorption

Large potential gains from freer movement are supported by the structural literature, but doubling is a model-dependent complete-liberalization result, not a verified central forecast. The old **~4%** Docquier–Machado–Sekkat figure belongs to a 2012 draft scenario; the [published 2015 paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/sjoe.12097) reports a **11.5–12.5%** medium-term benchmark. These are different versions/scenarios, not an arithmetic correction to an identical experiment. [SOURCE: conceptual audit §5; published paper]

The repo's fixed-gain illustration gives `200M × $7,500 / $110T = 1.36%` and `3B × $7,500 / $110T = 20.45%`; it would take 14.67B movers to produce 100% under those fixed inputs. This diagnoses the illustration, not other models with different gains and dynamics. [RECALC]

The US housing-start comparison is a scale benchmark, not a global capacity measurement or a fixed construction ceiling. A rent rise transfers income from tenants to landlords before secondary effects; it cannot all be charged as lost global output. Aggregate benefits do not require every locality to gain, although compensation and distribution remain policy questions. The June global-absorption and taxpayer-closure “kills” therefore do not follow from the cited local cost channels. [INFERENCE; conceptual audit §§5,7]
