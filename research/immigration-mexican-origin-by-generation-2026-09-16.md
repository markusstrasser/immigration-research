**Verdict:** For Mexican-origin residents the measured record is clear on characteristics and thin on causal impact. First generation: 41–43% of adults without a high-school diploma, prime-age men employed at 89% (the highest of any group), worker median earnings 59% of third-plus non-Hispanic whites, SPM poverty 23%, household program use 60–62% driven by Medicaid and EITC with cash assistance at 4–5%, a partial annual fiscal balance $6.7k–$9.0k per adult below the all-native mean, and the lowest crime of any group. Second generation: the dropout share falls to 10%, BA attainment doubles to 25%, employment reaches the native level, worker median earnings rise to 76% of white natives, SPM poverty halves to 13–15%, household program use is 52–53% (young heads with children), cash assistance equals natives', and arrest timing is statistically indistinguishable from non-Hispanic whites, Blacks and third-plus Hispanics. Third-plus (self-identified): BA 27%, earnings 81% of white natives, poverty 11%, program use 42%; incremental progress over the second generation is small but the group is selected by ethnic attrition and its crime cannot be separated. Causal *impact* on natives is established for one channel only, and it is contested in magnitude: Borjas–Katz put the 1980–2000 Mexican inflow at about −8 points on native dropouts' wages and about −1 point on all workers in the short run, with Card- and Peri-style designs near zero. On the repo's own annual partial ledger (modeled payroll, federal and state income taxes minus selected cash and non-cash transfers, adults 25–64), Mexican second-generation adults sit **$6,000 to $7,600 per adult below third-plus non-Hispanic whites** and $4,800 to $5,900 below the all-native mean, with standard errors near $400; the self-identified third-plus sits $4,800 to $6,300 below whites. The shortfall is taxes, not transfers: the Mexican second generation pays about half the income tax of white natives and draws about $1,000 *less* in selected cash transfers. No Mexican-specific lifetime balance survives the 2026-09-05 repair. [SOURCE: tables below; linked memos] [INFERENCE for the synthesis]

## 4. Economic impact on natives: the one causal channel with Mexican-specific estimates

Borjas & Katz 2007 (NBER w11281), Table 11, simulate the 1980–2000 immigrant influx in a nested-CES model with education-experience cells. Predicted change in the native weekly wage, actual influx versus a counterfactual with no Mexican immigration:

| Native group | Short run, capital fixed: actual / no-Mexico | Long run, capital elastic: actual / no-Mexico |
|---|---|---|
| All workers | −3.3% / −2.2% | +0.1% / +0.6% |
| High-school dropouts | −8.2% / −0.1% | −4.8% / +2.7% |
| High-school graduates | −2.2% / −1.2% | +1.1% / +1.6% |
| College graduates | −3.8% / −3.9% | −0.5% / −1.1% |

[SOURCE: NBER w11281 Table 11, read from the PDF this session]

So the Mexican component of the 1980–2000 inflow is about −8 points for native dropouts in either run, about −1 point for the average worker in the short run and −0.5 in the long run, and nothing for graduates. This is a structural simulation whose elasticity of substitution between dropouts and high-school graduates drives the dropout result; the Card, Peri–Sparber and Ottaviano–Peri line treats dropouts and graduates as close substitutes and immigrants as imperfect substitutes for natives, which pushes the same number toward zero, and Mariel remains "a disputed magnitude, not a proved zero" ([disconfirmers memo](immigration-economics-disconfirmers-2026-06-25.md)). Nothing in the repo identifies the Mexican-specific wage effect after 2000, when the Mexican-born workforce stopped growing. Level: **contested evidence, model-dependent sign for the average worker, negative but disputed magnitude for dropouts.** [SOURCE: memos cited] [INFERENCE on the model dependence]

Second-order channels (housing, local services, construction supply, prices) are assessed in the [second-order effects memo](immigration-second-order-effects-2026-09-05.md) for low-skill inflows generally; none is Mexican-specific.

## 6. Crime by generation

From today's crime memos and the September 5 corrections, all verified against sources there:

- First generation. Texas 2018 violent-felony arrest-charge rate, unauthorized versus native-born: ratio 0.4571 ([conduct denominators](immigration-conduct-denominators-2026-09-05.md)). Mexico-born men 18–39 institutionalized 1.13% in 2023 against 2.05% for all natives and 1.12% for native non-Hispanic whites, with ICE detention inflating the foreign-born row ([Mexican-origin memo](immigration-mexican-origin-generation-incarceration-2026-09-16.md) §2). The first-generation advantage is an adult-arrival floor that does not transmit ([generational mechanisms](immigration-generational-crime-mechanisms-2026-09-16.md) §1).
- Second generation. NLSY97 (Inkpen 2024): 85% of first-generation Mexican respondents reach 18 without an arrest versus 78% of the Mexican second generation, and the second generation shows "no statistically significant differences in time to first arrest compared to non-Hispanic white or Black individuals or third/fourth-generation Hispanic respondents". This is the only US design that separates the Mexican second generation from third-plus whites on a justice outcome. Level: A for the numbers, one cohort.
- Second plus third-plus pooled. US-born Mexican-origin men 18–39 institutionalized 5.90% in 2000 (3.45× native whites), 1.92% in 2023 (1.72× raw, about 2.1× after reallocating generically coded Hispanic prison records), now below the all-native 2.05% (ladder entries 65–66). All-Hispanic sentenced-prisoner rate 606 per 100,000 adults versus 231 white and 1,218 Black in 2023 ([race/ethnicity](immigration-crime-race-ethnicity-2026-09-05.md)); the 2009–2021 decline was property and drug for both Hispanics and whites (ladder 70).
- Third generation alone: not separable in any US crime source; attrition biases the self-identified pool upward.

## 8. Which offences whites commit more, and the enforcement wedge

Operator question (2026-09-16): what crime do whites do more of? Three sources, each answering a different question.

**Sentenced state prisoners per 100,000 residents, 2021, non-Hispanic white vs Hispanic** (BJS, transcribed in `../infra/immigration-fiscal/acs_institutional_2026_09_16/bjs_offense_by_ethnicity_result.txt`): violent 92 vs 256; murder 19 vs 51; sexual assault 33 vs 68; robbery 11 vs 43; assault 20 vs 75; property 30.0 vs 30.4; burglary 15 vs 19; motor-vehicle theft 1.4 vs 2.7; **fraud 3.0 vs 1.9**; drug 25 vs 36; public order 19 vs 35; all offences 168 vs 358. Fraud is the only category where the white per-capita prison stock exceeds the Hispanic one; property overall is level; everything else is 1.2–3.8× higher for Hispanics before age adjustment. Within the white prison population the shares are more sexual-assault- and drug-heavy (19.8% and 15.0%) than within the Hispanic one (19.1% and 10.1%), which is a composition statement, not a rate. [SOURCE: BJS p22st Tables 16–17, Vintage 2021 populations]

**Federal offenders where whites are over-represented.** Child-pornography offenders sentenced federally were 77.1% white and 12.8% Hispanic in FY2023 and 71.2% white and 18.0% Hispanic in FY2024, against roughly 58% and 19% of adults. [SOURCE: USSC Quick Facts, Child Pornography Offenders, FY2023 and FY2024 PDFs read this session] Fraud, tax and securities offenders are majority white but not over-represented per capita in the sentencing data read here; the under-prosecution of financial crime relative to street crime is the reason the incarceration comparator understates white *harm*, as noted in the [Mexican-origin incarceration memo](immigration-mexican-origin-generation-incarceration-2026-09-16.md) §7. Self-reported illicit drug use and drunk driving are highest among whites in NSDUH [TRAINING-DATA, not verified this session]; the 2010s rural white jail growth tied to opioids and methamphetamine is the white-specific incarceration trend of the decade [TRAINING-DATA].

**Victim-reported offending, which does not pass through police or courts.** NCVS 2012–15: offenders were perceived as white in 43.8% of violent victimizations, Black in 22.7%, Hispanic in 14.4%, against population shares (age 12+) of 65.0%, 12.3% and 15.6%. Per capita that is 0.67 for whites, 1.85 for Blacks and 0.92 for Hispanics, a Hispanic/white ratio of about **1.4×** in victim reports against **2.4–2.9×** in age-specific male imprisonment (§6, ladder 70). [SOURCE: BJS, *Race and Hispanic Origin of Victims and Offenders, 2012–15*, Tables 2 and 11, PDF read this session] The two ratios are not the same quantity (victimization counts incidents, prison counts sentence-years, and the NCVS excludes homicide and drug crime), but the gap between them is the size of the enforcement, charging and sentencing wedge plus offence-severity mix. Nothing in either source separates the Mexican second generation from other Hispanics.

## 9. Can it be settled: designs and data for "the Mexican second generation vs third-plus whites"

The question has two halves, and neither is answerable from the data the country routinely publishes, because no US arrest, court or prison record carries parents' birthplace and no census or ACS record has carried it since 1970.

*Crime.* What exists: NLSY97 (one cohort born 1980–84, self-reported arrests; Inkpen 2024 finds no significant difference in time to first arrest, §6); Add Health Wave V (born 1976–82, parental nativity, self-reported arrest and incarceration; not yet used for this contrast in the repo); the NLSY79 children (Bersani & Pittman); CILS (no third-plus comparator). Each is one cohort, self-reported, with a few hundred Mexican second-generation men. What would settle it: (1) an FSRDC project linking CPS ASEC parental birthplace, or the 2000 decennial long-form and ACS household structure for people observed as children with their parents, to the Census Bureau's Criminal Justice Administrative Records System, which holds state court and corrections records keyed to the Bureau's person identifier ([Census CJARS page](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/cjars.html)); the Opportunity Insights linkage that produced the Mexican second-generation earnings result already observes incarceration on 2010 Census Day for children linked to parents' birthplace and income, so incarceration by parental origin at equal parental income is computable in that infrastructure today [INFERENCE from Abramitzky, Boustan, Jácome & Pérez 2021 and Chetty, Hendren, Jones & Porter 2020; not run]. (2) Texas DPS cannot do it: it fingerprint-matches status, not parentage. (3) The only cheaper route is pooling the three surveys with a common outcome (any arrest by 25) and reporting the Mexican second-generation vs third-plus-white contrast with and without parental income and education; that is a week's work and would give a bound with confidence intervals of several points.

What "cause more crime" would then mean. A raw contrast answers "do they offend more"; the same contrast at equal parental income and place answers "is it generation or class"; neither answers "does admitting their parents raise crime", which needs the counterfactual of the parents' non-admission and the children's non-existence, and is not a measurable quantity. The European register decompositions (ladder 72) show what to expect: about 70% of the first-generation gap is composition, the descendant gap in Denmark and the Netherlands is not. The US self-report evidence so far puts the Mexican second generation at the native level, not above it.

*Contribution.* The annual partial ledger is now computed (§5): Mexican second-generation adults are $6,000–7,600 below white natives, driven by taxes, with cash transfers lower. What is missing for a definitive statement is (1) the health and schooling side by generation, which the existing MEPS transport and ACS school-exposure scripts can produce with the same group definitions in an afternoon; (2) a lifetime, not annual, comparison, which needs age-earnings profiles by generation (the CPS cross-section supports a synthetic-cohort version) and an assumption about the third generation's education; (3) taxes outside the ledger (employer payroll, sales, property, corporate) that scale roughly with earnings and would widen the dollar gap without changing the sign. The sign of "contribute less than white natives" will not change under any of these, because it follows from BA attainment of 25% versus 44% and earnings 71% of white natives'. The open question is the sign of the complete balance, which for the all-origin second generation is positive in NAS 2017 and for the Mexican second generation is undetermined.

## 10. Disconfirmation and limits

- CPS ASEC under-reports program receipt and top-codes earnings; levels are conservative, and gaps are the informative quantity. The 2024 file reproduces every cell within sampling noise.
- Generation-2 and generation-3+ Mexican cells (about 2,500 adults each) give standard errors near 1 point on shares and about $1,500 on medians; the 2024 bracket values show the year-to-year movement.
- Self-identification defines the third-plus group; the Mexican-ancestry population with two US-born parents is larger and better off than the measured one. Nothing here corrects for it.
- The economic tables describe residents in 2024, not the 1980–2000 arrival cohorts' children specifically; second-generation adults 25–64 are the children of arrivals from the 1960s to the 1990s.
- No regression adjustment was run; "the second generation's welfare use exceeds white natives'" is a raw comparison, and the all-origin memo shows most of the generational excess is age and children.
