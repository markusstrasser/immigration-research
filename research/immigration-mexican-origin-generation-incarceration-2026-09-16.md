**Verdict:** The "Mexican second generation is 3.5× native whites" figure is Rumbaut's 2000-census table (US-born Mexican-origin men 18–39 incarcerated 5.90% vs 1.71% for native non-Hispanic whites). It pools second and third-plus generations, and it is 23 years old. Recomputed from ACS 1-year PUMS via the Census API, the same ratio is 2.56× (2010), 1.91× (2019) and 1.72× (2023), or about 2.1× in 2019–2023 once prison records coded generically "Hispanic" are reallocated. US-born Mexican-origin men now institutionalize at about the all-native rate (1.92% vs 2.05% in 2023), so counting them as "native" no longer inflates the native benchmark; removing them moves it by 0.02 points. The Mexican-origin population is 39.0M (CPS 2024): 31% immigrants, 35% second generation, 34% third-plus, and the third-plus share is understated by ethnic attrition. Among low-skill origins, Salvadorans and Guatemalans, Vietnamese, Chinese and (by 2023) Cambodians, Laotians and Hmong have US-born rates at or below Mexican-Americans', and Vietnamese and Chinese second generations out-attain native whites in education and earnings. # Mexican-origin population by generation and the incarceration comparator, 2000–2023

## 1. Where the 3.5× comes from, and what it pools

Rumbaut, Gonzales, Komaie, Morgan & Tafoya-Estrada 2006, Table 1 (2000 census 5% PUMS, men 18–39 in correctional institutions): foreign-born Mexican 0.70%, US-born Mexican 5.90%, native non-Hispanic white 1.71%, all US-born 3.51%, all foreign-born 0.86%, native Black 11.61%. 5.90/1.71 = 3.45. [SOURCE: https://escholarship.org/uc/item/8798n03x, Table 1, verified this session]

The authors flag the pooling themselves: "Almost all of the U.S.-born among those of Latin American and Asian origin can be assumed to consist of second-generation persons—with the exceptions of the Mexicans and Puerto Ricans, who may include among the U.S.-born a sizable but unknown number of third-generation persons." The census and ACS have no parental birthplace, so "US-born Mexican" is a second-plus-third-plus-generation mixture defined by Hispanic self-identification. The one US dataset that separates the Mexican second generation from third-plus non-Hispanic whites on a justice outcome (NLSY97, Inkpen 2024) finds no significant difference in time to first arrest; see [generational crime mechanisms](immigration-generational-crime-mechanisms-2026-09-16.md) §1.

## 3. Does counting the second generation as "native" hide the crime cost?

Two separate claims get merged in that argument.

1. **"Immigrants have lower crime rates than natives" is first-generation-specific.** True and documented across the repo: the adult-arrival floor does not transmit ([generational crime mechanisms](immigration-generational-crime-mechanisms-2026-09-16.md)). The Mexican lineage as a whole (immigrants plus descendants) sits between native whites and the native average, not below both.
2. **"The native benchmark is inflated by second-generation crime."** Arithmetically `r_native = a·r_2 + (1−a)·r_3+` ([crime memo](immigration-crime-rates-unauthorized-vs-native-born.md) §comparator identity). Including Mexican-Americans raises the native benchmark only if their rate exceeds the rest of natives'. In 2000 it did (5.90 vs 3.51), but they were ~5% of native men 18–39, so removing them moves the benchmark from 3.51 to about 3.38. In 2023 their rate (1.92) is below the all-native rate (2.05); removing them raises the benchmark to 2.07. The inflation claim is real in 2000 and gone by 2019–2023. [INFERENCE from the table above]

What survives is the white-comparator version: against native non-Hispanic whites the Mexican-origin US-born rate is 1.7–2.1× in 2023, down from 3.5× in 2000. Whether that residual is behaviour, arrest and prosecution exposure, age structure, or coding is not identified by GQ counts.

## 7. Why did the ratio fall? A state decomposition and the detection-technology hypothesis

Operator hypothesis (2026-09-16): the convergence is a technology effect, with the street crime Mexican-Americans are exposed to becoming easier to detect and less profitable while white crime is more sophisticated and less detectable, and "all crime decreased since better cameras and algorithms".

**State decomposition (same ACS tabulations, `by_state.py`, `for=state:` filter):**

|---|---|---|
| California: Mexican / white / ratio | 3.91 / 1.79 / **2.19×** | 1.73 / 0.75 / **2.30×** |
| Texas | 4.69 / 2.14 / 2.19× | 2.58 / 1.53 / 1.69× |
| All other states | 4.61 / 1.67 / 2.76× | 1.66 / 1.11 / 1.50× |
| US | 4.37 / 1.71 / 2.56× | 1.92 / 1.12 / 1.72× |

Three readings. (1) California's decarceration (realignment 2011, Prop 47 2014, Prop 57 2016) halved both groups' rates and left the ratio unchanged; it is not the source of national convergence. (2) The convergence is in Texas and the rest of the country, where the white native rate fell by a third while the Mexican-American rate fell by almost two-thirds. (3) The US-born Mexican-origin population outside California and Texas nearly doubled (1.09M → 2.01M men 18–39), so part of the national change is the second generation growing up in new destinations with lower rates. [SOURCE: `by_state_result.txt`] [INFERENCE for the readings]

**What the detection-technology hypothesis predicts, and what the record shows.**

- *Detection did not improve.* If cameras, phones and databases raised the probability of catching offenders, clearance rates would rise. FBI clearance by arrest fell for violent crime from 47.5% (2000) to 45.5% (2019) to 36.7% (2022), for property crime from 16.7% to 17.2% to 12.1%, and for homicide from 93% (1962) to 64% (1994), 61% (2019) and 54% (2020) (Cook & Mancik 2023, *Annual Review of Criminology*, DOI 10.1146/annurev-criminol-022422-122744). Cook and Mancik's preferred explanation is a rising evidentiary standard for arrest; prison admissions per homicide rose while clearance fell. The falling clearance is concentrated in gun homicides with Black victims. So "algorithms catch street criminals more often" is not what happened at the arrest margin. [SOURCE: https://drugpolicyfacts.org/table/clearance (FBI UCR series); Cook & Mancik 2023]
- *Timing.* The large crime decline was 1991–2000, before camera networks, license-plate readers or predictive tools existed at scale; the second decline (2007–2014) precedes most of them too. Incarceration peaked in 2008–09 and fell for policy reasons (drug and property reclassification, sentencing reform, COVID releases) on top of lower crime. The institutionalization series here is mostly a punishment series, not a crime series. [INFERENCE; policy chronology is standard]
- *The version with evidence is opportunity and deterrence, not detection.* Farrell, Tseloni, Mailley & Tilley 2011 (*JRCD* 48(2), DOI 10.1177/0022427810391539): electronic immobilizers and central locking drove the vehicle-theft collapse, and reduced "debut" crimes may have cut onset into other offending. Edlund & Machado, "It's the Phone, Stupid: Mobiles and Murder" (NBER w25883): county antenna build-out lowered 1990s homicide, with effects concentrated in urban counties, among Black and Hispanic young men, and in gang and drug-related killings; the mechanism is that phones dissolved turf-based street dealing and its profits. Klick, MacDonald & Stratmann 2012 find state-level phone penetration associated with fewer rapes and assaults, not property crime. Doleac 2017 (*AEJ: Applied* 9(1)): DNA database expansions deter profiled offenders and reduce crime rates. This is the operator's mechanism in a specific form: technology removed the profit from the crime types to which young urban Hispanic and Black men were most exposed (turf drug markets, car theft), and that shows up as fewer of them entering the system. It is a 1990s–2000s story that would explain the 2000→2010 step better than the 2010→2023 one. [SOURCE: the four papers, abstracts and NBER text read this session]
- *"White crime is more sophisticated."* Fraud and financial crime are under-prosecuted, so an incarceration comparator understates white *harm*; but native whites in prison are there mainly for drugs, violence and property, the same offences as everyone else, and the white native rate also fell by a third. The relevant white-specific 2010s shock is the opioid and methamphetamine wave in rural and small-town areas, where jail incarceration rose while urban jails emptied; that is the leading candidate for why white rates fell less outside California, and it is a drug-epidemic effect, not a detection-technology one. [INFERENCE; the rural-jail divergence is documented by Vera's Incarceration Trends but not re-verified here]

**Where this leaves the hypothesis.** Rejected in the "better cameras and algorithms → more detection → less crime" form: detection fell. Supported in the "technology removed the opportunity and profit from street crime types" form for the 1990s, with published causal evidence that the effect was concentrated among young Hispanic and Black men. Untested for 2010–2023, where the within-state pattern (flat ratio in California, convergence elsewhere) points to differential drug-epidemic exposure and to compositional change in where the second generation grows up. The decisive next test is offence composition: if the Mexican-American decline is disproportionately drug and property offences while the white decline is not, the opportunity story survives into the 2010s; BJS National Prisoner Statistics by Hispanic origin × offence can show it. [INFERENCE]

**The structural fact.** European tables exist because of population registers: a personal ID number links birth, parents' birthplace, residence, education, income and convictions for everyone. The Rockwool 2026 study (Andersen, Andersen, Bäckman, Christensen, van Hazebroek, Mitchell, Nieuwbeerta, Skardhamar et al., Study Paper 283) runs full-population male 15–30 conviction data for Denmark 1991–2017, Sweden 1991–2017, Norway 1995–2017, and the Netherlands and New Zealand from 2006, with a binary western/non-western origin coding and reweighting decompositions on own and parental education, employment and income (education unavailable for the Netherlands and New Zealand). Read in full on 2026-09-16 (63 pp.; the paper prints no tables and no standard errors, so every magnitude below is a chart read). Findings: convictions fell for every group in every country; the immigrant gap "would have been more than halved" under identical composition, with the terminal-year share closed running from 63% (Sweden) to 100% (Norway); Denmark's composition-adjusted immigrant gap is *not* negligible, "relatively stable at an average of +20" over the last decade, and only Norway's goes to zero; the descendant gap is not composition, about 205% above comparable majority men in Denmark and 265% in the Netherlands, and Denmark's conditional descendant gap has risen from roughly 100 to 205 between 2006 and 2017; New Zealand's non-western groups convict *below* the majority in both generations. The MENAPT versus Southeast Asian split is not in this paper (footnote 5 says the binary coding was forced); it comes from the Danish summaries of a companion analysis and is carried here at that lower grade. The paper does not discuss differential policing, selective return migration or register coverage as limitations. [SOURCE: Study Paper 283 PDF, pp. 6, 14 and Figures 2–4, verified 2026-09-16; https://en.rockwoolfonden.dk/publications/a-statistical-decomposition-of-nativity-gaps-in-criminal-convictions-using-full-population-data-from-five-developed-democracies/; `../infra/immigration-fiscal/rockwool_full_2026_09_16/RESULT.md`] The US has no register, no personal ID linkage across agencies, and the Census dropped the parental-birthplace question after 1970 (CPS restored it in 1994; ACS never had it). **The informal-economy channel (unreported earnings, off-the-books firms).** It is a first-generation channel. SSA's Chief Actuary (Actuarial Note 151, 2013, as restated by CRS IF10820) counts 7.0M unauthorized workers in 2010: 3.1M paying payroll tax (1.8M of them on someone else's number, which lands in the Earnings Suspense File, $98B of wages in TY2016) and 3.9M in the underground economy, so 56% off the books. Bernhardt et al. 2009 independently find 57% of low-wage immigrant workers with no pay stub. IRS net misreporting is 1% for withheld wages and 57% for non-farm proprietor income (Pub 5784), which is the mechanism: unreported income is a self-employment and cash-wage phenomenon. Two routes price the lost tax at about $2,400 per unauthorized adult-year (SSA bottom-up: off-books share × average earnings × combined rate, net of suspense-file credits; ITEP's legalization counterfactual, +$40.2B on $96.7B paid), and Hurst, Li & Pugsley 2014 show households understate self-employment income by about 25% for natives too, so the ledger should book the *differential* exposure, which pulls the line to −$1,500 to −$2,400. The CPS ASEC 2025 tabulation that resolves the differential: self-employment income is 7.0% of earnings for the Mexico-born against 4.2% for third-plus whites, and 3.5% for the Mexican second generation, 3.4% for the Mexican third-plus; unincorporated self-employment is 8.5% of the employed Mexico-born, 4.7% of the Mexican second generation, 6.0% of whites. So the Mexican second generation is *less* exposed to unreported self-employment income than the white reference, and the channel books at $0 for it (the one surviving second-generation mechanism, formal-system avoidance by US-born children of unauthorized parents, Desai, Su & Adelman 2019, bounds at about −$317 per adult-year, 3.5% of the gap). For the Mexico-born cell it is a widening of roughly −$550 to −$900 per adult-year once the unauthorized share of Mexico-born adults (about 37% [TRAINING-DATA: Pew 2022 estimates]) is applied. On competitiveness, the one costed number is that full-workforce misclassification lets a contractor underbid a compliant one by 4.9% of project cost (16.1% of labour cost; Ormiston, Belman & Hinkel, RSMeans costing), while the Georgia administrative-record studies (Hotchkiss, Quispe-Agnoli & Rios-Avila) find documented-worker wage effects under 1% with unstable sign; the damage runs through tax and insurance evasion rather than wage undercutting. The asymmetry is the finding: the channel widens the first-generation gap and leaves the second-generation gap intact, so it increases measured convergence. ## 13. What the ledger cannot see: housing prices and capacity costs

The ledger is a head count times an average operating cost. Three channels sit outside that form: prices (rents), capital and capacity (new prisons, school seats), and congestion on incumbents (class size, waits). Two lanes on 2026-09-16 measured what can be measured.

**Housing.** The canonical elasticity is Saiz 2007: +1% of population from immigration raises rents and values about 1% (range 0.8–1.6; Mariel: renters +9% in a year gave rents +8–11%, concentrated in low-quality units occupied by poor Hispanic renters). The best-identified modern estimate is Cabral & Steingress (EER 2026): +2.2% rents and +3.8% prices per 1% of county population, split between +6–8% in permit-restrictive counties and 0 to −2% in permissive ones, which is the published form of the repo's own +1.4% inelastic-metro incidence (ladder 57). Renters at the bottom of the quality distribution bear it; owners capitalise it. Removal is not arrival with a minus sign: Howard, Wang & Zhang (Nov 2025), on the staggered Secure Communities rollout, find residential construction −5.7%, new-construction prices +4.5%, existing-stock prices zero on average with a gradient from +3.4% in tracts with the fewest low-education foreign-born to −4.1% in the most, and homeownership −93 bp, because undocumented construction labour complements domestic labour. The story the operator recalled is real, Doral, Florida (WSJ, late September 2025: rents at a three-year low, vacancy 6.5% to over 10%), and it does not hold in the repeat-rent index: Zillow's Doral series was $2,864.6 in October 2025, above five months of 2023–24, with its trough of $2,837 arriving in January 2026, about 2 points below the national. Across seven 2025–26 stories, no metro shows a deportation-timed rent collapse; Austin's −10.8% from peak is a 2022 completions event three years before the enforcement surge, San Diego (cited at −5.7%) is at an all-time high, and the "first national rent decline in five years" is an asking-rent series (Apartment List) while the repeat-rent index rose 4.8%. Konczal's arithmetic, removing 1M renters saves the average tenant about $4.40 a month, is the right order of magnitude. A verified negative: the citation "Howard, Liebersohn & Ozimek 2023, JUE" used in the lane brief does not exist. [SOURCE: Saiz 2007 JUE 61(2) doi:10.1016/j.jue.2006.07.004; Saiz 2003 REStat 85(3); Cabral & Steingress EER 182 (2026) / BoC SWP 24-40; Howard, Wang & Zhang 2025 (trouphoward.com); Wilson & Zhou Dallas Fed WP 2607 (2026); Zillow ZORI city CSV re-read 2026-09-16; `../infra/immigration-fiscal/housing_deport_2026_09_16/RESULT.md`]

**Capacity and congestion.** NAS 2016 (ch. 7–8) names congestible public goods, says most studies attribute them per capita, and runs its eight scenarios varying only the pure public goods (defense, interest); congestible goods get one convention, average cost, and no scenario prices marginal cost above average. NAS's own sensitivity across the two conventions it does run is $160,000 of lifetime NPV per immigrant, and it says congestion "may be irrelevant" for a one-year marginal calculation but is "a central concern" for long-run costs of a growing population; the ledger here is a long-run comparison on the short-run convention. Prison capital: recent state prisons cost about $270k–$290k per bed (Alabama Elmore 4,000 beds $1.08B; Utah; Indiana), or $10.5k–$15.6k per prisoner-year amortised at 30–50 years and 3–4%, on top of $57.9k operating, so +18% to +27% where capacity binds. Whether it binds splits by state: BJS Prisoners in 2023 Table 21 puts state custody at 98.6% of lowest capacity and 88.7% of highest (my re-aggregation on the 40 jurisdictions with full data: 96.7% and 87.9%), eight states over their most generous measure, 22 under 85%, the federal system at 100% of rated. Per adult 25–64 the capital term is $46–$140 of difference against whites at realistic costs (§12 rates), under 3% of the fiscal gap. Congestion on incumbents is weak where measured: Figlio & Özek 2019 find precise zeros on incumbent Florida students from the Haitian influx, powered below 1% of a standard deviation, with the scope condition of a small shock into a prepared system; CBO's 2025 state and local report scores schools as an unpriced residual. The honest statement is two-sided: where capacity is slack, average cost overstates marginal cost. Gaps: school construction cost per seat, ICE bed-day cost, hospital capacity. [SOURCE: NAS 2016 ch. 7–8; BJS NCJ 310197 Table 21 (p23st.zip, re-aggregated 2026-09-16); al.com 2024-04-25; CBO 61256; Figlio & Özek 2019; `../infra/immigration-fiscal/capacity_sweep_2026_09_16/RESULT.md`] [FRAMING-SENSITIVE: the average-vs-marginal convention is the single largest discretionary choice in any immigration fiscal accounting]

**Schools: is there an incumbent-student cost from immigrant or English-learner concentration?** Not in US data, and the design fact that decides it is Figlio, Giuliano, Özek & Sapienza (ReStud 2024; NBER w28596, Florida, about 1.3M students): a school-fixed-effects regression of native achievement on immigrant share gives a negative that flips positive under sibling fixed effects (+2.8% SD math, +1.7% SD reading for exposure rising from 1% to 13%), so any district- or school-level English-learner-share regression measures family sorting, not classroom effects. Diette & Oyelere (IZA J Migration 2017) ran Latin-American share against limited-English share in one North Carolina dataset: origin share has no effect, limited-English share a small negative for boys and Black students, zero for girls; the cost attaches to English-learner status, not ethnicity, and the Mexican second generation is English-proficient by school age (Hunt, JHR 2017, finds the positive on native high-school completion null specifically for native-born Hispanics). No concentration threshold is supported; the one paper with a teacher-time mechanism (Irazoque 2026, Venezuelans in Colombia, −1.8% SD) loses significance at higher concentration. NAEP does not carry a literacy story: English-learner fourth-grade reading held flat 2019→2022 while most groups fell, and the 2024 decline hit both. Teacher turnover in ESL and bilingual posts runs 19% against about 15%, and 27% of California English-language-development posts were unfilled in 2020–21, a certification-supply problem with no causal link to inflows. The one robust behavioural response is Betts & Fairlie's native flight to private secondary schools, about one native per four immigrants, triggered by non-English home language. Verified negative: no administrative-data study of the 2022–24 New York, Chicago or Denver surge cohorts measures incumbent outcomes. So the school channel is head-count operating cost plus the still-unpriced capital cost per seat; the classroom-quality line books at $0 per pupil-year.
