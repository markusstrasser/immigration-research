**Verdict:** Evidence supports substantial adverse spillovers in particular settings, but does not establish an overall loss for existing residents from low-skill immigration. The strongest skeptical case combines persistent low earnings, costly redistribution, rapid inflows into constrained housing/services, and difficult integration. GDP growth cannot settle the balance. [INFERENCE from sources below]

# What follows from the second-order costs argument

September 5, 2026. Frame: existing residents' living standards and institutional quality, distinguishing workers, renters, owners and taxpayers. An all-resident or global objective would also count migrants' gains. [FRAMING-SENSITIVE]

Employers can profit while other residents bear housing, service and coordination costs. If those uncompensated costs exceed incumbent benefits, welfare falls despite production growth. Establishing that net balance for a defined admission policy is the unresolved step. [INFERENCE]

## GDP does not settle incumbent welfare

GDP measures domestic value added, including output serving newcomers. Government purchases and payroll count; transfers are not themselves production. Extra work maintaining an existing service level can raise measured GDP without improving welfare, and uses resources with alternative purposes. [SOURCE: BEA [GDP accounting](https://bea.gov/index.php/news/blog/2025-06-03/expenditures-approach-measuring-gdp), [government spending](https://www.bea.gov/help/faq/552), [well-being framework](https://apps.bea.gov/well-being/archive/docs/background-materials-prototype-measures-of-economic-well-being-and-growth.pdf); INFERENCE about the policy comparison]

GDP per resident also mixes composition with outcomes. **Illustration, fixed prices and output equal to income:** 100 residents earning $60,000 produce $6m. Ten newcomers earn $30,000 each. If incumbents now earn $60,500, output reaches $6.35m while the population mean falls to $57,727. If incumbents instead earn $59,500, output still rises to $6.25m. Higher total GDP and a lower population mean therefore accommodate either incumbent gains or losses. [CALCULATION; hypothetical, not an immigration estimate]

Our native/Mexico-born fiscal and racial wage comparisons describe resident populations. Birthplace, education, earnings, English proficiency and unauthorized status are distinct; these comparisons do not identify a low-skill admission policy's causal effects. [SOURCE: [current findings](immigration-clarity-update-2026-09-05.md), [glossary](immigration-glossary.md); INFERENCE]

## Evidence by mechanism

| Mechanism | Checked evidence | What limits the conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Housing demand | Wilson–Zhou estimate higher local rents and house prices from March 2021–March 2024 unauthorized-worker inflows, using an administrative measure and leave-out shift-share instrument. [March 2026 working paper, Table 6](https://www.dallasfed.org/~/media/documents/research/papers/2026/wp2607.pdf) | Identification depends on instrument assumptions. Rent increases partly redistribute between tenants and owners; they are not a national resource-loss total or an intake threshold. |
| Local services | CBO estimates a **$9.2bn direct 2023 state/local net cost** from its specified surge, or **$9.8bn in a broader alternative** including crowding and broader revenues. Some translation, legal and administrative costs already enter. [June 2025 report](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2025-06/61256-immigration-state-local.pdf) | Budget-based counterfactual estimates, partly allocated using demographics. Alternatives cannot be added; the surge is mixed-status, not skill-defined. |
| Federal finances | CBO's **July 2024 forecast** projects roughly **$0.9tn lower deficits over 2024–2034**, excluding discretionary spending; a separate illustration adds **$0.2tn discretionary funding**. [CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/60569) | An older conditional forecast, not realized receipts or a current projection. Its decade cannot be directly netted against one year's local cost. |
| Language administration | California reports a **$133.2m average annual court-interpreter appropriation, FY2020–21 through FY2023–24**, plus recruitment, training and scheduling demands. [2025 study, pp.1–3](https://languageaccess.courts.ca.gov/system/files/2025-07/2025%20Language%20Need%20and%20Interpreter%20Use%20Study.pdf) | Whole-program funding includes existing residents and deaf/hard-of-hearing users, not marginal immigration costs. Wages, contracting and eligibility also affect spending. |
| Construction supply | Howard–Wang–Zhang find construction falling and new-home prices rising after Secure Communities enforcement, using staggered-treatment imputation. [November 2025 manuscript](https://www.trouphoward.com/uploads/1/2/7/7/127764736/howard_wang_and_zhang_-_cracking_down_pricing_up_-_nov_2025.pdf) | Short/medium-run disruption of established networks; the foreign-born low-education proxy includes lawful residents. Removal is not the inverse of new admissions, but a demand-only housing account misses this supply channel. |
| Competing workers | Czech commuters entering German border markets reduced native wages and employment. [Dustmann–Schönberg–Stuhler, 2016 manuscript of 2017 paper](https://docs.iza.org/dp10114.pdf) | Commuters lacked residence, limiting household-demand effects. Lower local employment includes foregone entry, not necessarily national job destruction. |
| Prices and tasks | Cortes finds lower immigrant-intensive service prices; Peri–Sparber simulate task adjustment mitigating low-education native wage losses from mixed-skill 1990s inflows. [Cortes](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/589756), [Peri–Sparber](https://giovanniperi.ucdavis.edu/uploads/5/6/8/2/56826033/peri_sparber_task_specialization_immigration_2010.pdf) | Real incumbent benefits can coexist with competitor losses. Sector-specific prices and task simulations do not establish complete welfare effects or universal adjustment. |
| Automation | Bracero exclusion induced mechanization/crop changes without statistically detected domestic wage or employment gains. [Clemens–Lewis–Postel, 2017 manuscript of 2018 paper](https://docs.iza.org/dp10512.pdf) | Historical agriculture with available technology; domestic is not strictly native-born. Automation's capital costs and worker outcomes determine its welfare value. |
| Trust | A review of 1,001 estimates from 87 studies finds a pooled partial correlation of **−0.0256** between ethnic diversity and trust, stronger for neighbors/local diversity but also negative for generalized trust. [Dinesen–Schaeffer–Sønderskov, 2020](https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/230771020/Ethnic_Diversity_and_Social_Trust_Final_version_2020.pdf) | Not a percentage decline. Predominantly observational cross-sections do not identify a low-skill admissions effect or its welfare magnitude. |
| Politics | Tabellini finds restriction/redistribution responses alongside native male employment gains in 1910–1930 US cities, using war/quota shocks and settlement instruments. [Paper, §§5–7](https://igier.unibocconi.eu/sites/default/files/media/attach/Gifts_of_the_Immigrants,_Woes_of_the_Natives_Tabellini.pdf) | Wages are not directly observed; income uses occupational proxies. | Ethnic networks | Swiss refugee assignment evidence finds co-national networks helping employment in 2008–2013 subsidiary-protection cohorts. Concentration is not automatically integration failure; incumbent net welfare is not established. |

## When the economic case for restrictions holds

Guerreiro–Rebelo–Teles show that a static native-welfare model can favor excluding low-skill entrants when optimal redistribution benefits low-skill natives and newcomers must receive identical tax/transfer treatment. Different immigrant taxation can instead permit entry with congestion charges. The theory assumes strong substitution and single-worker households, omits descendants and abstracts from other externalities. Its quantitative section relaxes within-skill native/immigrant substitution and calibrates 1994–2008. This is a conditional economic case, not an estimate of today's complete balance. [SOURCE: [NBER26154, February 2020 revision](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26154/w26154.pdf), §§1–2,4.2–5; [published article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmoneco.2020.04.009)]

## Deductions and the remaining test

1. **Judge the next arrivals against local capacity.** Pace, destination and composition matter: arrivals can congest services or supply construction and care. Past averages and employer demand do not establish the next admission's public value. [INFERENCE]
2. **Measure complexity through burdens and outcomes.** Staffing, compliance time, processing delays, service quality, trust and conflict have different mechanisms. Some burdens depend on policy design; counting laws or forms does not measure welfare. [INFERENCE]
3. **Count each effect once.** Interpreter payroll already in court budgets is not an extra cost. Count service deterioration remaining after spending, not also the deterioration that spending prevents. Productivity, wages, profits and resulting taxes likewise overlap as measures of gains. [ACCOUNTING INFERENCE]
4. **Precaution is a decision under uncertainty.** Protecting vulnerable incumbents or avoiding difficult-to-reverse institutional risks can justify cautious admissions under stated risk weights. It does not establish that all missing effects are negative or that current policy has a measured net loss. [FRAMING-SENSITIVE]

A stronger overall conclusion needs a defined admission cohort and credible comparison areas: incumbent real disposable incomes by worker/renter status, spending **and** service quality, housing demand **and** supply, and panel trust/cooperation outcomes. The comparison must isolate admission exposure from pre-existing trends and destination selection. A consistent discounted balance—or bounds establishing its sign—would resolve more than regressions on immigrant shares that leave selection unanswered. [INFERENCE]
