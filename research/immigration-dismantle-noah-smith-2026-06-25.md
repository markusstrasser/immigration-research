# Noah Smith's pro-immigration economics — corrected assessment

**Date:** 2026-06-25; materially revised 2026-09-05. **Section of:** consolidated dismantling memo.

**Verdict:** Smith's average-effect arguments and his concern about local surge costs can both be true. The June memo too often treated a local cost as a refutation of an aggregate claim, and inferred causal or distributional conclusions from unmatched comparisons. This revision retains the questions that evidence can support and withdraws those inferences. [INFERENCE]

**Scope:** The June pass recorded primary retrieval of Smith's essays through Exa. Retained quotations preserve that record; the September pass checks material inferences rather than certifying every quotation. See [instrument caveat](../notes/llm-bias-caveat.md). Prior repo findings are locators, not independent confirmation of themselves.

## Average effects and local pressure are compatible

Smith's [Mass deportation would accomplish nothing](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/mass-deportation-would-accomplish) argues that demand and supply responses can leave average wage and cost-of-living effects small. In [America, please be reasonable on immigration](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/america-please-be-reasonable-on-immigration), he writes:

> "But to be fair to the Republicans here, immigration, unlike the economy, actually is a big problem right now. Since 2021, there has been an absolute flood of people crossing the southwestern border illegally… **This is a massive problem.** It's a short-term logistical problem in terms of how to house and feed the millions pouring into the country — a task that is now straining the resources of many heavily Democratic cities. It's **a long-term fiscal problem, because these people will require heavy government support for their health care, housing, and education; this will end up coming from city and state governments**, since they're barred from federal welfare programs." [SOURCE: noahpinion.blog/p/america-please-be-reasonable-on-immigration, verbatim, 2024-01-29]

The June memo also retained this characterization of his policy preference; it is a summary with embedded quotations, not one continuous verbatim statement:

> Smith advocates prioritizing high-skilled legal immigration while accepting "some low-skilled immigrants, but far less than 2 million per year," characterizing mass low-skilled migration as **an "altruistic luxury" versus an economic necessity.** [SOURCE: same essay]

A small national average, a costly local adjustment and opposition to mass deportation are logically compatible. So are positive total fiscal effects and a negative municipal balance. The existence of different emphases across essays does not demonstrate dishonesty, suppression of costs or an internal contradiction. A stronger charge would need a specific statement that extends an average result to every person, locality or period. [INFERENCE]

## Claim 1 — Native wages

Immigration changes labor supply, labor demand, capital accumulation and task allocation. Demand does not mechanically offset supply one-for-one; the size and timing are empirical. The established small average native-wage effects discussed in Smith's [wage essay](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/why-immigration-doesnt-reduce-wages) do not imply that every competing worker benefits. [SOURCE: essay; [NAS Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/23550/chapter/2); INFERENCE]

The repo's E-Verify exercise uses QWI's **EarnS: average monthly earnings of stable/full-quarter workers**. The relevant QWI endpoint has no nativity/citizenship variable, so this is neither a native-born-only outcome nor an hourly wage. Its policy coefficient also is not a direct immigration elasticity: the confidence interval cannot rule out a response to a different inflow without the policy's effect on immigration and an identification argument linking the two. The Mariel dispute concerns another population and design. The June inference to native wages or the recent surge was too strong. [SOURCE: [Census QWI variables](https://api.census.gov/data/timeseries/qwi/se/variables.html); INFERENCE; [conceptual audit](immigration-conceptual-audit-2026-09-05.md)]

The [July 2024 CBO report](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2024-07/60165-Immigration.pdf) separately models incumbent wage effects and adjustment. That is more relevant than asserting that all earlier studies lie outside the surge's support. A lack of established external validity warrants uncertainty; it does not establish large negative effects. [SOURCE; INFERENCE]

## Claim 2 — Housing and the cost of living

Smith acknowledges local housing costs, including "Immigration… might be straining the housing markets in a few big cities" and "These prices should go up (and in the case of housing, **it definitely does**)." Those concessions must be retained when assessing his [rents essay](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/is-immigration-raising-rents-in-america) and deportation essay. [SOURCE: June quote record]

The repo's metro panel reports a foreign-born-share/rent-level correlation around +0.69, rising to +0.74 in one supply-elasticity group, and a rent-growth correlation around −0.17. These describe sorting and co-movement; they do not identify immigration's causal contribution to either rent levels or the national rent spike. A negative growth correlation does not establish that immigration made no contribution, and a positive level correlation does not establish the size of a demand shock. [SOURCE: [metro panel](immigration-msa-rent-elasticity-panel-2026-06-25.md); INFERENCE]

The [Dallas Fed study](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/papers/2026/wp2607) offers separate econometric evidence of effects on housing prices and rents. Its estimates are specific to its period, population, treatment denominator and identification assumptions. A statistically insignificant permit response is not proof of no supply response or a permanently binding capacity ceiling. [SOURCE; INFERENCE]

**The question that remains:** tenants, landlords and purchasers of immigrant-intensive services can face different changes. A rent increase is partly an income transfer to landlords, not wholly a loss of national output. To disprove a small net cost-of-living effect requires a comparable basket and population; to demonstrate regressive net incidence requires households' combined gains and losses. The June memo established neither by listing the rent channel alone. [ACCOUNTING / INFERENCE]

## Claim 3 — Inflation

Smith's [disinflation essay](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/did-immigration-bring-down-inflation) disputes the claim that immigration was the main cause of 2023 disinflation. That is compatible with an effect on particular sectors or places. A shelter-price effect does not refute a claim about the dominant cause of headline inflation, and the shelter share of the CPI is not a causal attribution. The repo's descriptive rent-growth correlation cannot settle that attribution either. [INFERENCE]

The precise national contribution requires a counterfactual and timing evidence; this repair does not supply a new inflation decomposition. Smith's narrow argument should be evaluated as stated, rather than against an impression inferred from its title. [SCOPE LIMIT]

## Claim 4 — Fiscal effects

The [July 2024 CBO report](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2024-07/60165-Immigration.pdf) projects approximately **$0.9T lower covered federal deficits** and **$8.9T additional nominal GDP** over 2024–2034 from the incremental 2021–2026 surge. The headline deficit result excludes discretionary appropriations and state/local budgets. These are projected counterfactual differences, not realized totals. The GDP and deficit quantities are not additive welfare benefits. [SOURCE: summary and Figure 2]

The separate [June 2025 state/local report](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2025-06/61256-immigration-state-local.pdf) estimates a negative state/local balance for the surge population in 2023. It addresses another government level and period. That finding can coexist with a positive federal or total balance; it does not make a net-positive aggregate statement false. Matching periods and populations is necessary before aggregating. [SOURCE; INFERENCE]

Smith's quoted concern about city/state budgets establishes that he recognizes this distinction. Exclusion from a federal benefit does not mechanically transfer its entire cost to states: state provision, unmet need, private spending and behavioral responses differ by program. Schoolchildren counts can locate demand but are neither incremental school costs nor a net fiscal balance. [INFERENCE]

The Colas–Sachs indirect fiscal channel is relevant, but it is not proof that every NAS estimate is an upper bound and cannot simply be added to CBO, which already models indirect effects. The correct question is whether Smith's particular fiscal claim has the same population and accounting scope as its evidence. This memo does not establish a general federal-versus-local sign law. [SOURCE: [Colas–Sachs](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20220176); conceptual audit §§7–8; INFERENCE]

## Claim 5 — Crime

The reported Texas individual-level arrest and conviction comparisons support lower recorded rates for unauthorized immigrants than for all native-born residents in the periods studied. They do not identify a nationwide causal effect of status, and arrests, convictions and incarceration are distinct outcomes. A homicide-conviction result does not validate every arrest ratio or eliminate all measurement differences. [SOURCE: [Light, He and Robey](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2014704117); [Cato Texas report](https://www.cato.org/policy-analysis/illegal-immigrant-murderers-texas-2013-2022)]

The June claim that comparison with non-Hispanic-white natives is race-matching for predominantly Hispanic immigrants was incorrect. It changes the comparator; it does not make the all-native descriptive rate dishonest or inflated. A causal analysis would need a justified adjustment set and comparable outcomes, periods and populations. [INFERENCE]

Nor can a null county-level association with unauthorized share overturn individual rate comparisons. For generations, `r_native = a*r2 + (1-a)*r3`; adding second-generation residents raises the native comparator relative to third-plus natives only if `r2 > r3`. Knowing merely that `r2 > r1` does not determine the sign. The appropriate conclusion is that first-generation evidence does not automatically answer a descendant-inclusive question. [FORMAL CHECK; conceptual audit §6]

## Claim 6 — Mass deportation

Smith's statement that its economic benefits "would likely be negative overall" is not refuted by identifying one local benefit or a harmful sectoral effect. The title's “nothing” should not be interpreted as asserting literally zero physical changes when the essay discusses costs. Output losses from removing workers also are not automatically equal to incumbent welfare losses; first-order production arithmetic is not a complete policy counterfactual. [SOURCE: deportation essay; INFERENCE]

Reversing an admission scenario is not generally the same policy as deporting established residents: timing, selection, enforcement costs and adaptation differ. A net evaluation must include those differences and relevant humanitarian and distributional objectives. The June memo's literal-title rebuttal and automatic CBO-reversal argument are withdrawn. [INFERENCE]

## Assessment and limits

Smith's essays leave useful questions about adjustment, incidence and policy comparison. This pass does not demonstrate that their average conclusions are false or that the author hides local costs. It also does not establish that all effects are small. The distinction is between a supported estimate with limits and an unsupported universal extrapolation. No corrected national wage, rent, crime or fiscal magnitude is asserted here, and no local SIPP-based fiscal output is used while its identified construction defects remain unresolved.
