# 2026-10-05: The main case counts the descendants who stopped identifying as Mexican: $390.3–461.2bn

## Context

The v4 main case ($371.4–434.8bn) prices 39,712,493 people: the Mexico-born, the US-born with a Mexico-born parent,
and the US-born who report Mexican origin. Descendants of Mexican immigrants who no longer report Mexican origin were
outside it. They were left out because a survey cannot see them, not because they cost nothing: a zero set by
convention. On 2026-10-05 the operator asked that the main case move to include them ("shouldn't the main case move
then? I mean it doesn't matter if it's like only a few M or B").

Three measurements make that possible:

- **How many.** The CPS links children to co-resident parents, so third-generation identity loss is observed: 11.2%
  today (ladder 158). One further step is measured for children of identified third-plus parents (12.0%;
  identity_loss_propagation_2026_09_27, arm b, central there). On the account's frame (factor 0.997189: the row-4
  reweight touches only the Mexico-born) that is 3.04M people beyond the union, a lineage of 42.75M.
- **What they are like.** Adults lost one generation later are not ahead of identifiers (carryover_identity_2026_09_27
  §2). Third-generation adults who do not identify close C3 of the identifiers' college gap to third-plus whites:
  IPUMS-CPS basic monthly files 1994–2026 give 0.57 (SE 0.26) from 526 unique adults, and pooled with NLSY97
  C3 = 0.557 (SE 0.246) (g3_identity_pooled_2026_10_05; ladder 280).
- **What they cost.** Priced under v4's own rules on the engine with the enlarged group, so the finite-removal responses
  see the larger group (main_case_lineage_2026_10_05).

## Alternatives considered

1. **Keep v4's frame.** Coherent as "people who are Mexico-born or say they are Mexican", but it prices identity, not
   the population the immigration produced. Rejected: the operator's standard is the full cost, and the people and
   their costs are measured.
2. **Count people by their share of Mexican-immigrant ancestry** (½ per Mexico-born parent, ¼ per grandparent): the
   people-conserving rule of the lineage cost lane. Over the whole lineage it gives $275.4–375.8bn: the third-plus share
   is only a stated bound (0.30–0.67), because 70% of third-plus members have no grandparent data; every unknown at
   nothing or in full gives $239.6–422.1bn. Rejected as the headline, kept beside as the ancestry-share reading. The
   account asks what the people who exist cost others; a mixed person's other ancestry already shows in their measured
   cost (third-generation non-identifiers close 56% of the gap), so cutting the head count as well would count it
   twice. The operator chose whole people ("A then", 2026-10-05 22:54 JST).
3. **Net of a replacement child** (a native parent would likely have had a child with someone else). Not used: the
   account is a static removal and models no alternative history for anyone; the operator declined the frame. Kept as
   a footnote in the lineage lane ($383.4–450.7bn at full replacement).
4. **Arm a or arm c as the central.** Arm a (losses stop at the third-generation rate, 1.81M) ignores a measured further
   loss; arm c (losses compound, 4.27M) needs an assumed generation mix. Both stay as the band: $380.4–447.6bn and
   $400.2–474.9bn.

## Decision

The main case is v4 plus the lineage on arm b, counted as whole people: **$390.3–461.2bn a year** at specifications
48 / 11, or **$9,129–10,789 per member** of the 42.75M-person lineage. Change from v4: +$18.9 / +$26.4bn. The cash set
is $307.4–383.4bn. C3 ± 1 SE gives $387.3–465.2bn.

- Later losses (1.09M) cost what an identified third-plus member costs: $8,541 / 11,731.
- Third-generation-rate losses and their descendants (1.94M) cost (1 − C3) of that plus C3 of a third-plus non-Hispanic
  white of the same ages ($2,274 / 3,472): $5,052 / 7,133.
- [FRAMING-SENSITIVE] Whole people, as the account counts its members.
- [FRAMING-SENSITIVE] The added people are priced at the identified third-plus age mix; the bias most likely runs low
  (children cost more and went unidentified more often in 1994–2006).

## Correction, 2026-10-08

Alternative 2's last argument is wrong: "a mixed person's other ancestry already shows in their measured cost ... so
cutting the head count as well would count it twice." A member's measured cost and the share of it attributed to an
origin are separate quantities. Splitting a $6,000 member half and half gives $3,000 + $3,000, and nothing is counted
twice. The outside red team of 2026-10-08 found this on all four axes of its FAQ packet; the lead verified it. The
choice of whole people rests on the record's first reason, unchanged: the account asks what the people who exist cost
others, and it models no alternative history for anyone (alternative 3). The ancestry share answers a different
question, an allocation that must add to a national total across origin accounts, and it stays beside the headline.
FAQ 19 was corrected the same day. The operator was told, since the argument was part of what he chose on.
