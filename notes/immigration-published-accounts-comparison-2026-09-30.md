Date: 2026-09-30. Working note for the essay's novelty and completeness claims, not a finding.

Provenance: a researcher subagent (claude-opus-5-5) wrote this on 2026-09-30 from search
excerpts, repo memos and one page-level fetch. No study was read end to end, and every grade is
an LLM's [INFERENCE] about scope, not about quality. The lead verified on the source pages only
the two Manhattan Institute quotes (the 2024-09-19 report and the 2025-10-23 update). The rest is
[UNVERIFIED] until a study is read. Before the essay leans on a row, read that study's methods
section, starting with the gaps listed below.

**Verdict:** "Nobody has done even half of this" fails if you count channels. At least eight accounts touch 4–6 of our 8 channels in partial form: Denmark's Finance Ministry, van de Beek et al. (Netherlands), Suomen Perusta (Finland), SSB (Norway), CEPII (France), Konjunkturinstitutet (Sweden), NAS 2017 and Cato 2026 (all US immigrants). Manhattan Institute 2025 has a federal-only Mexico row. A narrower claim holds within everything searched: no account puts social costs to other residents (2), their distribution by income (3) or a sending-country ledger (5) in the same ledger as the fiscal account. None covers the Mexican-origin population of all generations. None adds a return on public capital, government enterprises and accrued pensions to an annual account. None is substantial on 4 channels; the best, van de Beek, is substantial on 2.

## Channel key and grading

1. Full fiscal account: all government levels and lines, long-run service responses, capital return, enterprises, accrued pensions.
2. Social costs and benefits to other residents: crime victims, congestion, PM2.5, crashes, housing, scale.
3. Distribution among other residents, by quintile.
4. Generational split plus a life-course ledger against a native reference group.
5. World ledger: sending country, place premium, welfare weights.
6. Debt legacy of past gaps plus a back-cast.
7. Comparator groups on the same rules.
8. Robustness: break conditions and a drift audit.

Grades: **S** means the study covers the channel's core for its group at roughly our depth. **P** means partial or reduced form. **P+** on channel 1 means every level and every line is present, but the capital return, enterprises and accrual are missing. **–** means absent. "Touched" counts S, P+ and P. [INFERENCE]

## Does any study cover 4 or more channels for one group?

In partial form, yes:
- **Denmark FM, MENAPT immigrants and descendants:** channels 1, 4, 6, 7, 8.
- **Suomen Perusta, Somalia- or Iraq-born with children:** channels 1, 4, 7, 8, plus a realized 1995–2011 series (6).
- **van de Beek, any one origin region such as Morocco:** channels 1, 4, 7, 8. The back-cast (6) is reported only in aggregate.
- **SSB, the R3 group:** channels 1, 4, 7, 8, with a forward fiscal gap.
- **MI 2025, Mexico row:** channels 1, 4, 7 at most. It is federal only and covers 30 years.
- **NAS 2017 and Cato 2026:** 5–6 channels, but for all immigrants, not one origin group.

At substantial depth, no: nobody reaches 4. The maximum is 2 (van de Beek on channels 4 and 7).

Channels no fiscal account covers for a group, within this search:
- **Channel 2, social costs to other residents.** The only priced pieces sit outside group accounts: CBO's crowding and productivity terms, and the GE models' market size. Finland and Denmark charge justice budgets to groups, which is fiscal, not victim cost.
- **Channel 3, distribution among other residents by income.** Only skill-group wage and welfare splits exist, in the GE models, NAS's wage chapters and Colas–Sachs.
- **Channel 5, world ledger.** The GE welfare models count OECD natives only. Place-premium work (Clemens and co-authors) is never joined to a host fiscal account.

This is a verified negative only within the searched set. [INFERENCE]

## Strongest competitors and where each beats us

1. **van de Beek et al. (NL, 2021/2023; IZA DP 17569, 2024).** Closest in spirit: one generational-accounting frame over all lines, generations 1 and 2 for up to 87 origin regions, natives on the same rules, and a 1995–2019 back-cast plus a 2020–2040 projection.
   - Beats us on **data**: CBS register microdata for every resident, against our survey-keyed allocation.
   - Beats us on **frame**: lifetime NPV with remigration, where our main case is one annual year.
   - Lacks channels 2, 3 and 5, and has no capital return or enterprises. Its authority is middling: an FvD/Renaissance-Instituut-funded first edition, and an IZA working paper.
2. **Danish Finance Ministry (annual since 2015).**
   - Beats us on **authority**: official, recurring, and the ministry answers parliament from it.
   - Beats us on **data**: full-population registers, with justice costs keyed by charges.
   - It is annual and 2019 is the latest year. It has a 2018 lifetime projection. It shows a within-group spread (13% of MENAPT immigrants net positive above 150k DKK), our within-group distribution in another form, but no channel 3.
3. **Cato, Bier, Howard and Salazar (Feb 2026).** The direct US analog of our channel 6, with the opposite sign: a 30-year back-cast with interest saved and a debt/GDP counterfactual. It uses the NASEM model the NAS authors shared.
   - Beats us on **back-cast length and model lineage**: 1994–2023 against our 2005–2024.
   - Covers all immigrants, not an ethnic-origin group of all generations, and has no social, distribution or world channels. The repo already catalogues its contested moves.
4. **NAS 2017.** Beats us on **authority** (National Academies consensus panel) and on the **dynamic frame** (75-year projections, 8 scenarios, generations 1, 2 and 3+). No origin groups, no social costs, no debt legacy; the wage chapters sit outside the account.
5. **Dynamic official models: CBO 60165, SSB DEMEC, Raffelhüschen generational accounting.**
   - Beat us on the **dynamic frame**: macro feedback, interest-rate effects and horizons to 2100.
   - CBO 61256's "potential effects" is the only official estimate found that prices a non-budget crowding cost next to the budget; it sits close to our channel 2 on congestion.
   - SSB's dilution of per-capita returns on government wealth is the closest published analog to our return on public capital.
6. **Manhattan Institute 2025 update.** The only **Mexico-specific** number found: the average Mexican immigrant "increase[s] the national debt by $10,000 each over 30 years", including descendants. It is federal only, excludes defence and pure public goods, and has no social or distribution channels [SOURCE: MI 2025 via WebFetch]. For contrast, it has India at −$1.6m (reduces the debt), China −$0.8m and El Salvador +$50k.
7. **Register back-casts: Konjunkturinstitutet 1983–2022 (a government commission) and CEPII 1979–2011.** Longer series than ours, on better data (KI) or peer review (CEPII). No debt legacy seen, and no ethnic-origin generations.

## What survives, and suggested wording

Claims that fail:
- "Nobody has done even half of this." Four or more channels are touched, in partial form, by at least eight studies.
- "Nobody has done a generational account against natives." NAS, Denmark, the Netherlands and Finland all have one.
- "Nobody has done a debt legacy." Cato 2026 has one, for all immigrants.

Claims that hold within the searched set:
- No account puts a full federal-state-local ledger (with long-run responses, capital return, enterprises and accrued pensions), the non-fiscal costs and benefits to other residents, their distribution by income, the sending-country ledger and the debt legacy for one origin group of all generations into one reconciled account.
- Nobody has done this for the Mexican-origin population. The only Mexico row is MI 2025's federal-only 30-year figure.
- No US account uses race or ethnic comparators (whites, Black residents) on the same rules; the European register studies do this for origin groups against natives.

Suggested plain wording for the operator to judge [INFERENCE]: "The closest published accounts, national register studies in the Netherlands and Denmark, cover about half of these pieces for their origin groups, on better data than any US survey allows. None adds what other residents bear outside the budget, who among them bears it, what the migrants and their home country gain, and the debt the gaps leave behind, and none does it for one American ethnic group across its generations." This makes the concession to data quality explicit, which matters because a reader who knows the Dutch or Danish work will raise it.
