## Verified substantive defects

### 1. Homicide costs condition on imprisonment while the headline says clearance

**Materiality: high for the $1.5–1.8m claim; limited for the observed victim–offender matrix.**

- `research/immigration-homicide-victim-offender-and-treasury-cost-2026-09-18.md:338-350` assigns every offender a prison spell using released-prisoner mean time served and a life-sentence mixture. Lines 354-364 call the resulting total the expected treasury cost of one *cleared* homicide. Lines 514-516 concede no conviction-given-clearance probability was sourced, but mistakenly describe that missing probability as necessary only when scaling to all homicides.
- A clearance is not a conviction with imprisonment. The missing conviction/sentencing probability is required even conditional on clearance. The displayed calculation assumes it is one. In the Hispanic row, the offender component is $1,503,436 of $1,547,846, so this is the dominant component, not a peripheral correction. [INFERENCE from the documented construction]
- Correction: label these “cost scenarios conditional on conviction and the assumed prison sentence distribution.” To claim an expected cost per cleared case, weight the prison/offender channel by conviction and disposition probabilities; do not multiply the victim channel by the same probability. Preserve the existing period-profile, discounting and life-share caveats.
- An additional readily checkable prose error: lines 25-27 say the $13.7m VSL is “roughly a hundred times the treasury line”; against $1.5–1.8m it is roughly 8–9 times. This is easy to fix alongside the substantive label, not a separate research project.

### 2. NCVS cost tables impute pair-specific severity, then mislabel treasury expenditure as victim incidence

**Materiality: high if $91 is used to price an externality onto non-Hispanic incumbents; moderate for the descriptive $91 scenario.**

- `infra/immigration-fiscal/ncvs_victim_offender_2026_09_18/analysis.py:409-420` computes one mean offence cost for each **victim** ethnicity and applies that mean to every offender ethnicity within the victim row. Thus it assumes offence severity/cost is independent of offender group conditional on victim group. The 2022–24 victim × offender × offence-type joint distribution is not measured by this operation.
- `research/immigration-ncvs-victim-offender-off-the-murder-margin-2026-09-18.md:32-45` presents the resulting dollars as the violence each offender population “costs victims outside the group,” including the criminal-justice component. Criminal-justice expenditure falls on the financing tax base; victim ethnicity alone cannot allocate that burden. Same-group victimization also generates costs for taxpayers outside that group. [INFERENCE: accounting incidence]
- Correction: call this an **imputed cost of incidents involving out-group victims**, with the conditional-severity assumption explicitly adjacent. Keep victim losses and criminal-justice spending separate. A fiscal externality onto incumbent taxpayers needs their tax-incidence share, not the victim diagonal.
- The printed “bounds” vary only the simple-assault unit price. They are sensitivity endpoints, not identification bounds on the final transfer: they do not bound unknown offender ethnicity, pair-specific severity, population scope or sampling uncertainty. Lines 3 and 32 should say “simple-assault price scenarios.”
- **Independent check passed:** using only the published derived matrix, victim cost table and population CSV, I reconstructed Hispanic pooled person-years age 12+ = 156,526,080; imputed inter-group criminal-justice cost = $14,230,090,667.495; annual ratio = **$90.9119468621**. The September 19 threefold-unit repair is correct. Hispanic intra-group share independently recomputes to **0.4044431995**. This checks arithmetic, not the severity/financing assumptions.

### 3. Crime stock-to-flow conversion is not an identified lower bound, and the corroboration claim is numerically false

**Materiality: high for the precision/confidence assigned to the +$1,421 estimate; does not overturn the directly measured institutionalization excess.**

- `infra/immigration-fiscal/crime_cost_2026_09_16/RESULT.md:58-73` divides murder victim cost by 15 years served. That is a released-prisoner mean, while the numerator is the current stock including lifers. The later homicide memo explicitly recognizes the release sample excludes lifers (`...homicide...md:338-345`). Applying a released-prisoner duration to the whole stock overallocates annual murder victim cost when the omitted lifers have longer occupancy. The steady-state caveat at crime-cost RESULT lines 238-239 does not make this a lower bound: missing offences push down while duration/composition assumptions can push up. This matters because murder supplies about 65% of the excess (RESULT lines 88-94).
- Result lines 235-241 already honestly acknowledge the Hispanic-to-Mexican offence-mix bridge, pooled generations and constructed white arrest counts. Those are scope limits, not newly discovered bugs. But the stated “lower bound” and “central number is about right” conclusion (210, 239) exceed those inputs.
- `research/immigration-confidence-ladder.md:67` says both routes agree within 8–10% **in every arrest year**, while that very row gives stock +$1,421 and flows +$1,750 / +$1,563 / +$1,201. Independent arithmetic gives deviations **+23.2%, +10.0%, −15.5%** from the stock estimate. Even relative to each flow the outer deviations exceed 10%.
- Correction: remove the universal 8–10% corroboration sentence; say the scenarios are of the same broad order, with different populations and assumptions. Withdraw the mathematical lower-bound claim. Prefer admissions/actual served-duration information if estimating a flow, or leave the stock route as allocation of historical crime harm across prisoner-years. Do not call its output observed annual victim losses or the isolated second-generation crime cost.

### 4. Texas age “standardisation” changes only the denominator, and the status explanation misstates the primary

**Materiality: medium. The pooled all-age foreign-born result survives.**

- `infra/immigration-fiscal/crime_cost_firstgen_2026_09_18/firstgen_cost_weighted.py:56-85` changes all-age charge rates using population age shares. It does not restrict charges to ages 18+ or 18–39. The “18–39 standardisation” at `research/immigration-first-generation-crime-cost-weighted-2026-09-18.md:39` is consequently not age standardization. It is all-age charges per resident in that age band. The same denominator-only limitation applies to the headline per-adult 0.59 ratio, although child offending may be a relatively small component there. The 18–39 version can be particularly misleading for differently aged immigrant populations.
- Correction: retain **0.79 as the pooled all-age cost-weighted charge ratio**. Label age-denominator scenarios literally, or obtain matching age-specific charge numerators before using “per-adult arrest rate”/“age standardised.”
- Lines 32 of that memo incorrectly explain the legal numerator as including “anyone with a DHS record at booking.” Light, He and Robey's primary explicitly classify anyone designated illegal by DHS as undocumented; their legal category combines persons designated legal, noncitizens not designated illegal, and foreign-born citizens. A DHS record alone is not the rule. Source: [PNAS Materials and Methods](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2014704117), paragraphs beginning “We combine data…” and “Legal immigrants comprise…”.
- This primary also explicitly reports legal-immigrant homicide parity and higher sexual-assault arrest rates. The qualitative pattern is not an invented discovery. The repo's 0.79 weighted-charge ratio is a descriptive cost index, not measured total social cost; the existing arrest/charge caveat is good.
