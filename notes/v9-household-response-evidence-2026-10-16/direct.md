**Verdict:** Direct US family studies document both added work/job seeking and childcare-constrained reductions after detention/departure, but this search did not verify an identified average adult stayer earnings/hours effect. The new Danish registry study is materially stronger quantitative evidence, with publicly downloaded code and saved figures, but treats a deportation **order**, not verified physical exit. Its temporary negative employment response is heterogeneous and conditional on identification and migration assumptions; it cannot supply the requested US actual-departure coefficient.

## Verified US evidence

### Boyce and Launius, 2020: directly affected households, job seeking rather than realized labor effect

[SOURCE: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2331502420973976, Research Design and Employment and Education; full text in direct/sage-0.md. Underlying 2019 institutional report https://bmi.arizona.edu/sites/bmi.arizona.edu/files/BMI-Immigration-Dragnet-2019.pdf, pp. 9–10, 21, Appendix 1 pp. 32–33.]

125 Pima County households interviewed December 2016–June 2018, recruited through service sites and snowballing; 519 members, 299 adults and 220 minors. Household required 12 months' local residence at time of arrest. A retrospective 110-question instrument records 231 arrests; financial calculations use 140 completed cases in 2006–2018. Employment coping uses all 125 households: 64 report shifts in earning responsibilities, 26 seeking additional hours, 10 seeking another job, and 28 previously non-employed seeking work (19 spouses and 9 adult/teen children). Neither observed job entry nor hours/earnings changes are estimated; no SE/CI. Released detainees and ongoing cases are included. Formal removal is not isolated. Do not convert these counts into a causal employment elasticity. The report notes service-connection, low-income and recall selection; no untreated comparison. No raw data/code link appears in retrieved article/report. [GAP] The 110-question instrument and person-level earnings/timing fields would require author access.

### KFF Artiga and Lyons, 2018: opposite signs in actual separated families

[SOURCE: https://www.kff.org/racial-equity-and-health-policy/family-consequences-of-detention-deportation-effects-on-finances-health-and-well-being/; PDF copy https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eoDownloadDocument?documentID=5094&eodoc=true, printed pp. 6–7 and 10.]

Structured summer-2018 interviews with 20 individuals in affected households and 12 providers, in Sunnyvale, Fresno, Los Angeles, Washington DC and Houston. Most respondents were Hispanic women with children, commonly partners, but some parents, siblings and teenage/adult children. Some adults worked more or added jobs; others stopped or reduced work because the absent partner had provided childcare. No frequencies, average earnings/hours effect, SE/CI or control group. Recruitment denominator and a raw-data/code release were not located. A featured mother's husband left for a residency appointment and was denied re-entry; her case is actual absence but not a conventional deportation. This illustrates why case-level departure classification matters. Survivor selection and return migration remain unresolved.

### Chaudry et al., 2010: household earnings are not stayer earnings

[SOURCE: https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/publication/28331/412020-Facing-Our-Future.PDF, Tables 1.3 and 3.1, Study Limitations, Lost Employment and Income; saved full extracted text direct/chaudry-2010.md.]

85 families/190 children across six US sites; interviews generally 2–5 or 9–13 months after arrest, some repeated. Table 1.3 records 80 interviews in 2008 and 23 in 2009. Table 3.1 gives weekly household income and worker counts, including the arrested member, released members forbidden to work, and remaining workers. It therefore cannot supply an adult stayer effect. Only 20 families experienced parental deportation by last contact. The sample omitted whole families who left, most currently detained persons unless a relative was interviewed, and difficult-to-reach families; follow-up selection worsens these problems. The authors explicitly reject statistical generalization from their nonrandom recruitment. No SE/CI or untreated comparator; no public microdata/code link located. Relevant mechanism: one Miami mother reported constantly working after her husband's deportation, while childcare and transportation needs constrained others. No numerical adult stayer effect is inferred from Table 3.1.

### Martinez-Donate et al., 2024: actual removal cohort, adult work not the quantitative outcome

[SOURCE: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmh.2024.100233; full PDF https://saludfronterizamx.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/1_0-1.pdf, pp. 2–5, 8–9, Tables 1–3; scratch direct/martinez-donate-2024.pdf.]

Between the Lines recruited 61 US families after a first Mexican-parent deportation and 51 control families in 2019–2020; youth were US citizens aged 13–17. Exposed parents were recruited at Tijuana, Nogales and Matamoros deportation stations; controls via US promotoras. Baseline included retrospective one-year-before measures and a six-month follow-up. Only 56% of exposed families versus 88% of controls completed follow-up; FE analyses use 79 retained families (approximately 34 exposed, 45 controls). Outcomes are child health/behavior, housing/food hardship and schooling, not adult labor. Fourteen qualitative caregiver interviews include work/childcare remarks. Eligibility required family members to be on good terms; authors acknowledge that some missing families may have moved to Mexico. Questionnaires/interview guides available from first author on request; no public raw data/code package located. This is not evidence that an adult earnings effect is zero.

### Patler and Gonzalez, 2020 online / 2021 print: no labor-market avoidance found

[SOURCE: https://doi.org/10.1093/socpro/spaa069; primary full text https://academic.oup.com/socpro/advance-article/doi/10.1093/socpro/spaa069/6019952?guestAccessKey=3e90b455-e59c-46a6-9ee6-0ebe601b12da; Methods, Findings and labor-market discussion; saved direct/patler-gonzalez-2020.md.]

*Compounded Vulnerability: The Consequences of Immigration Detention for Institutional Attachment and System Avoidance in Mixed-Immigration-Status Families* analyzes 104 interviews in 42 California families: 42 detained parents, 36 non-detained partners and 26 children. Recruitment through four detention facilities required detention for at least six months; partner eligibility required an established pre-detention relationship and consistent communication. The sample therefore conditions on connected families with prolonged detention. Partner citizenship/status: 49% US citizen, 17% documented noncitizen, 34% undocumented. Most children were 11–18, with some up to 30. The authors explicitly report no evidence of labor-market avoidance; many spouses and older children worked more to make ends meet. This is useful disconfirmation of a universal withdrawal/fear mechanism, not a statistical null or population-average employment increase. No numerical adult earnings/hours effect, SE/CI, untreated comparator or public microdata/code release was located. [GAP] Calendar interview window was not independently pinned in this audit.

### Ojeda et al., 2020: deportees' reports of relatives needing new work

[SOURCE: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.00148; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.00148/full, Methods and Table 2.]

*Deported Men's and Father's Perspective: The Impacts of Family Separation on Children and Families in the U.S.* uses a January–May 2013 Tijuana free-clinic sample: 601 male patients, 303 ever deported Mexican men; 227 reported US-resident family (158 nuclear, 69 extended). Table 2 reports relatives needing new jobs in 15% overall, 18% with nuclear and 10% with extended family (between-type chi-square p=.146). This is deportee reporting, no family validation, heterogeneous time since removal and no adult-only outcome. It does not establish realized job entry or identify an employment effect. No SE/CI for these percentages was located; no public raw-data/code package was retrieved. This is secondary to the directly interviewed remaining-family studies above.

## Danish quantitative evidence: order shock, public code, restricted microdata

**Sources and access.** Michael T. Light, Lars H. Andersen and Noa Hendel, *Deportation’s Fallout: Evidence from Denmark*, AJS just accepted, DOI https://doi.org/10.1086/742317. Replication DOI https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/BVCW55, public version 1.0 dated 2026-05-22; downloaded through the official Harvard Dataverse browser interface after HTTP clients/API returned 403. Original ZIP is `direct/denmark-replication.zip`; extracted package `direct/denmark-replication/`; saved images are `direct/run_analysis-cell-*.png`. Repository README identifies a second public code route https://github.com/manselans/Deportations-Fallout.git (not independently fetched). Dataverse metadata is CC0; package code is MIT. README lines 57 and 116–125 explicitly say individual administrative microdata require Statistics Denmark approval and cannot be redistributed; access route https://www.dst.dk/en/TilSalg/data-til-forskning/mikrodataordninger. Therefore **public code and saved graph outputs, no public raw individual data**. No private contact attempted.

**Treatment and sample.** Public code `etl/define_population.py` lines 18–34 and 127–158 loads first court deportation-order dates and sets D=1; comparison defendants have convictions/prison or suspended sentences and no deportation order, not a randomly selected untreated population (lines 94–124). Convicted persons with Danish citizenship are excluded (226–227). Partners are linked from the year before conviction; only registered spouse/partner links and each partner's first exposure are retained (`etl/find_spouses.py` 19–46, 76–91). This verifies an **order-date** estimand in primary code. The institutional research summary explicitly includes orders never executed: https://rockwoolfonden.dk/udgivelser/partnere-til-udvisningsdoemte-kriminelle-arbejder-mindre/. It reports 421 exposed partners in the labor-market sample and approximately 3,000 comparison partners, nine in ten women, 45% Danish citizens, 16% EU and 39% other. These counts are summary-derived, not recovered Table 1 cells; notebooks have commands to export tables but do not embed the table values. [GAP] Exact model-specific N and age distribution remain unavailable.

**Outcome/time/identification, verified in code.** Monthly outcome coverage is 2008–2021. Employment is positive monthly recorded wages, not a survey job-search measure (`etl/construct_panel.py` 156–163). Wages and full-time shares sum across a partner's jobs; wages are measured in thousands of DKK; transfers are separate. Each is capped at its 99th percentile (`etl/spouse_outcomes.py` 23–101). These are **individual partner**, not total household, outcomes. The panel spans months −18 through +18 around conviction/order. Main Figure 2 is individual fixed effects plus event-time dummies and treatment-by-event-time interactions, with **month −7 as reference**, errors clustered by individual, and **90% confidence intervals** (`dynamic_did.py` 18–20, 211–256; `figures/figure_2.py` 22–36). Main call supplies no extra covariates. Court order versus no order is observational; a causal reading needs counterfactual parallel changes and no differential concurrent criminal-justice/family shock. The surviving saved appendix includes a residency-seniority RD plot, but its assumptions/estimates were not fully audited. Do not label the main event study randomized or an IV estimate.

**Scale from primary saved figures, explicitly approximate.** The figures are raster outputs embedded by the authors in `notebooks/run_analysis.ipynb`. No machine-readable coefficient/SE table is present. The following are visual readings, deliberately rounded; they are not independently rerun estimates:

| Outcome and anchor | Approximate result | Interpretation/precision |
|---|---|---|
| Employment, Figure 2 / notebook cell 8, month +6 | About −4 percentage points; 90% CI roughly −7 to 0 pp | Relative to month −7 and conviction-only comparison; original treated employment level about 37–38% before order. |
| Employment, same figure, month +12 | About −1 pp; 90% CI roughly −5 to +3 pp | Near-zero estimate with uncertainty allowing either sign. |

These common six-month horizons avoid choosing the most negative month. Figure 3 / cell 12 shows drops in full-time share and earnings, with less complete recovery than employment. Its row order and units were traced to `figures/figure_3.py` lines 24–31 and 50–61: fulltime, wages (thousands DKK), wages plus transfers. The left axis labels are cycled by `names[i - 1]`; row titles and the actual variable passed to estimation establish the interpretation. No exact earnings coefficient or hours conversion is asserted here.

Early pre-order changes and the −7 baseline matter: the saved earnings/full-time graphs already show deterioration before order. A number at t=0 is not the clean response to a member's physical departure that month. The original institutional summary associates pre-order change with criminal charges, but that mechanism is not separately identified by the main graph. [INFERENCE]

**Migration and missingness.** Registry migration events are used to mark observed in-country periods, with return migration allowed; absent wage records while in Denmark are zero-filled, while out-of-country absence is not automatically zero employment (`etl/construct_panel.py` 42–122, 154–163). Thus the main result is based on recorded monthly outcomes, not a fully observed fixed cohort of permanent stayers. The primary abstract reports 13% of partners leaving within 18 months of order. Figure 5's saved informative-attrition test is **coefficient .083, p=.004** (`run_analysis.txt` 118–119); its code regresses current participation with next-period observation added. This rejects a no-association version of noninformative observation. The saved worst-case bounds straddle zero after order (cell 18), so unrestricted missing-outcome assumptions do not fix the sign. IPCW and kNN imputations preserve the short-term negative pattern (Figure 7 / cell 22), conditional on their models/covariates; that is more limited than proving migration irrelevant. Code reads reveal migration/observation handling, not an assurance that all location records or migration dates are error-free. [INFERENCE]

**Heterogeneity and disconfirmation.** Figure 9 / cell 26 separates asylum, Danish, EU/EEA and family-reunified partners. EU/EEA shows the clearest persistent negative pattern; Danish partners show small positive post-order estimates with mostly wide intervals crossing zero. Asylum pretrends are pronounced; family-reunified partners show an early drop followed by recovery. The institutional summary reports 38% of EU partners left within 18 months, compared with 13% for non-EU; these are summary numbers, not reread table cells. This difference makes selection and transport especially important. No age-stratified labor coefficients were located in the package; age is observed and used in some adjustment models. Avoid asserting homogeneous harm or a universal added-worker response.

**Remaining gaps.** Full manuscript, Table 1/2 numeric output, exact dynamic coefficients/SEs, treatment-execution records, exact model Ns, age heterogeneity and complete appendix identification arguments remain unverified. Public data-free replication cannot reproduce microdata results. Package imports also reference modules absent at those exact paths in the ZIP (e.g. `deportations_fallout.config` and `deportations_fallout.results.*`); runnable reproducibility is not established. Saved figures and source definitions were inspected, not successfully regenerated.

## Transport to v9 and what can be claimed

[INFERENCE] US citizen status is insufficient to identify a person who survives the v9 lineage removal: Mexican-origin US-born spouses/children, including relevant descendants, may themselves be removed from the modeled population. Teenage/adult children in the US qualitative samples should not automatically be counted as remaining nonmembers. Even a spouse outside that lineage faces a present-day detention/order shock containing separation, childcare disruption, fear, legal costs and uncertain reunion; this is different from the v9 counterfactual. The studies still identify plausible directions of mechanism and reasons a static zero-behavior assumption may fail. They do not provide a transportable sign, scale or taxpayer-dollar correction.

The evidence supports the modest statement: **remaining adults sometimes seek or do more paid work, and sometimes reduce it when childcare or other constraints bind; measured order-related responses in Denmark are temporary, heterogeneous and selection-sensitive.** It does not justify applying a US adult earnings multiplier after physical household removal. This is an outcome-specific research result, not an exhaustive claim that no such study exists.
