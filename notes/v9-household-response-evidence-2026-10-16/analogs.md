**Verdict:** Remaining adults sometimes work more after losing a household earner, but the response depends materially on income replacement, the departed person's prior economic role, and the surviving adult's scope to adjust. The strongest US evidence here concerns widows near age 60; a randomized migration opportunity produces imprecise employment effects among relatives left behind. These studies support the existence of a behavioral margin, not a common departure elasticity or a signed dollar correction to v9. [INFERENCE from the sources below]

Scope: adjacent causal studies, searched 2026-10-16. No microdata re-estimation or model update. Direct deportation is assigned elsewhere. All estimates below concern the **remaining adult**, except where an exclusion is explicit; earnings are not government receipts. The target framing is a stayer's labor response, not the welfare value of bereavement or migration. [FRAMING-SENSITIVE]

## 1. US spouse death and survivor-benefit eligibility: strongest directly relevant US administrative evidence

Coyne, Fadlon, Ramnath and Tong (2024), *Household Labor Supply and the Value of Social Security Survivors Benefits*, AER 114(5), 1248–80; DOI 10.1257/aer.20190813. [Publisher](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20190813); [author full text](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~yfadlon/pdfs/HouseholdLaborSupplyandValueofSocialSecuritySurvivorsBenefits.pdf); [final appendix](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles/materials/20624).

IRS/SSA records, 1999–2014. Death-timing design compares 63,707 widows whose husbands died in 2002–03 with 74,214 whose husbands died four years later; ages 50–70. Joint filing identifies prior spouses. The separate eligibility design uses 293,857 widows and the age-60 cutoff. Employment means positive W-2 earnings; neither hours nor informal earnings are observed. These are **all spousal deaths, not a sudden-death-only sample**. [SOURCE: §§4.1–4.2]

Table 1 in the author manuscript, ages 58–61, estimates death effects allowing variation by benefit eligibility:

| Remaining widow | Annual wage earnings, dollars (SE) | Participation, percentage points (SE) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Ineligible at observed age | +1,290 (545) | +2.048 (0.874) |
| Eligible | −605 (461) | −1.690 (0.866) |
| Ineligible minus eligible | +1,895 (661) | +3.737 (1.112) |

The table has 67,140 household clusters; its before/after death specification uses post-event years 1–3 within the narrow observed-age sample. In the separate age-cutoff design, full-year eligibility reduces earnings by $1,751 (SE301) and participation by 2.866pp (SE0.349), years 1–2 after death; final Appendix Table C.1 confirms these values. The causal effect of benefit eligibility is distinct from the death effect and from the interaction between death and observed eligibility. [SOURCE: author Table 1, §§4.2.2/5.2; final Appendix C.1]

**Transfer limit:** permanent bereavement, near-retirement ages, an insured deceased worker and prior joint filing differ from departing working-age caregivers or mixed-status households. Death also removes consumption needs and may release care time. The death design needs parallel counterfactual trends; the benefit design needs smooth age-related confounding. I have not verified the dollar price-base convention, so do not inflation-convert these estimates into fiscal inputs. [INFERENCE/GAP]

## 2. Denmark: earner role changes the sign

Fadlon and Nielsen (2021), *Family Labor Supply Responses to Severe Health Shocks: Evidence from Danish Administrative Records*, AEJ Applied 13(3), 1–30; DOI 10.1257/app.20170604. [Publisher and final appendix link](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.20170604); [author full text](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~yfadlon/pdfs/FamilyLaborSupplyResponsestoShocks.pdf).

Linked national registers, 1980–2011; fatal-event sample covers deaths at ages 45–80 in 1985–2011. Same-cohort households experiencing the event five years later provide controls. Mean survivor age before death is about 63. Table 1, year/age/household effects, averages years 2–4:

| Remaining spouse | Annual earnings in 2000 DKK (SE) | Participation, pp (SE) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Widows | +2,707 (181) | +1.64 (0.10) |
| Widowers | −906 (430) | −0.17 (0.16) |

Clusters: 335,670 widows, 166,703 widowers. This is a **sign contrast**, not a generic gender parameter: the paper's income-share analyses find similar sensitivity across genders facing comparable income losses. Nonfatal heart-attack/stroke households show no meaningful average spouse response where income losses are substantially insured. [SOURCE: Table 1, §§III.A–B; Appendix A.1]

**Transfer limit:** fatal events are not restricted to unexpected deaths. Health deterioration, grief, released caregiving and Danish insurance rules remain relevant. Their mechanism comparisons favor income-loss self-insurance, but do not experimentally isolate every care or preference channel. [INFERENCE; source §III.A.2]

## 3. Tonga migration lottery: actual departure with no precise adult employment response

Gibson, McKenzie and Stillman (2011), *The Impacts of International Migration on Remaining Household Members: Omnibus Results from a Migration Lottery Program*, REST 93(4), 1297–1318; DOI 10.1162/REST_a_00129. [Institutional record and final PDF](https://bia.unibz.it/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-impacts-of-international-migration-on/991005772265201241). Final PDF downloaded and Table 8 read; the earlier IZA draft has different adjusted coefficients and must not be substituted.

New Zealand's Pacific Access Category lottery instruments actual household-member migration. Legal eligibility rules define comparable stayers among lottery winners and losers; otherwise whole-household departure creates selection. Most stayers are parents/siblings rather than spouses: spouse and dependent children ordinarily accompany the migrant. Survey timing averages ten months after departure (median eight), so permanent migration is observed only in its initial adjustment period. [SOURCE: §§IV.A–B]

Final Table 8, panel B, adults 18–45, household-clustered SEs: male employment **+9.0pp (SE16.7; n85)**; female employment **−5.3pp (SE9.5; n91)**. Both are inconclusive. Unadjusted estimates are +8.4pp (12.3) and −10.3pp (10.0). Small samples admit large effects of either sign; absence of significance does not justify a zero response. [SOURCE: final Table 8, p1311/PDF p15]

**Transfer limit:** migrants can remit; the remaining relatives receive both absence and remittances, in a different labor market. A household-income decline includes the emigrant's foregone local earnings and therefore cannot itself be relabeled a decline in stayers' labor supply. [INFERENCE]

## 4. Sweden custodial versus home monitoring: stronger incarceration design, co-parent labor null

Grenet, Grönqvist and Niknami (2024), *The effects of electronic monitoring on offenders and their families*, Journal of Public Economics 230, 105051; DOI 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2023.105051. [Publisher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272723002335); [original final article PDF, mirrored](https://www.antoniocasella.eu/nume/Grenet_EM_2024.pdf); [author manuscript](https://www.parisschoolofeconomics.com/grenet-julien/wp/Grenet_Gronqvist_Niknami_2023.pdf). Final DOI/title checked and Table 6 extracted from final PDF.

Sweden's 1997 reform permitted electronic monitoring instead of custody for sentences up to three months. DiD compares these sentences with 4–12 months, 1992–98, using administrative records. **Table 6 is a reduced-form eligibility effect, not the effect of actual physical departure**: the authors lack individual monitoring/incarceration data. It follows the other parent of children aged 11–14 at sentencing, n10,600; co-parenthood does not guarantee current co-residence. [SOURCE: §§2–3.5; Table 6]

Full controls, three-year average after trial: co-parent employment **−0.4pp (SE2.0)**; log earnings **+0.009 (SE0.141)**; log disposable income **−0.048 (SE0.027)**. The income result reaches only the paper's 10% threshold, not 5%. Authors suggest reduced transfers as a possible explanation; this mechanism is not separately identified. A disposable-income change cannot be relabeled lost labor earnings. [SOURCE: final Table 6 col5 and §3.5]

**Transfer limit:** short custody, nonrandom sentence-length groups, parallel-trends assumption, and a supervision reform that preserves the offender's earnings as well as presence. It cannot supply a general permanent-removal effect. The publisher states that authors lack permission to share underlying data. [SOURCE: Data availability; INFERENCE on transfer]

## Implication for the conditional fiscal sensitivity

The evidence rejects treating all lost adults as interchangeable. A defensible future parameter must specify pre-departure earnings contribution, childcare/elder-care contribution, remaining dependents, stayer baseline employment, transfer eligibility, remittances and horizon. Co-residence after departure is itself an outcome; conditioning only on observed surviving households can discard complete departures or restructured households. [INFERENCE]

The empirical bridge still missing is a departure-induced change in the relevant v9 **nonmembers'** earnings/hours, with household composition followed and taxes/transfers recomputed. Present evidence supports separate scenario labels for lost earner, lost caregiver and insured/compensated loss. It does not assign their weights or their dollar effects. Do not multiply these study coefficients by the fiscal sensitivity's entire eligible denominator. [INFERENCE]
