It is not an individual arrest effect, a randomized enforcement-policy effect, or a measure of days of learning lost. [SOURCE: research questions; Attendance; Analysis] [INFERENCE]

The setting is two anonymous high-school districts in small urban California cities, school years **2014–15 through 2017–18**. District A experienced recorded arrests; District B is in the same county but **more than 100 miles away**, with no recorded arrests within 50 miles. ## Identification and measurement

Attendance is the complement of the mean student share of scheduled class periods missed during a week. The author first estimates segmented interrupted time series in A, with a baseline trend and event-specific immediate level and subsequent slope terms, then adds weekly district enrollment and student-composition covariates. Newey–West standard errors use lag 1, chosen after an autocorrelation test. These standard errors address a specified serial-correlation pattern, not omitted calendar shocks, endogenous enforcement timing or exposure misclassification. The manuscript does not supply the test statistics/p-values or a broad alternative-lag sensitivity table. [SOURCE: Attendance; Baseline; Robustness Checks] [INFERENCE]

Comparative ITS uses District B to control common developments and tests differential levels/trends. Its causal interpretation needs an untreated comparison district, comparable untreated attendance trajectories, no differential coincident shock, and stable outcome/enrollment recording. A county shared across districts more than 100 miles apart does not establish those assumptions. Media coverage can itself change family behavior; local arrest timing, publicity and enforcement intensity are bundled. These data cannot distinguish the effect of an arrest from news, fear, parental work disruption, district response or actual family removal. [SOURCE: Comparative Interrupted Time Series; Threats to Validity; Discussion] [INFERENCE]

Selection is consequential: “the regressions include only those incidents that yielded statistically significant results in Equation 1.” Thus the comparative/subgroup results follow screening on the same outcome series. There are thirteen incident weeks, multiple slope/level tests and many overlapping demographic groups, with no reported multiplicity correction or selection-adjusted inference. Printed standard errors are conditional model errors, not protection against this search. Inference across incidents is also not a randomized dose-response experiment. [SOURCE: Comparative Interrupted Time Series; Table 3] [INFERENCE]

Student eligibility requires at least six consecutive weeks, described as three observations before and after a disruption. ## Misuse and disconfirmation

The strongest supported interpretation is that several salient, relatively large local enforcement incidents coincide with immediate attendance declines, particularly in migrant/Latinx and other vulnerable groups, and two are followed by sustained slope changes under the fitted specification. The largest subgroup result is a useful warning about external effects on students. The disconfirming evidence against a universal per-raid rule is the small-incident imprecision/nulls, nonimmigrant-status measurement, outcome screening, one distant comparison district, unresolved data/model defects and lack of national representativeness. [SOURCE: Results; Tables 2–3; Limitations] [INFERENCE]

The background also describes an earlier anonymized study in the opposite direction: “higher numbers of deportations related declines in absences”. That earlier result uses young children and regional annual enforcement measures, so it is not a matched refutation of the weekly high-school result. The supplied preprint's anonymized citation does not establish whether the phrase is accurate or a wording error; the underlying study was not independently read here. [SOURCE: Empirical Background; INFERENCE]

Likely overclaims are “deportation lowers attendance everywhere by 11%,” “11% of undocumented children stop going to school,” or a national dollar cost produced by applying a daily funding price. The limiting passages are “no information available related to students' immigrant-origin status,” “not generalizable other schools,” and the explicit difference from Average Daily Attendance. Even eleven **percentage points** of period attendance for one subgroup after one incident is not eleven percent of students, not an average across raids, and not a welfare or budget estimate. [SOURCE: Discussion; Limitations; note 4; Table 3] [INFERENCE]

This politically charged assessment uses an LLM instrument with known framing dispositions; the safeguards here are exact exposure/outcome definitions, source-version separation, nulls and selection threats, matching the repository's existing evidence contracts, and retaining unresolved defects rather than silently repairing them.
