## 4. Identification, assumptions and author-named threats

This is a deterministic **cohort-component + CES production + demographic cash-flow projection**, not IV, DiD or an exogenous natural experiment. Eight scenarios cross family/labor emphasis with 0.5/1/1.5/2 times baseline within-cell net migration rates; ninth stops immigration but retains low emigration. Main Table 1 says baseline rates **2021**; prose pp.6–7 and supplement B repeatedly say **2018**. Preserve the discrepancy; use 2018 when describing the stated substantive construction, without claiming the unpublished implementation has been verified. [SOURCE: Table 1 p.6; policy text pp.6–7; supplement B]

Labor emphasis is not simply the same retained immigrants with another label. Supplement B.2 assigns **65% work, 13.6% student/exchange, 21.4% other visas**, changes age/race/sex and imposes different emigration; a later sentence says **13.5%** students. It begins from the same 2018 gross immigrant count, then subtracts different cell-specific emigration. “At similar levels” therefore does not hold retained stock or cumulative immigration fixed. It also explicitly says **“Labor scenarios do not account for undocumented migration,”** whereas main p.7 says scenarios do not distinguish authorized/unauthorized immigrants. [SOURCE: supplement B.2; policy text p.7]

GDP combines two education bins and native/foreign-born labor with assumed substitution elasticities **σm=20, σe=2**, constant returns, proportional capital adjustment and **1.4% annual productivity growth**. Headline results use human-capital externality **λ=.45**, with .0/.75 considered. Cell employment rates are frozen; newly arriving people inherit stock immigrants’ employment rates. These are imposed mechanisms, not identified effects of visa reforms. [SOURCE: GDP Projections p.8; supplement C.1–2]

Authors name: fertility declines may reverse; fixed racial fertility differences may converge; future skilled-worker supply may be constrained; wars/depressions may change flows; work visas may shift from Asia toward higher-fertility Africa; State/DHS visa records cannot be directly joined to ACS education/employment; CPS misses benefits below age 15 and mixes or misreports benefit categories; real-wage growth is omitted from Social Security; tax/benefit rates are fixed. The higher/converging pre-COVID fertility sensitivity is said to leave substantial scenario differences, but no numerical sensitivity table is provided. [SOURCE: Projection Caveats pp.9–10]

The strongest case for the paper is its explicit integration of age, nativity, selection, emigration and descendants: immigration’s effects extend beyond first-year payroll taxes. The strongest disconfirmation inside its own results is that population growth and aggregate GDP do not establish gains per resident: family scenarios have slower per-capita growth than zero immigration. Its own limitations rule out complete-fiscal or policy-causal claims. [SOURCE: Results pp.15–19; INFERENCE]
