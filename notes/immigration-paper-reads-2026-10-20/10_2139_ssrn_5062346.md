## Question, population, period, unit and estimand

“In this paper, we study how immigration affects the way in which local governments set their effective income tax rates, and the consequences this has for the distributional impact of immigration.” [SOURCE: I; ligature artifacts normalized in quotations hereafter]

“Our main sample covers the pre-COVID period 2010 to 2019 and includes working-age native (Swiss national) men and women aged between 25 and 64.” Spouses of any age/nationality are added, because married/civil-union couples are tax units. “The earnings analysis as well as the analyses related to the applicable tax rates and the cantonal tax rates are thus conducted at the household rather than individual level”. The regression observation is a **municipality-year or canton-year first difference**; gross-earnings outcomes are local percentiles of positive employee earnings net of social-security contributions. [SOURCE: IV, Appendix B]

The treatment is “the change in the stock of immigrants ΔI divided by the total population P in the base period”. It is **net change in foreign-national residents**, including refugees, excluding cross-border commuters, rather than admissions or an immigrant-origin dynasty. Table 1 instead defines descriptive immigrants as foreign-born: that definition should not be substituted for the treatment. The estimand is the local outcome response to instrument-predicted inflows, decomposed into earnings/automatic tax-bracket changes and political tax changes. [SOURCE: III Eq.5–6; IV; Table 1 note; Appendix B]

## Identification and limits

First differences with year effects; municipality results generally include canton effects. Earnings controls change in native age/education; tax controls are baseline demographics, unemployment/manufacturing, earnings and urban status. Instrument: national net immigrant inflows by origin × municipality's share of that origin in **1996**, divided by lagged local population (25 origin groups). Canton estimates cluster on canton and report wild-bootstrap and Anderson–Rubin inference; municipality estimates cluster on municipality. [SOURCE: III, Eq.5–6; Tables3–10]

The necessary exclusion is exogeneity of historical settlement shares to omitted **changes** in local outcomes. Authors explicitly cannot perform pre-trend tests: “The absence of a clear pre-treatment period prevents us from testing for pre-trends”. Footnote16 rejects a many-exogenous-shocks justification in this setting. Origin-level Rotemberg weights concentrate on Portugal (**0.595 canton; 0.601 municipality**); the design is largely West European migration. Ongoing prior inflows may still affect current outcomes; Appendix A4/A9 add lagged decade inflows. [SOURCE: V.A/C, fn16, Fig.A4, TablesA4/A9]

Geneva is excluded from preferred canton results to improve instrument strength, after observing outlier first-stage behavior. Political-interaction evidence is suggestive: weak first stages in Table8, municipal interaction insignificant with canton effects. Restricted positive-earner samples omit zero-earnings natives; earnings changes combine pay/employment and selection, not pure wage changes. Actual household deductions, capital income, wealth and native-specific social aid are not observed. These are real limits to disposable-income and political-motive conclusions. [SOURCE: IV; V.A/C/F/G; fn10/20/26/32; Appendix B; INFERENCE]

## Misuse risk

“Unfortunately, not all types of income and transfers are observable in our data ... neither capital income nor social welfare transfers are recorded.” [SOURCE: II.B] Calling this a complete disposable-income study overstates observation.
