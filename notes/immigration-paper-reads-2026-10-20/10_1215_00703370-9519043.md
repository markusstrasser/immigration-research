## 3. Question, population, period, unit and estimand

Question in the authors' words: “how does the prevalence of poverty risk factors vary across immigrant generations?”; “how does the penalty (i.e., the magnitude of poverty risk) associated with each factor vary”; and “what share of observed poverty rates, and inter-generational differences therein, can be explained (statistically) by the uneven distribution of risk factors?” [SOURCE: Introduction]

The main population is **Hispanic children of all races, ages17 and younger in the United States**, in the **2014–2018 ASEC survey waves**. Income and annual work refer to the prior calendar year, so these are broadly **income/work years2013–2017**, not income years2014–2018. Age and residence are observed at survey time. The five surveys are pooled cross-sections, with some people appearing in consecutive samples: “cases should be interpreted as person-period observations.” Main Hispanic analytic N is **50,875 unweighted person-year observations**, not50,875 independent children. The white third+ comparison sample's N and each Hispanic generation's N are not reported in the supplied main text/tables. [SOURCE: Data, pp.7–8]

Groups: first = foreign-born children, naturalized and noncitizens pooled; second-two = native-born with two foreign-born parents; second-one = native-born with one foreign-born parent; third+ = native-born with native-born parents. The paper includes people born abroad to US-citizen parents in the latter native category. Child race/ethnicity is the household respondent's classification. Hispanic here does not mean Mexican origin, and foreign parent does not mean Mexico-born. [SOURCE: Measures; notes14,16]

Outcome: binary Census **Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM)** status of each child, based on the resource-sharing family unit. It accounts for near-cash benefits, taxes/credits, geographically adjusted thresholds, cohabiting partners, foster children and certain unattached children. It excludes public education and public healthcare from resources; it is not a full social-consumption account. [SOURCE: Measures; note13]

Estimands: weighted poverty proportions; prevalence of five risk-factor categories; generation-specific adjusted **linear probability model coefficients**, in probability units; predicted poverty after replacing a group's distribution of a category with the Hispanic third+ distribution (and separately white third+) **while retaining that group's coefficient schedule and control/nonfocal means**. Exact wording: “While not a formal decomposition, this exercise follows the logic of Kitagawa-Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition.” These are statistical composition contrasts, not average treatment effects of education, marriage, migration, or a government programme. [SOURCE: Methods; Results “Decomposing”; INFERENCE]

## 5. Identification, assumptions and threats

Design: descriptive pooled cross-sections and separate LPMs by generation. Models include all five risk domains and controls for child's age, relation to head, head's sex, family size and region. The paper says it clusters repeated observations by person identifiers and uses robust SEs for heteroskedasticity. It weights using IPUMS March-supplement person weights. It does not document replicate-weight design inference or household-level cross-child clustering in the supplied text. These are precision questions to investigate with the full appendix, not demonstrated failures of the reported point estimates. [SOURCE: Data; Methods; notes12,25; INFERENCE]

The standardizations need comparable measurement and sufficient overlap across categories. For a purely descriptive exercise they do not need to identify a causal intervention. Treating them as policy effects would additionally require education, work, residence and family-structure changes to leave coefficient schedules and other determinants invariant, and removal of confounding/selection. Neither is established. Parent education, wages, English, authorization, immigrant cohort and location selection can covary. The model cannot distinguish discrimination from other reasons for residual coefficients or group gaps. [SOURCE: Conceptual framework; Methods; note9; Discussion; INFERENCE]

Authors' explicit threats: undercount of disadvantaged recent/unauthorized immigrants can bias early-generation poverty downward; socioeconomic selection out of Hispanic identity can bias later-generation poverty upward; “These two sources of bias operate in opposite directions, with the net effect unknown.” Return migration of US-born Mexican children can select the observed second generation. Aggregating third and fourth+ masks potentially different outcomes; state destination classes conceal substate variation; citizenship, legal status and language are not modeled as common risk factors. [SOURCE: Data; Measures; notes9,15,17,18,23]

The within-family work indicator is an economic resource-unit measure, not a father's labor-force participation rate. Working-age adults are24–64; low work is under0.5FTE per adult;1FTE=1,750annual hours. Hence the cutoff is875annual hours **on average per eligible adult**. A two-adult unit with one1,750-hour worker sits exactly at the “adequate” threshold. The age exclusions and resource-sharing premise are material; counting ages18–23 or treating all jobholders equally would yield a different explanatory variable. [SOURCE: Measures; notes20–21; CALCULATION:1,750×0.5]

The policy recommendations in the Discussion go beyond the identified contrasts. For example, “improving the educational attainment of foreign-born parents would reduce poverty” is reasonable as a hypothesis but not an effect estimated by this design. It is not enough to replace observed parent education labels and interpret the predicted change as the return to an adult-training programme. [SOURCE: Discussion; INFERENCE]

## 10. Misuse risk, unresolved gaps and closeout

Likely misuses are claiming “immigration causes poverty,” treating the observed generation ladder as a family's future trajectory, citing low employment's49.1-point coefficient as the main explanation for generational inequality, interpreting standardization as the return to adult education, interpreting SPM poverty reduction as budget savings, or citing the manuscript's family-structure prose despite the conflicting table/figure.
