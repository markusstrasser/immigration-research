## Identification and source weaknesses

The strongest design argument is to compare two mixed-origin parental pairings rather than endogamous Hispanic vs White families, reducing broad ancestry/composition differences. Its defining assumption is nevertheless demanding: HW and WH children are “alike on every trait that matters in the labor market, both observable and unobservable.” The paper says “If this holds, surname is the only systematic difference between them.” That is an identifying assumption, not a result established by similar averages or assortative mating citations. Parent gender, origin, migration selection, English exposure, location, resources, union stability and actual naming/marriage practices may differ even for mixed-origin couples. [SOURCE: Introduction; Empirical Approach] [INFERENCE]

Actual surname is not observed. Paternal Hispanic birthplace proxies a likely Hispanic surname under patrilineal naming; maternal/paternal ethnicity also changes household composition and cultural exposure. A U.S.-born parent is called White/non-Hispanic by birthplace, even though a U.S.-born parent can have Hispanic ancestry or a Hispanic surname. The source's overall 3% native-born Hispanic share does not identify that conditional probability among people who partner with Hispanic immigrants. Married women's own surnames may also reflect spouse names. Misclassification is not necessarily classical attenuation and cannot be quantified here. [SOURCE: Sample Construction; Introduction; Tables 1/5; notes 1/3] [INFERENCE]

Synthetic parents are **group averages, not linked actual parents**. The text says parent ages at childbirth **25–40**, but its example uses **20–35**, changing who enters the constructed cell. It explicitly states “used exclusively for descriptive summary statistics and are not included as control variables in the main regression analyses.” Nearby text says the proxies allow control of background, then repeats they are not controls; the explicit exclusion governs how the main estimates should be interpreted. The sensitivity paragraph later claims controls for parental background, but the recovered wage tables show education controls without a parental-background control row; that claim is not demonstrated by their displayed grids. [SOURCE: Sample Construction; Sensitivity Analysis] [INFERENCE]

The descriptive balance evidence does not show equality. Table 4 HW−WH total household schooling is **−1.18 years (SE .04)**, paternal schooling **−1.49 (.02)**, maternal schooling **+.29 (.02)**, log family income **−.05 (.01)**, paternal log hourly earnings **−.04 (.01)**, maternal **+.02 (.02)** and fertility **+.23 (.01)**. The article stresses more educated HW mothers and smaller gaps than WW−HH, but lower total resources remain a concrete alternative explanation. Being more similar than two more different groups does not establish exchangeability of this pair. Group-cell SEs also do not measure uncertainty from falsely matching a particular adult to an average parent. [SOURCE: From the Data; Table 4] [INFERENCE]

State-clustered SEs account for some geographical error dependence; they do not remove selection into interethnic unions, repeated CPS observations, sample-definition differences or all survey-design uncertainty. The supplied main text does not specify a weights/rotation/de-duplication sensitivity, exact number of clusters or an identification test beyond descriptive comparisons. State, year and state-year fixed-effect labels appear together in some models; their overlap does not create independent adjustment dimensions. [SOURCE: Empirical Approach; Tables 7–8] [INFERENCE]

The author admits “residual selection bias may remain, particularly regarding cultural and attitudinal factors”. Note 3 explicitly says the study does not account for origin-specific “gender-specific migration patterns”. These are directly relevant rival explanations for changing whether the father or mother is the immigrant parent. The observed family-type coefficient can describe a gap while its causal attribution remains unproven. [SOURCE: From the Data; note 3] [INFERENCE]

## Misuse and disconfirmation

The strongest supported finding is a conditional association between immigrant-parent sex/configuration and adult outcomes within White mixed-origin families. It is consistent with surname signaling, but also with unequal family resources, origin/sex selection and other household pathways. Disconfirming evidence for a clean surname-only interpretation includes the author's explicit “residual selection bias” admission, lower HW household resources, missing actual names, null education sex-difference tests and the education-controlled earnings uncertainty. Conversely, attenuation with schooling/occupation cannot disprove discrimination acting through schooling or occupational access. [SOURCE: Introduction; From the Data; Tables 4/6/8; Sensitivity Analysis] [INFERENCE]

Likely overclaims are “a Hispanic surname causally costs 5%,” “women face a statistically larger penalty,” “education proves discrimination is absent,” or “this explains 5% of the national fiscal deficit.” The limiting passages are the assumption that the two parental configurations are “alike on every trait”, the acknowledgment that “residual selection bias may remain”, and note 3's warning that results “may not generalize to Hispanic children with USborn parents”. The actual regression regressor is family configuration; its causal interpretation is conditional on assumptions not demonstrated by the available data.
