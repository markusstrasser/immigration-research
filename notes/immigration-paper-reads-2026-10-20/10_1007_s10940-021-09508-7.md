## Question, population, period, unit and estimand

The questions are: “How much do cohorts differ in their crime-proneness? How important are cohorts to our understanding of changes in crime rates? To what extent are changes in crime rates due to current conditions or to the past conditions that predispose cohorts to be more or less criminal?” [SOURCE: Introduction]

Nationwide US age-arrest data cover **1980–2016**. The state panel covers **1973–2016**, with 16 selected states reduced to **15 panel units** by combining Wyoming and Idaho. Fifteen units × 44 years gives **660 state-years**; first differences give **645**, the effective sample later cited by the paper. Table 2's reporting diagnostics and Table 3's descriptive comparisons instead cover **1974–2016**. Title and captions say 1980–2016, but the cumulative discussion and Figure 3 endpoint run through **2015**. These windows should remain explicit. The text does not give a complete one-year age-cell/sample-count inventory; Figure 1 displays ages roughly 10–65. [SOURCE: Age-Arrest Data, Estimation Sample, fn4, Tables 2–3, Potential for Improvement, Figures 1–3; INFERENCE: sample arithmetic]

Initial units are national/state **age × year arrest counts**, smoothed from one-to-five-year bins. Covariate regressions use state-year differences in estimated **period deviations**. This is not a panel of identified individual offenders. “Birth cohort” means year of birth, not immigrant generation or arrival cohort. [SOURCE: Methods; INFERENCE: distinction]

“We are primarily concerned not with arrest counts, but with criminal offense rates.” The constructed outcome is:

`μ_it = A_it / (R_t × Q_t × N_it)`

A is arrests at age i/year t; N is population; R is crime reporting probability; Q is arrests per reported offense. The model decomposes log μ into age, period and birth-cohort components, each comprising a linear slope and deviations. Because birth year + age = current year, outside information is required to separate their linear slopes. The estimand is a **conditional historical decomposition of national offense rates**, not the causal effect of immigration or a particular prevention policy. [SOURCE: equation 8, APC identification section; INFERENCE]

## Identification and named threats

The strongest case is that ordinary crime regressions omit cohort composition while APC dummies alone cannot separate linear trends. Spelman exposes that underidentification, estimates a period proxy on a larger state panel and propagates uncertainty into national curves. This is a useful alternative to silently choosing an APC constraint. It remains a conditional proxy strategy. [SOURCE: APC problem, Approach, Method; INFERENCE]

1. **Identified before the proxy:** nonlinear age/period/cohort deviations and slope combinations `κ1=π+γ`, `κ2=α−γ`. The intrinsic estimator obtains a convenient valid solution; it is not the final identification of the separate slopes. [SOURCE: equations1–3, Method]
2. **Period trend:** regress differences of estimated period deviations on differenced covariates; the negative intercept estimates π. Requires a sufficiently complete/stable period characteristic function, correct functional form and handling of serial correlation. “any estimates will remain contingent on the specification.” [SOURCE: equations4–7, Approach]
3. **Transport:** apply the convenience panel's period slopes to national κ values. Nonsignificant comparisons of observed state covariates do not establish equality of unobserved national slopes. The author admits state differences in deviations and age/cohort slopes. [SOURCE: Estimation Sample, Potential for Improvement; INFERENCE]
4. **Measurement:** use national R for every state and assume Q is similar across ages. Juvenile-specific reporting is acknowledged but unavailable; co-offending participation data are missing. Thus constructed offense rates are not directly observed age-specific offenses. [SOURCE: Age-Arrest Data]
5. **Inference:** feasible GLS on differences, accounting for heteroskedasticity/cross-state dependence; **1,500 bootstrap replications**. Tables5–6 use **one-tailed** p<.05, while Table7 uses **two-tailed** p<.05. [SOURCE: table notes, fn5]
6. **Endogeneity:** prison–crime simultaneity is admitted. Instruments fail for violent crime; the retained model uses uninstrumented prisons. Failure to reject a Granger test for police/security does not establish exogeneity. Agreement with a “consensus” estimate is not identification. [SOURCE: Estimation ofπ, fn6; INFERENCE]
7. **Omitted-variable adjustment:** `π=πhat×[1+δ(1−R²/Rmax)]`; **δ~Uniform[0,1] is assumed**, not measured. Rmax comes from Poisson variance for murder or an IV attenuation ratio for other crimes; empirical Bayes shrinkage follows. This does not bound every omitted variable, measurement error or transport failure. [SOURCE: equation9/Table7; INFERENCE]

Disconfirmation: the paper admits missing cellphones/immobilizers and imperfect wage measures; fn8 finds remaining nonrandom variance for every offense; violent-crime prison IV fails. **Table5's larceny RESET p=.031**, confirmed in layout line854, contradicts the blanket prose “there is no evidence that the functional form of any of the models was incorrect.” Other reported RESET/stability tests generally do not reject. [SOURCE: Table5, fn6/8, Conclusions; INFERENCE]
