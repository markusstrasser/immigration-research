## Question, population, period, unit and estimand

“Specifically, this study tests whether effects of funding reductions differ in rural districts compared with districts in other contexts.” The natural experiment is Kansas's 2015 Block Grant Bill, freezing district revenue at its 2015 level in FY2016–2017, less 0.4% for the Extraordinary Need Fund. The unit is district-year; post-treatment includes 2016–2018. Kansas assessments cover 2010–2018, without 2014; national SEDA covers 2009–2016. “Year indicates spring of each academic year throughout the article.” [SOURCE: Introduction; Kansas Policy Context; Measures]

Between-state estimand: “the extent to which achievement changed in Kansas districts that grew relative to comparable districts in other states (with similar pretrends) after the Block Grant Bill”. Within-state estimand: “the extent to which achievement changed in districts that grew relative to those that shrank after the Block Grant Bill”. Rural means every county containing a district school had fewer than 10 people per square mile in 2018. This is an effect of a funding regime interacted with enrollment growth, not an effect per immigrant or a race-specific incumbent effect. [SOURCE: Analysis, equations 1–2; Measures; INFERENCE]

## Identification and what could fail

Two DID designs with district fixed effects, time controls, district-clustered robust SEs and staged demographic/population/poverty-trend controls. Math donor states are Arkansas, Connecticut, Delaware, Idaho, Illinois, Maryland, Missouri, North Carolina, North Dakota, South Dakota, Utah and Vermont, selected to be within ±0.015 SD of Kansas pretrends. Within Kansas, grew means tested enrollment increased 2015→2016; controls include total/tested/weighted enrollment. No excluded instrument estimates a funding elasticity. [SOURCE: Data; Analysis]

The required assumption is parallel counterfactual achievement trends. Authors name contemporaneous other-state policies, different growth/decline pretrends, assessment changes, small rural sample, only one SEDA post year, and particular Kansas context. KSDE changed assessments and grades after 2013; annual standardization cannot itself equate the instruments. District clustering also does not automatically solve inference for one state-level treated policy; that is my additional concern. Controls partly address composition but may condition on changes caused by treatment. [SOURCE: Analysis; Conclusion; INFERENCE]

This paper measures score consequences under frozen grants, not the response elasticity under general enrollment removal. ## Misuse risk

Likely misuse: treating the −0.38 rural district-score estimate as learning lost by every native pupil because of immigration, claiming all rural definitions show significant losses, or treating small total budgets as sufficient proof of a larger per-pupil effect. The authors explicitly limit transfer: “this study examines districts in Kansas, which represent particular economic, demographic, and policy contexts.” Their abstract omits the ELA disagreement and the alternative-rural precision changes.
