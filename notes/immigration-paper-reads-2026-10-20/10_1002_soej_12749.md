## Question, population, period, unit and estimand

“I study the determinants of choosing to identify as Hispanic among individuals who are eligible—those whose parents, grandparents, or themselves were born in a Spanish-speaking country.” Empirical sample is **White-race ancestry-eligible children age17 or younger in intact/co-resident families**, not all Hispanic adults and not a complete national ancestry census. Main period2004–2021; some table notes say1994–2021. “Self-reported” ethnicity is usually reported by a parent/caregiver. Birthplace/linked parental birthplace identifies eligibility conditional on observing family links. [SOURCE: Abstract; §3.1; Tables1/2/6; §6]

“While my aim is not to establish a causal effect of bias on self-reported Hispanic identity, I intend to illustrate a correlation between bias and self-reported identity.” Estimand: child-level probability of reported Hispanic identification associated with one SD of a constructed state bias index, adjusted for individual/family composition and **region×year** effects, with state-clustered SE. ## Identification, assumptions and source contradictions

This is an observational linear probability model, not a natural experiment. Region×year effects remove common regional movements; they do not remove persistent state-specific differences. Note14: “I do not include state fixed effects because of lack of with-in state variation in bias.” State clustering addresses correlated residuals, not selection into states or the validity of the proxy. Table4 marriage regressions instead cluster by household despite state-level exposure. [SOURCE: equation4; note14; Table4 note; INFERENCE]

Threats authors name: voluntary/nonrepresentative IAT, changing test-taker composition, household proxy reporting, selective migration, intermarriage, reverse causality from group composition. Combining proxies reduces classical measurement error only under assumptions about proxy errors and the latent construct; it does not establish that skin-tone IAT plus mostly Black-attitude questions identifies Hispanic-specific prejudice. No-correlation scatterplots cannot rule out reverse causality generally. [SOURCE: §§3.2/6; note11/14; INFERENCE]

Material internal discrepancies checked against table/figure evidence: prose says approximately96/95/85% identification by G1/G2/G3, while Tables1/2 show95/93/79%; §3 says91% and age8.6, Table1 says90% and8.5. Table2 and Table6 source notes say1994–2021, main sample says2004–2021. §5 says parental education is **positively** associated, but Figures5/6 and Table3 show largely negative coefficients. These are source inconsistencies, not evidence to merge into one clean estimate. [SOURCE: §§3.1/5, Figures5/6, Tables1–3/6]

## Misuse risk

Title/abstract wording can be cited as “prejudice causes Hispanics to hide identity”; the paper itself says the aim is **not** to establish a causal effect. Another misuse is treating observed relatives' ancestry as “perfect” coverage of all descendants, or calling G3 child attrition a Mexican-specific adult loss rate. The causal disclaimer, White-race/co-resident restriction, state-FE omission and missing appendix are material limits. [SOURCE: §§3/6, note14; INFERENCE]
