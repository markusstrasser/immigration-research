Question: “the question of how immigration flows affect inequality within the receiving economies is still open and largely debated.” Their move is to model “both the wages and the prices of goods and, therefore, the profits of employers and the welfare of employees.” [SOURCE: §1, p.2]

Population definition: “We distinguish between natives N, who are born in the home country and non-natives I, who are born in a foreign country.” These are Italian residents classified by **birthplace**, two occupational skill groups and employment status. The main market population comprises employed/unemployed employees plus self-employed employers; the public-good denominator also includes inactive and non-working-age people. This is not a Mexican-origin, unauthorized-status, ethnicity, admissions-cohort or descendants estimand. [SOURCE: §§3.1,3.8, pp.6,13; Eq.26; §5.1]

Time: “The model is estimated year by year over the period 2008-2017, i.e., we consider each year separately assuming that the economy is near its equilibrium.” These are repeated fitted equilibria, not a causal panel of individual immigrants followed through a lifetime. The infinite-horizon Bellman utilities are model values conditional on each year's fixed state/parameters. “Consider a continuous time infinite-horizon economy with constant stocks of physical and human capital and technology.” [SOURCE: §§5,3.1, pp.18,6]

Main stock counterfactual: “The first scenario envisions an economy with no non-natives”; they set non-native counts and recompute the equilibrium, “keeping the parameters at their original estimated values, while allowing all endogenous variables such as wages, prices, etc. to adjust.” The objects are differences in GDP, taxes/contributions, per-worker GDP, public goods, market tightness, wages, unemployment and discounted worker/job-match utility between the fitted economy with immigrants and the fitted no-immigrant economy. ## 4. Identification and assumptions

This is a **structural calibrated/estimated general-equilibrium counterfactual**, not an IV/event-study difference in outcomes with an externally identified migration shock. There is no experimental control group. A two-sector CES production structure combines intermediate labour outputs; a Diamond–Mortensen–Pissarides search/matching structure makes job finding, job destruction and vacancy creation endogenous. Natives/non-natives have the same within-skill value added but different match probabilities, job destruction and foreign outside options. Nash bargaining determines wages; free entry makes the value of an unfilled vacancy zero. Relative product prices feed back to wages, filled-match values and demand. [SOURCE: §§3–4, pp.6–18]

Fitting: “The seventeen model's parameters are estimated using the method of simulated moments ... assuming that the bargaining power is the same for natives and non-natives, i.e β=βN=βI, since both βs cannot be identified simultaneously.” The next section and Appendix P say **sixteen** free parameters, consistent with imposing the equality. Twenty-six moments are HP-filtered and matched using the identity weighting matrix. Footnote21 says a variance weighting would give some administrative/large-survey moments extremely large weights, hence equal weights. This is a modelling decision, not proof of equal measurement reliability. Good fit to included moments is in-sample calibration support, not an independently identified no-immigration counterfactual. [SOURCE: §§5.2–5.3, pp.21–23; Appendix P; INFERENCE for external-validation distinction]

Required assumptions carrying the result:

- Annual economy near equilibrium despite the recession period; native/immigrant occupational skill and labour-market flow proxies correctly capture productivity/matching heterogeneity. No capital, technology or human-capital accumulation; no savings/investment; inelastic labour supply and risk neutrality. Working-age employees are central, with inactive dependents entering public-good congestion rather than a separately estimated age-by-program fiscal account. [SOURCE: §§3.1,3.4–3.5,5, Appendices H,I,K]
- Everyone consumes both final goods with the same CES preference parameters. Baseline final-good substitution elasticity ε=1, with public/private utility weight ι=1 and congestion ζ=1. The authors explicitly admit these three are not identified. The reported welfare compensation for low-skilled workers is therefore conditional on an assumed public-good utility valuation and congestion rule. [SOURCE: §§3.4,5.1,6.1.1; Eq.26]
- Government public-goods spending is a fixed sector-value-added share; unemployment benefits are a wage fraction; all workers have the same modelled access. The tax gap is rebated as a common lump sum to employees/employers. **“In equilibrium, the government primary surplus is equal to zero, i.e., TGE=NDT+IT as in any closed economy with no investments and savings, independent on any fiscal parameter.”** The headline fiscal benefit is more provision per capita under this closure, not an observed change in a government deficit, a tax-minus-expenditure incidence table or accrued retirement promises. [SOURCE: §§1,3.8, pp.4,12–13; Appendix F, Eq.60]
- η and λ are common across skill groups and exogenous; only non-native unemployed people exit abroad. Native departure is suppressed. Matching-function elasticity0.4; discount rate monthly0.01; tax rate0.43 all years; bargaining power equal by nativity in estimation. The shocks hold calibrated/estimated parameters fixed. [SOURCE: §§3.1,3.6,5.1–5.2,6; notes7–8; Appendix K]

Authors' acknowledged threats include imperfect skill proxies and immigrant occupational downgrading (I); imputed skill for the unemployed (I); no wage release before2008; wages capped/truncated €250–€3,000; exclusion of extra salaries; tiny high-skill immigrant wage sample (notes26–27); missing comprehensive relative-price data and the wage-price proportionality conjecture (C); limited producer-price-sector coverage and explicitly anecdotal validation (B); unidentified public/private valuation and congestion (5.1); smaller effective benefit access than formal entitlement (note29); nationality replacing birthplace in the temporary-contract comparison (Table15); assumptions on firing litigation and minimal2015 Jobs Act aggregate impact (K.2). These limits attach to both favorable and unfavorable immigration results. [SOURCE: listed sections]

No structural parameter standard-error matrix, bootstrap distribution or confidence intervals for Figures1,3–7 and Tables3–4 are reported in this supplied manuscript. Sensitivity curves are alternative model assumptions, not sampling confidence bands. The regression standard errors in Tables5–6 do not quantify all counterfactual uncertainty. [SOURCE: complete manuscript, Tables5–6; INFERENCE]

## 9. Misuse risk and strongest fair interpretation

Strongest fair interpretation: at the fitted Italian2008–17 states, modelling goods prices, labour frictions and government provision jointly can yield substantial aggregate production/revenue benefits and highly uneven incidence. Low-skilled employers can lose in per-match value even while low wages fall, because their output price also falls. The stock-removal baseline allows increased public goods to compensate low-skilled employees; nearby assumptions can reverse their welfare sign. This is a substantive check against claiming that wage competition alone determines every incumbent's gain or loss. [SOURCE: §§4.4,6,Appendices D/E; INFERENCE]

Likely overstatements and limiting language:

- **“Published evidence proves low-skill immigration pays fiscally in America.”** Source text is a2024 Italian manuscript; the journal has not been read. “we consider each year separately assuming that the economy is near its equilibrium.” Transfer across country, population, institutions and counterfactual is unmeasured. [SOURCE: §5; version check]
- **“The government collected~€75bn more, so the net surplus was€75bn.”** “the government primary surplus is equal to zero ... independent on any fiscal parameter.” The paper's definition of net fiscal benefit is increased per-capita provision under a closure, not deficit reduction. It includes social contributions without a matched old-age accrual account. [SOURCE: §3.8; Appendix F; K.3; INFERENCE]
- **“All low-skilled natives are at least as well off.”** “Within this setup, and given the available set of information, we cannot identify ι,ζ andρ.” Public/private valuation is set to perfect substitutability; sensitivity changes signs, and Table4's marginal-shock low-skilled utilities are negative. [SOURCE: §5.1; §§6.1.1,6.2]
- **“The model proves employers always gain from cheap migrant labour.”** “the sign of the impact of an immigration shock is undetermined a priori”; wage and output-price effects compete. Conversely, the working manuscript's lower filled-match value does not establish lower **aggregate** low-skill-sector profits. [SOURCE: §4.4; version-check publisher abstract; INFERENCE]
- **“The observed relative prices validate immigration as their cause.”** “Overall, this anecdotal evidence provides support to our findings.” Selected prices and a trend are compatible with other supply/demand shocks; Appendix B does not isolate migration causally. [SOURCE: Appendix B; INFERENCE]
- **“Higher social contributions are a full lifetime fiscal gain.”** Listed public-good categories omit old-age accrual and the model fixes human/physical capital and technology. Infinite-horizon utility is not a discounted multi-generation tax-and-benefit account. [SOURCE: §§3.1,3.8; Table17; INFERENCE]
- **“These results persist in the long run.”** The publisher's current abstract explicitly says: “Whether these effects persist in the long run remains an open question.” That limits claims about the journal too, without importing unread journal tables. [SOURCE: publisher abstract, https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/741640]

The relevant frame is **other-resident incidence under matched counterfactuals**; a global-output, immigrant-welfare or employer-profit frame answers a different question. The scope restrictions and unexplained source discrepancies apply symmetrically to favorable aggregate results and unfavorable low-skill effects.
