## Question, population, period, unit and estimand

**Question:** “The wage change observed in a targeted market will depend not only on the size of the native response, but also on its composition.” The paper separates an actual wage-offer change from changing composition of observed employed natives and attenuation when natives withdraw. [SOURCE: §1]

**Population:** “Our sample is restricted to persons aged 18-64 living in European France. We exclude all persons who are self-employed, are in military occupations, are enrolled in school, or do not report their educational attainment.” Wages are monthly, net of employee payroll contributions, for full-time native workers, trimmed at the bottom/top 0.5% of the hourly-wage distribution; alternatives use hourly wages and part-time workers. An immigrant is “a person born outside France who is either a noncitizen or a naturalized citizen. All other persons are classified as natives.” This is not Mexican origin, unauthorized status, or a descendants-inclusive lineage. [SOURCE: §2]

**Period and unit:** “Our empirical analysis covers the 1982-2016 period”; baseline census cross-sections are 1982, 1990, 1999, 2007, 2016, matched to pooled LFS waves, with **22 regions × 5 dates = 110 region-year cells** per gender regression. “In this descriptive analysis, the unit of observation is a region-year cell.” Individual wage regressions estimate adjusted cell wages, which become outcomes in the second-stage cell regression. [SOURCE: §2, §4.1, Table 4 notes]

**Estimands:** The reduced form permits the native employment response; the full model conditions on the log native labor force. The authors define the latter coefficient as “the downward movement along the short-run labor demand curve after immigrants enter the local labor market.” The shock is `m_rt = log(1 + M_rt/N_rt)`, with both-sex labor-force counts in the baseline. The dependent variable is the estimated population wage-offer mean after a Heckman correction, not the mean actual earnings of everyone (zero earnings included), total household resources, national real income, or net taxes. The region-only empirical model is also described as capturing own effects, cross-skill effects and capital adjustments; therefore even the paper's “short-run” label does not supply a uniformly timed event-study horizon. [SOURCE: §§3.3–4.1, equations 17, 24a–b; INFERENCE: distinction between outcomes/horizons]

## Identification, assumptions and disconfirmation

The strongest case for the paper is the combination of an explicit selection model, sizable observed employment responses among women, stable adverse corrected coefficients across many specifications, and a negative wage effect even in high-attachment female subsamples. It is not merely a claim that every small measured wage effect must be biased. [SOURCE: §§3–7; INFERENCE]

1. **Selection stage:** gender-specific probits estimate employment, including age, education and region-time fixed effects. For women the excluded variables are marital status, under-six children and mortgage-free homeownership; for men marital/child variables enter both wage and probit equations, so only homeownership independently identifies selection. Add the resulting inverse Mills ratio to individual full-time wage regressions; use their region-time fixed effects as selection-adjusted wages. Requires the Heckman distribution/functional-form assumptions and excluded variables affecting selection but not potential wages conditional on controls. Ownership may proxy omitted productivity/resources; children and marriage may affect wages beyond reservation wages. The paper itself changes these exclusions and uses a linear-probability/uniform-error alternative. [SOURCE: §§4.1–4.2,6.1]
2. **Immigration IV:** 1968 region shares for 11 nationality × four education groups allocate later national immigrant totals; education-specific native shares allocate later native totals. The instrument is `log(1 + predicted M/predicted N)`. Requires baseline distributions uncorrelated with later unobserved regional wage growth; serial local demand and adjustment to previous immigration can violate this. The 1976 family-reunification change motivates the gender setting; it is **not itself the identifying instrument or a randomized policy contrast**. [SOURCE: §§1,4.3]
3. **Native-supply IV:** predict female native labor force using the native shift-share population, adjusted by actual-minus-predicted regional population growth in 1968–82, multiplied by the region/date fraction female without under-six children. Requires old sorting, growth trends and current child-presence shocks to affect wages only through labor supply. The authors explicitly concede “the absence of compelling exogenous shocks in native labor supply” and report reduced forms as well as full models. [SOURCE: §4.4]
4. **Inference:** cell-size weights; clustered region SEs; 1,000-replication wild-cluster bootstrap p-values because there are only 22 regions. Report bootstrap inference alongside conventional stars: they disagree materially. Baseline first-stage KP F=32.06 women/21.00 men; two-endogenous-regressor SW F=12.91/15.15 women and 14.09/16.53 men. These support baseline relevance, not exclusion or uniform strength in robustness models. [SOURCE: Tables2,4, §5.1/5.3]
5. **Other margins:** correction only covers employment participation. Regional migration is asserted small based on cited French evidence, but department migration is acknowledged; occupational/schooling/multiple-margin selection remains untreated. The theoretical supply model initially holds capital fixed, while the regional empirical coefficient can include capital response and complementary skill effects. [SOURCE: §§3.3,4.1,6.2,7]

**Evidence against an overconfident interpretation:** Appendix B has a rejection for female homeownership outside Paris in 1982 (statistic 0.23, **p=0.00**); Table 6's top-1%-predicted-employment result loses significance under the recommended bootstrap (**p=.29**); several full-model robustness instruments are weak (C2 SW around 2.5–3.5, C3 around 5–8). Non-rejection of remaining exclusion tests cannot establish validity. These limitations coexist with strong selection sensitivity in the better-powered reduced forms. [SOURCE: Appendix B, Tables6,C2,C3; INFERENCE]

**Appendix A, not a blanket indictment of panels:** “We assume that immigration affects the wage structure by only shifting the mean … of the wage distribution.” Derivation uses joint normality, fixed reservation wage and unchanged variance. Panel bias vanishes with perfect serial correlation in earnings. A3's sufficient positive-bias condition is participation above0.5 after an adverse shock; A4's closed-form comparison assumes **independent employment outcomes across dates**, an illustrative special case, not a claim this holds in observed French panels. Quote: “The panel estimator produces a larger positive bias if” the specified covariance inequality holds. Therefore the paper shows continuous-employment restriction **can** fail, not that every panel study is necessarily worse than every cross-section. [SOURCE: Appendix A1–A4]

## Misuse risk, limits and remaining gaps
