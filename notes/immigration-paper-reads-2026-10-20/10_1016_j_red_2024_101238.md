## 3. Identification, assumptions and threats

No instrument, natural experiment, sampling uncertainty or confidence interval identifies the model's headline. The counterfactual solves a general-equilibrium search-and-matching system. Free entry responds to expected surplus; lower immigrant outside options and evaded income/payroll tax raise hiring profitability. Baseline workers are perfect substitutes in one matching pool; capital adjusts at a fixed real return. Unemployment and bargaining parameters, ownership, taxes and service access are imposed/calibrated. Steady-state public debt is fixed and the lump-sum tax on natives/legal immigrants closes the budget; the congestion extension instead adjusts services. [SOURCE: §§2–4]

The authors themselves test equal legal/native wages, 5%/0% legal–illegal wage gaps, zero undocumented search cost, higher return/separation rates, CES imperfect substitution, segmented job markets, higher impure spending, public-consumption preferences and unequal service access. They acknowledge missing intergenerational aging/pension dynamics in §6; no skill distribution or Mexican-lineage generation structure is estimated. “In lack of good empirical estimate” they set CES share parameter .6; its job-acceptance feasibility does not empirically identify it. [SOURCE: §§4.2.5, 5.1–5.2, 6; Appendices A–C]

Strongest case for the paper: even low direct tax payments can coexist with positive native consumption when employment and profits respond. The decisive vulnerability is magnitude: the calibrated wage disadvantage generates the job-creation channel, while the baseline freezes most spending. Calling zero income/payroll contributions a universally conservative assumption misses its simultaneous role in lowering employers' costs and increasing endogenous profits. [SOURCE: §2/FN2] [INFERENCE]
