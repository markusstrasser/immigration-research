## 4. Design, assumptions and threats

Tables1–2 are weighted least-squares comparisons: age fixed effects in schooling; age and state fixed effects in log earnings, separately by sex, among positive earners. No IV, randomized treatment, or identified discrimination component. “Earnings represent the market's valuation of a worker's entire package of abilities and attributes,” but gaps also incorporate hours, discrimination and unmeasured family/school quality. Controlling for education partitions an association; its57% attenuation is not the causal effect of eliminating a schooling deficit. Positive-earner selection excludes nonworkers; Figure1 uses men to reduce, not eliminate, participation bias. [SOURCE: pp.175–179; Tables1–2 notes]

Figure1 compares repeated cross-sections, so changing origin/generation/identity composition can matter. Figure2 predicts schooling at age35 from a quartic in age and sex×birth-state×birth-year cell indicators; birth year is survey year−age−1; cohorts must appear in at least two surveys. ItsCA/TX contrast is state of **birth**, not current state of residence or policy treatment. Historical schooling differences do not identify segregation's causal contribution. The text offers farming and discriminatory schooling as hypotheses needing work. [SOURCE: pp.179–185,Figure2 note]

Table3A pools unlike generations/cohorts. Table3B's ages50–59 and25–34 are a25-year synthetic parent-child spacing, not linked families: “the older age group from a particular immigrant generation potentially represents the parental cohort for the younger age group in the next generation.” Assumes little schooling change after25 and comparable cohort composition; second→third-plus comparisons are less accurate because G4+ people are included in the latter. The strongest disconfirmation of a literal stagnation interpretation is the paper's own cohort-adjusted progression, below. [SOURCE: pp.188–189]

The second threat is selective identity measurement: first/second generations can be located with birthplace; G3+ identification needs subjective origin reporting. “Although available evidence makes clear the direction of this bias, data limitations have made it difficult to assess the magnitude of the bias.” Alternative surname/Spanish-language markers have the same selection problem. CPS excludes institutions, unlike the Census/ACS schooling samples; race comparisons involving Black men can therefore shift simply through custody exclusion. Historical links have low, nonrepresentative match rates and lose married women; modern Census–tax links omit undocumented immigrants and their descendants in the cited studies. [SOURCE: pp.189–193,fn.5–6]

## 10. Misuse risk and remaining gaps

Strongest favorable reading: Hispanic descendants make largeG1→G2gains, nonidentifiers are positively selected, intermarriage/English use show considerable integration, and uncorrectedG3+statistics omit some success. Strongest adverse reading: even US-born/young identified Mexican Americans retain large schooling and earnings gaps, college convergence has failed, and measured labor-market convergence is slower than a simple assimilation narrative predicts. Both survive the full read. [SOURCE: Tables1–3,Figures1–3,Conclusion]

The authors' actual limiting passage is: “The bottom line is that later-generation Hispanics are likely doing better than previous research suggests, but how much better is hard to say at this point.” Using the paper to assert fiscal payback, complete assimilation, measured eventual parity, or a quantified hidden-lineage dividend outruns it. Using Table3A to assert permanent stagnation ignores their cohort and attrition corrections. Using English-language assimilation or intermarriage as evidence of lower crime or budget costs changes the outcome. [SOURCE: Conclusion,p.194; INFERENCE]
