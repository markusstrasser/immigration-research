### Claim 2 — "America, but with fewer immigrants" (Econlib) — [VERIFIED]

Live econlib.org returned HTTP 403 to curl; fetched via Wayback raw body 2026-09-22:

Sumner introduces the study and block-quotes the abstract:

> David Levey directed me to a recent study of this question, by Alessandro Caiumi and Giovanni Peri.

> Using these estimates, we calculate that immigration, thanks to native-immigrant complementarity and

The post marks "native-immigrant complementarity and college skill content of immigrants" with

- No crowding out: VERIFIED as paraphrase. The exact words are "no significant crowding out effects on

### Claim 7 — the "110 million" counterfactual — [VERIFIED verbatim]

### Claim 4 — "Immigration, wages, and the Phillips Curve" — [PARTIALLY VERIFIED — the framing is not his]

Fetched live (HTTP 200), 2026-09-22:
https://www.themoneyillusion.com/immigration-wages-and-the-phillips-curve/
Post date on page: "This entry was posted on May 03rd, 2024". Note the live title capitalizes "Phillips
Curve". The post body is six short paragraphs.

What the post actually argues, verbatim:

> As you may know, the actual Phillips Curve is about the relationship between unemployment and wage
> inflation. That's the relationship A.W. Phillips actually identified, and it's still the correct
> version. Price inflation doesn't correlate well with unemployment. Painful disinflation is and always
> has been about getting wage inflation down.

> It's ironic that the thing that may well cost Biden the election (a surge in undocumented immigration)
> is the very thing that might (and I emphasize might) allow the first recession-free disinflation. The
> first American soft landing.

> PPS. It looks to me like wages are about 8% above pre-Covid trend and NGDP is about 10% above trend. I
> suppose the gap is unexpectedly high immigration. So Fed policy has been roughly 8% to 10% too
> expansionary over the past 5 years.

Sumner confirms the wage/NGDP mechanism explicitly in the comments, answering Travis Allison on
6 May 2024 at 08:02:

> "Are you saying that the gap between the 8% increase in wages vs the 10% increase in NGDP can be
> explained by high immigration?"
> Yes.
> "If so, are you implying that immigration depressed wages relative to prices?"
> No. It may be, but that's not what I'm saying. I'm saying it depressed wages relative to NGDP.

Claim-by-claim:
- Disinflation without a recession: VERIFIED as paraphrase. His words are "the first recession-free
  disinflation. The first American soft landing", hedged twice with "might (and I emphasize might)".
- Hourly wages lag nominal GDP: VERIFIED — "wages are about 8% above pre-Covid trend and NGDP is about
  10% above trend", and in comments "it depressed wages relative to NGDP".
- **"Under nominal-GDP targeting": NOT IN THE POST.** The phrase "NGDP target" appears nowhere in the
  post body. It appears only in the comment section, raised by a commenter (Viennacapitalist, 5 May 2024)
  asking whether above-trend immigration should raise the NGDP target, and in Sumner's reply: "You can
  make an argument for doing the NGDP targeting in per capita terms, but I don't expect a significant
  increase in the population growth rate going forward. I think this is a blip." That is Sumner declining
  to make the adjustment, not arguing from an NGDP-targeting frame.
- **"Immigration is a supply shock": NOT FOUND.** The words "supply shock" do not occur in the post or
  its comments. "Supply-side" appears only in the sidebar blogroll.
- **"Real output rises": NOT stated in this post.** The post never mentions real GDP or real output. That
  argument is in a different Sumner post, "The Immigration Pillow" (Econlib, Jul 25 2024), which does
  give RGDP figures. Attributing it to this post is a merge of two posts.
- **Direction to flag: the post's closing sentence is a criticism of the Fed, not a case for immigration
  — "So Fed policy has been roughly 8% to 10% too expansionary over the past 5 years."**

### Claim 9 — Trustees Report immigration sensitivity — [VERIFIED for the gap, CONTRADICTED for the $1.5tn]

Source: 2024 OASDI Trustees Report. ssa.gov returns 403 to both curl and WebFetch; fetched the full PDF
via Wayback raw body 2026-09-22 and parsed it locally with pdftotext:
https://web.archive.org/web/2024id_/https://www.ssa.gov/OACT/TR/2024/tr2024.pdf
(The 2025 PDF was fetched from the same route but the body arrived truncated with an invalid xref and
would not parse. The numbers below are the 2024 report's. [GAP] 2025 not re-verified.)

Table VI.D3, page 190, "Sensitivity of OASDI Measures to Total Net Immigration Assumptions",
[As a percentage of taxable payroll]. Average annual total net immigration, 2034 through 2098:

| Valuation period | 829,000 (low) | 1,244,000 (intermediate) | 1,683,000 (high) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Actuarial balance, 25-year 2024-48 | -2.48 | -2.28 | -2.08 |
| Actuarial balance, 50-year 2024-73 | -3.37 | -3.06 | -2.76 |
| Actuarial balance, 75-year 2024-98 | -3.90 | -3.50 | -3.12 |

The report's own summary of that table, verbatim:

> The actuarial balance increases from -2.48 percent to -2.08 percent for the 25-year period, from
> -3.37 percent to -2.76 percent for the 50-year period, and from -3.90 percent to -3.12 percent for the
> 75-year period.

> The cost rate decreases with an increase in total net immigration because immigration occurs at
> relatively young ages, thereby increasing the numbers of covered workers earlier than the numbers of
> beneficiaries. Increasing average annual total net immigration by 100,000 persons increases (improves)
> the long-range actuarial balance by about 0.09 percent of taxable payroll.

Note the table footnote: only immigration is varied. "The total net immigration assumptions used for this
analysis are consistent with those assumed for the three alternative scenarios. All other assumptions
used for this analysis are from alternative II."

The gap, verbatim from the report's overview:

> The OASDI actuarial deficit is 3.50 percent of taxable payroll for the 75-year projection period
> through 2098 ... The open-group unfunded obligation for OASDI is 3.32 percent of taxable payroll over
> the 75-year projection period through 2098 ... Expressed in present-value dollars discounted to
> January 1, 2024, the open-group unfunded obligation for OASDI is $22.6 trillion over the 75-year
> projection period through 2098.

Verdict on "about $1.5 trillion on a gap of about $23 trillion":
- **"A gap of about $23 trillion": VERIFIED.** The report states $22.6 trillion, 75-year open-group
  unfunded obligation, present value discounted to January 1, 2024.
- **High immigration vs intermediate, in percent of taxable payroll: 0.38 percentage points**
  (-3.50 to -3.12) over 75 years. That is the answer the report actually gives.
- **"About $1.5 trillion": NOT IN THE REPORT, and too small.** Table VI.D3 is expressed only as a
  percentage of taxable payroll; the report publishes no present-value dollar figure for the immigration
  sensitivity. [CALCULATION] Converting on the report's own two anchors — the unfunded obligation is both
  3.32 percent of taxable payroll and $22.6 trillion, implying a present value of taxable payroll of
  $680.7 trillion — 0.38 percent of payroll is **about $2.6 trillion**, or 10.9 percent of the 3.50
  percent deficit. Measured inputs: the two published ratios. Assumed: that the actuarial-balance change
  and the unfunded obligation share the same payroll base, which they do by construction.
- So the correct statement is roughly **$2.6 trillion on a gap of $22.6 trillion**, i.e. the high
  immigration alternative closes about a ninth of the 75-year shortfall. Using $1.5 trillion understates
  the sensitivity by about 40 percent, while still leaving the qualitative point intact: immigration at
  the high alternative does not come close to closing the gap.

## Summary

Six of the nine claims survive contact with the primary pages, but three of the load-bearing ones are
paraphrases presented as quotes. The strongest are claims 3 and 8: Sumner's "3 million a year is a good
start" sentence and the Caiumi-Peri abstract are verbatim, word for word, and the NBER abstract is
identical to the text Sumner block-quotes at Econlib. Claim 1 is verbatim for every CBO number but has
two placement errors: the period is 2023 to 2034 rather than 2024-34, and "Our budget deficit was reduced
by the recent wave of immigration" is Sumner's comment five days after the post, not a sentence in it.
Claim 5 is real and quotable, with the correction that Sumner's reply was addressed to a different
commenter, so "replied without correcting it" holds only in the weak sense that he posted in the thread
and let the "undeniable" line stand. Claim 7 is verbatim but lives in the Econlib post, not
TheMoneyIllusion, and Sumner poses 110 million as a question to immigration opponents rather than
asserting that America is rich because it is large. Claim 2's "no crowding out" is a real phrase, "no
significant crowding out effects on employment", but it belongs to the 2019-2022 simulation and to
employment rather than to the headline 2000-2019 wage estimate. The two that should not be used as
written are claims 4 and 6. The Phillips curve post contains the wage-lags-NGDP observation, confirmed by
Sumner in comments as "it depressed wages relative to NGDP", but it contains no supply-shock language, no
nominal-GDP-targeting frame, and no real-output claim, and it closes by calling Fed policy 8 to 10
percent too expansionary. Claim 6's sentence does not exist: Sumner wrote only "Immigration helps our
economy, especially programs such as Social Security", with no Medicare and no "best solution", and when
he does name a best solution for Social Security elsewhere it is spending cuts and a carbon tax. On claim
9 the $23 trillion gap is right and the $1.5 trillion is wrong; the report gives 0.38 percent of taxable
payroll, which converts to about $2.6 trillion on its own anchors.
