## Mechanism exploration

Hypotheses considered, not preregistered: lost unpaid care could reduce a staying relative's employment; lost earnings could instead induce more work; scarce formal care could leave needs unmet rather than generate replacement family care; other caregivers or household restructuring could buffer the shock; measured service-price or generic labor responses could overlap an apparent new effect. None is treated as evidence merely because it is plausible.

## Primary evidence

**H1 — Kreider and Werner, January 31, 2025 working paper.** US ACS workforce and HRS care outcomes around Secure Communities; the HRS analysis restricts to adults over 65 with an ADL/IADL difficulty. Table 9 (PDF p35, printed p34), Medicaid sample: primary-family-care probability rises **7.5 percentage points (SE 3.5)** from 73.0%, *conditional on receiving any help*. The unconditional probability of having a family helper changes **−2.0pp (SE 2.6)** from 56.4%. Section 6.3.1 explicitly defines the conditioning; family help includes paid relatives. Thus the positive conditional share is not an estimate of additional caregiver hours, number of caregiving households, or foregone employment. The full-sample family-care outcomes are insignificant (Table 8). This does not show caregiver work losses are zero. [SOURCE: https://amandakreider.github.io/files/Immigration-Home-Care-2025.pdf, §§4.2/6.3.1, Tables 8–10; author PDF retrieved live, title/date checked]

**H2 — Frimmel, Halla, Paetzold and Schmieder.** The 2020 IZA full text studies Austrian parental strokes and adult children's labor outcomes. Its care-reform comparison uses stroke versus heart-attack families, before/after the health shock and before/after the 2007 formal-care reform (§8.5). Results support a role for formal care in buffering children's employment losses; alternative windows and exclusion of partly treated controls matter. The reform combined legalization, market access and a particular live-in care arrangement. This is not a US caregiver labor elasticity or an isolated Mexican-origin supply shock. The final publisher record is June 9, 2025, *Health of Parents, Their Children's Labor Supply, and the Role of Migrant Care Workers*, DOI 10.1086/729102; final full text was not accessible here. No working-paper number is labeled a final-version estimate. [SOURCE: https://docs.iza.org/dp13774.pdf §§8.1–8.5 and Table 3; https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/729102]

**H3 — Rubalcaba, Bucheli and Morales.** The authors' full working-paper text studies US-born Hispanic youth in mixed-status families using CPS and unusually high local ICE arrests in2014–2018. It finds increased youth work, consistent with household income smoothing, and tests citizen-parent families as a comparison. Above-trend arrests need an exogeneity assumption; they are not randomized. The publisher verifies the 2024 journal paper, DOI10.1007/s00148-024-01022-x; final full text is paywalled. Publisher note19 explicitly says the sample definition excludes youth whose noncitizen parent was already deported and who stayed with a citizen parent/relative. It therefore cannot identify the labor response of that surviving-household population. No numerical final-paper estimate is imported. [SOURCE: https://www.thecgo.org/research/immigration-enforcement-and-labor-supply/, author working-paper body; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00148-024-01022-x, Notes19–20]
