**Verdict:** A universal 100% ordinary-service response is an assumption, not an established empirical estimate. Evidence supports lower immediate budget responses for some services, but it does not identify a national long-run percentage for the entire Mexican-origin population. Nor does 100% form a hard upper bound: congestion and new capacity can make marginal costs exceed assigned average costs. Low observed spending can also mean worse services rather than low welfare costs. [INFERENCE from sources below]

# External evidence on the ordinary-service response

Date: 2026-09-20. Bounded primary-source check; parent task owns the exact local category decomposition and arithmetic. No code changed and no universal coefficient fitted. LLM selection/interpretation can be biased; sources below deliberately include evidence against both zero-cost and full-average-cost presumptions.

## What the parameter means

Let assigned ordinary-service spending for the population be S. The model's response coefficient r specifies the counterfactual budget difference as rS, with other model components held fixed. r=1 says that all of that assigned spending disappears in the comparison without the population; r=0.25 says one quarter disappears. This is a statement about a counterfactual budget response, not the fraction of people using services or the fraction receiving benefits. [Model interpretation supplied by parent task]

A child can use a classroom while causing little immediate extra spending if capacity is spare. The same enrollment change can trigger another teacher or school when capacity is full. A budget can also stay unchanged while class sizes rise. The last outcome is not equivalent to costless service provision. [INFERENCE; capacity mechanism grounded in GAO below]

## 1. CBO: empirical short-run education adjustment, assumed category responses

[SOURCE: CBO, *Effects of the Surge in Immigration on State and Local Budgets in 2023*, June 2025, education section and technical appendix](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61464).

CBO's 1999–2000 to 2019–20 state panel associates enrollment-growth increases of one percentage point with spending-per-student growth lower by 0.37 points; decreases correspond to a 0.34-point increase. Its regression includes state effects. This implies approximately 63–66% total-spending response per added/removed average-cost pupil to first order, **not a causal long-run estimate**. [SOURCE; arithmetic INFERENCE]

CBO's education totals are $5.7bn direct and $9.4bn potential; both contain $1.2bn for English learners. Their ratio is not a clean cost coefficient: CBO also assumes one nonsurge pupil leaves public school per six surge pupils. [SOURCE]

CBO assumes immediate proportional spending for police/fire/courts, health, housing/community and income-security services; transportation, other economic affairs, recreation/culture/libraries and government administration do not immediately respond. It distinguishes congestion from budget changes and explicitly acknowledges uncertainty in category assignment. [SOURCE]

**Limit:** These are near-term state/local surge calculations, with different populations and category weights. They cannot supply r for all generations, the federal government, or a stationary long-run counterfactual. The police/health coefficients are assumptions, not new causal estimates. [INFERENCE]

## 2. National Academies: average cost is defensible for some services, but not a universal marginal rule

[SOURCE: NASEM, *The Economic and Fiscal Consequences of Immigration*, chapter 7, pp.344–347](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/23550/chapter/12).

The panel says education and health provision can be approximately proportional to recipients, supporting average-cost allocation. Other services depend only partly on population. For congestible goods, incremental costs can be above or below average. Its capacity example is a school needing new buildings and teachers. Footnote 20 explains that models frequently fix service quality and provision levels, which requires higher spending as population rises. The panel also distinguishes one added immigrant from large multiyear inflows and says zero marginal public-goods costs become less tenable for the latter. [SOURCE]

**Limit:** This is a conceptual review, not an estimated ordinary-service elasticity. The zero-cost public-goods scenarios in chapter 8 do not empirically establish zero marginal costs for the entire ordinary-service block. [SOURCE: chapter 8, pp.363–365](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/23550/chapter/13).

## 3. GAO: fixed, variable and capacity-triggered school costs

[SOURCE: GAO-04-733, *Illegal Alien Schoolchildren: Issues in Estimating State-by-State Costs*, June 2004, “School-Capacity Approach,” table 1](https://www.gao.gov/assets/a243051.html).

GAO separates fixed administrative costs, per-student materials, and lumpy expansion costs such as teachers and buildings. A classroom with spare capacity mainly incurs variable costs for an extra pupil; stretched capacity activates expansion costs. GAO says incremental costs can be substantially above or below average and identifies extensive local data needs. [SOURCE]

**Limit:** A methodological finding, not a present-day national estimate. School funding transferred from state to district is not itself an additional consolidated-government resource cost; expenditure and funding must be reconciled. Building costs also need a consistent annual-capital treatment before comparing them with a current-spending ledger. [INFERENCE]

## 4. St. Clair: causal local evidence against assuming tiny costs everywhere

[SOURCE: Travis St. Clair, *The Fiscal Effects of Immigration on Local Governments: Revisiting the Mariel Boatlift*, author-hosted working-paper version, sections 5–7](https://wagner.nyu.edu/files/faculty/publications/Mariel%20Boatlift_4.pdf). [Published record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S016604622400084X).

The synthetic-control design uses ten pretreatment years and distinct city, county and school-district budgets. It finds sustained school-district spending increases and a short-lived city increase. The author-hosted draft reports roughly 26% higher school-district expenditure over 1981–1990; average city expenditure effects are not statistically significant. It approximates pupils using school-age population because consistent district enrollment data are unavailable. [SOURCE]

**Limit:** A single unusual refugee shock, sensitive to donor construction and subsequent local shocks. The draft's marginal-per-pupil multiplier differs from the published abstract, so do not quote that multiplier without obtaining the final full text. The directional result undermines a universal low-cost rule; it does not calibrate today's national r. An earlier competing Mariel study uses personal-income-scaled outcomes, making it a different fiscal estimand. [SOURCE, draft footnote 1; INFERENCE on generalizability]

## Interpretation for the current model

- **Short-run realized-budget question:** Allow category-specific r values below one where budgets/capacity adjust slowly. CBO supplies an evidence-informed education sensitivity and transparent service-category assumptions, not a nationwide estimate. [INFERENCE]
- **Long-run equal-service-quality question:** A lower response requires actual scale economies/spare capacity, not merely fixed appropriations. Persistent population differences eventually affect staffing, maintenance and capacity. No checked source estimates the weighted r for our exact population. [INFERENCE]
- **Welfare question:** Count changes in service quality/congestion separately. Setting r=0 and assuming zero quality effect is a stronger joint assumption than keeping spending fixed. [INFERENCE]
- **Large removal versus one entrant:** Costs need not reverse symmetrically; preexisting assets, employment contracts, local school closures and population geography matter. Do not transplant a small short-run shock elasticity to a large stationary counterfactual without a bridge. [INFERENCE]
- **100% is not a scientific ceiling.** A 0–100% scenario grid brackets fixed versus proportional costs but does not exhaust capacity-constrained or higher-need possibilities. [INFERENCE]
