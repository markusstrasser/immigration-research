# Definitions, quoted from the primary documents

## DHS Office of Homeland Security Statistics — reference date 2022-01-01

**Citation.** Bryan Baker and Robert Warren, "Estimates of the Unauthorized Immigrant Population Residing in the United States: January 2018-January 2022", OHSS, April 2024  
**URL.** https://www.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/2024-05/2024_0418_ohss_estimates-of-the-unauthorized-immigrant-population-residing-in-the-united-states-january2018%E2%80%93january-2022.pdf

**Definition.** “The resident unauthorized immigrant population is defined as all foreign-born noncitizens who are not legal residents as defined above. Most unauthorized immigrants either entered the United States without inspection or were admitted temporarily and remained past the date they were required to depart. Persons who are beneficiaries of Temporary Protected Status (TPS), Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA) or other forms of prosecutorial discretion, or who are residing in the United States while awaiting removal proceedings in immigration court are included among the estimates of the unauthorized population. Unauthorized immigrants applying for adjustment to LPR status under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) are considered to be part of the resident unauthorized population until they have been granted lawful permanent residence. Individuals who were paroled into the United States are considered to be unauthorized immigrants until they are admitted or otherwise acquire immigration status.”

**Coverage adjustment.** “This report assumes that the undercount rate for unauthorized immigrants in the ACS is 13 percent for those who arrived in the most recent year and declines by 7.5 percent with each year of presence.”

**Mexico share.** “Forty-four percent of unauthorized immigrants in 2022 were from Mexico, compared to 59 percent in 2010 and 55 percent in 2015.”

## Pew Research Center — reference date 2023-07-01

**Citation.** Jeffrey S. Passel and Jens Manuel Krogstad, "U.S. Unauthorized Immigrant Population Reached a Record 14 Million in 2023", Pew Research Center, August 21, 2025  
**URL.** https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/2025/08/21/u-s-unauthorized-immigrant-population-reached-a-record-14-million-in-2023/

**Definition.** “The U.S. unauthorized immigrant population includes any immigrants not in the following groups: 1. Lawful permanent residents (green card holders) 2. Refugees formally admitted to the United States 3. People granted asylum 4. Former unauthorized immigrants granted legal residence under the 1986 Immigration Reform and Control Act 5. Naturalized U.S. citizens who entered under categories 1-4 (above) 6. Temporary legal residents under specific visa categories, such as those for foreign students, guest workers and intracompany transfers.”

**Quasi-legal categories.** “As of July 2023, about 6 million immigrants had protections. They are included in the Center's estimate of 14 million unauthorized immigrants. These protected immigrants account for about 40% of our 2023 national estimate.”

**Coverage adjustment.** “Our final estimate of the U.S. unauthorized immigrant population, as well as estimates for lawful immigrants, includes an upward adjustment. We do this because censuses and surveys tend to miss some people. Undercounts for immigrants, especially unauthorized immigrants, tend to be higher than for other groups.”

## Center for Migration Studies of New York — reference date 2024-07-01

**Citation.** Ryan Allen, Robert Warren and Jose Pacas, "Estimates of the Size and Composition of the Undocumented Immigrant Population in the U.S. in 2024", Journal on Migration and Human Security, July 16, 2026  
**URL.** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23315024261466218

**Definition.** “More than one-third of the "undocumented" (5.4 million) in these estimates are, in fact, documented and known to the federal government, including non-citizens with temporary protected and humanitarian statuses, asylum seekers, and others - select survivors of crime, human trafficking, and juveniles in need of humanitarian protection - with pending or approved immigration applications or petitions.”

**Method.** “The estimation process begins with microdata from the 2024 ACS that represent all noncitizens who arrived in the U.S. after 1981. We put this microdata through a "logical edit" process that assigns probable legal status based on an extensive assessment of the characteristics of noncitizens reported in the microdata. After identifying likely authorized noncitizens, the balance of noncitizens is considered possible undocumented immigrants.”

**Coverage adjustment.** “We increased that undercount adjustment factor to 1.42 for arrivals in 2021 to 2024, yielding undercount rates of about 29 percent (Table A1, "All other countries"). For the six countries shown in Table A1, it was necessary to use undercount rates higher than 29 percent for arrivals in 2021 to 24.”

## Migration Policy Institute — reference date 2024-07-01

**Citation.** Ariel G. Ruiz Soto, Julia Gelatt and Jennifer Van Hook, "Following Years of High Border Arrivals, the U.S. Unauthorized Immigrant Population Hits New Peak", MPI commentary, 2026  
**URL.** https://www.migrationpolicy.org/commentary/us-unauthorized-immigrant-population-new-peak

**Definition.** “MPI's definition of the unauthorized immigrant population includes those who entered the country without authorization or overstayed a visa, including individuals who hold a liminal (or "twilight") status that confers temporary protection from deportation and access to work authorization, such as Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA), humanitarian parole, or Temporary Protected Status (TPS), as well as those with a pending asylum application.”

**Quasi-legal categories.** “drawing on administrative data, MPI analysts estimated that a maximum of 6.3 million people-or 40 percent of the overall unauthorized population-held a liminal status in mid-2024 (see Table 3). Due to overlap (for example parolees who applied for TPS, asylum, or both), the number and share was probably substantially lower.”

**Coverage adjustment.** “To incorporate improvements the Census Bureau has made in how it measures newly arrived immigrants and to correct for the historic undercounts of this population, MPI adjusted its methodology and revised upwards its estimates for 2021 through 2023. As a result, MPI's revised estimate of the unauthorized population as of mid-2023 now stands at 14.7 million.”

## Center for Immigration Studies — reference date 2025-01-01

**Citation.** Steven A. Camarota and Karen Zeigler, "Foreign-Born Number and Share of U.S. Population at All-Time Highs in January 2025", CIS, March 12, 2025  
**URL.** https://cis.org/Report/ForeignBorn-Number-and-Share-US-Population-AllTime-Highs-January-2025

**Definition.** “We defined illegal immigrants as all foreign-born residents who are not naturalized citizens, permanent residents, or who have valid temporary visas. Our estimate of illegal immigration does include those with pending asylum applications and parolees.”

**Method.** “We find that the number of post-1980 immigrants grew from 28.4 million in January 2021 (our prior estimate), to 31.4 million in January 2025. The January 2025 CPS shows 46.8 million post-1980 immigrants (legal and illegal) in the country. If our estimate of the post-1980 legal population is correct, then there were 15.4 million illegal immigrants in the January 2025 CPS (46.8 million minus 31.4 million).”

**Coverage adjustment.** “In the past, we adjusted upward our CPS-based estimates of illegal immigrants by 2.25 percent to reflect those missed by the survey. Figure 8 shows our estimate for the total illegal population in January 2025 is 15.8 million”

## CBO, other foreign nationals

**Citation.** Congressional Budget Office, "The Demographic Outlook: 2026 to 2056", January 2026, Appendix B  
**URL.** https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61994

**Definition.** “The final category, other foreign nationals, consists of people in the United States who are not part of the U.S. national, LPR+, or INA nonimmigrant categories. Other foreign nationals include people in the following groups who did not later become U.S. citizens or receive LPR, asylee, or nonimmigrant status: People who entered the United States illegally; People who entered legally in a temporary status and remained after that legal status expired; and People who were permitted to enter despite not being admissible as an LPR, asylee, refugee, or nonimmigrant. That typically occurs through the use of parole authority”

**Flows.** “CBO estimates that net immigration in the OFN category averaged -130,000 people from 2010 to 2019 and then totaled 830,000 people in 2021, 2.0 million people in 2022, 2.4 million people in 2023, and 1.3 million people in 2024.”

**2025.** “CBO now estimates that 360,000 more people left the OFN category in 2025 than joined it, meaning that net immigration of other foreign nationals equaled -360,000 people in that year. That estimate is smaller than the projections for 2025 that CBO published in January and September 2025: 1.1 million people and -290,000 people, respectively.”

**2025 inflow decomposition.** “A total of 380,000 people joined the OFN category last year, CBO estimates. That total is the sum of CBO's estimates for four groups: people who entered the United States between official ports of entry and were released into the country by CBP officials (20,000), people who entered at ports of entry and were released into the country by CBP officials (60,000), people who entered without encountering a CBP official (50,000), and people who entered legally in a temporary status but overstayed their term of residency (260,000).”
