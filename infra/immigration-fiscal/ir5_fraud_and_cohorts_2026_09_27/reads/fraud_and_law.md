**Verdict:** No parent-specific measured fraud rate exists in the sources checked (GAO, DHS OIG, USCIS BFCA references, State Visa Office tables, 9 FAM, PRM). The only sampled benefit-fraud rates cover other benefits (religious workers 33%); family I-130 fraud *detections* run ~640/yr across all relationships (well under 1% of decisions); consular misrepresentation findings are ~3–6k/yr across all immigrant visas. The documentary record instead explains the Mexican IR-5 pattern legally: ~70–80% of Mexican IR-5 green cards are in-US adjustments (State consular IR-5 issuance to Mexico-born was flat at 10–12k in FY2022–24 while DHS IR-5 doubled to 63k), which the statute allows only for parents who were inspected and admitted/paroled (overstayers, border-crossing-card holders), 245(i)-grandfathered, or military-parole-in-place; entrants without inspection must leave and face a 10-year bar that their citizen child's hardship cannot waive. Fraud is not ruled out: Texas midwife birth-certificate fraud is admitted by the government, uncounted, and is a real channel for a false "citizen" petitioner.

## B. The law on who adjusts (statute and regulation text, verified 2026-09-27)

All quotes read from Cornell LII text archived in `sources/law_*.txt`.

**Parents are immediate relatives only once the citizen child is 21.** INA 201(b)(2)(A)(i), 8 U.S.C. 1151(b)(2)(A)(i): "the term 'immediate relatives' means the children, spouses, and parents of a citizen of the United States, except that, in the case of parents, such citizens shall be at least 21 years of age." [SOURCE: sources/law_8usc1151_ina201_cornell.txt; https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/8/1151]
No numerical cap applies to immediate relatives, so IR-5 flow is set by (i) the number of citizens turning 21 with a foreign parent who wants a green card and (ii) processing capacity.

**Adjustment inside the US requires lawful inspection-and-admission or parole.** INA 245(a), 8 U.S.C. 1255(a): "The status of an alien who was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States ... may be adjusted ... if (1) the alien makes an application for such adjustment, (2) the alien is eligible to receive an immigrant visa and is admissible to the United States for permanent residence, and (3) an immigrant visa is immediately available to him". [SOURCE: sources/law_8usc1255_ina245_cornell.txt]

**Immediate relatives are exempt from the status-violation bars.** INA 245(c)(2) bars "an alien (other than an immediate relative as defined in section 1151(b) of this title ...) who hereafter continues in or accepts unauthorized employment prior to filing an application for adjustment of status or who is in unlawful immigration status on the date of filing ... or who has failed ... to maintain continuously a lawful status since entry". The 245(c)(4) visa-waiver bar carries the same "(other than an immediate relative ...)" carve-out. [SOURCE: same]
Consequence: a parent who was *admitted* (tourist visa, border crossing card, any inspected entry) and then overstayed and worked without authorization can still adjust inside the US once a 21-year-old citizen child petitions. Unlawful presence after an admission does not trigger the 3/10-year bars if the person never departs, because those bars attach on departure. [INFERENCE from 212(a)(9)(B)(i) text below: both bars require that the alien "again seeks admission ... [after] departure".]

**INA 245(i) grandfathering (the only route for entrants without inspection to adjust as parents).** 8 U.S.C. 1255(i)(1): "an alien physically present in the United States — (A) who— (i) entered the United States without inspection; or (ii) is within one of the classes enumerated in subsection (c) ...; (B) who is the beneficiary ... of— (i) a petition for classification under section 1154 ... that was filed with the Attorney General on or before April 30, 2001; or (ii) an application for a labor certification ... filed ... on or before such date; and (C) who, in the case of a beneficiary of a petition ... filed after January 14, 1998, is physically present in the United States on December 21, 2000; may apply ... The Attorney General may accept such application only if the alien remits with such application a sum equalling $1,000". [SOURCE: same]
Grandfathering attaches to the beneficiary of any qualifying petition filed by 30 April 2001 (the petition could be by a different relative or employer). A Mexican parent without inspection who was not named in such a petition (or its derivative) in 2001 or earlier cannot use 245(i) for an IR-5 case filed today. [INFERENCE from text; the USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 7 Part C details grandfathering, not re-read here — `[GAP]`]

**Present-without-admission is itself a ground of inadmissibility.** INA 212(a)(6)(A)(i): "An alien present in the United States without being admitted or paroled ... is inadmissible." [SOURCE: sources/law_8usc1182_ina212_cornell.txt]

**The 3- and 10-year unlawful-presence bars.** INA 212(a)(9)(B)(i): "Any alien ... who— (I) was unlawfully present in the United States for a period of more than 180 days but less than 1 year, voluntarily departed ... and again seeks admission within 3 years ..., or (II) has been unlawfully present in the United States for one year or more, and who again seeks admission within 10 years of the date of such alien's departure or removal from the United States, is inadmissible." Time under 18 does not count (212(a)(9)(B)(iii)(I)). [SOURCE: same]

**The waiver requires hardship to a citizen/LPR spouse or parent of the immigrant; a citizen child does not qualify.** INA 212(a)(9)(B)(v): "The Attorney General has sole discretion to waive clause (i) in the case of an immigrant who is the spouse or son or daughter of a United States citizen or of an alien lawfully admitted for permanent residence, if it is established ... that the refusal of admission to such immigrant alien would result in extreme hardship to the citizen or lawfully resident spouse or parent of such alien. No court shall have jurisdiction to review a decision or action by the Attorney General regarding a waiver under this clause." [SOURCE: same]
So an IR-5 beneficiary can get the waiver only if he or she separately has a USC/LPR spouse or USC/LPR parent who would suffer extreme hardship. The petitioning 21-year-old child's hardship counts for nothing. [INFERENCE: direct reading of the text]

**Permanent bar for re-entry after >1 year unlawful presence; no family waiver.** INA 212(a)(9)(C)(i): "Any alien who— (I) has been unlawfully present in the United States for an aggregate period of more than 1 year, or (II) has been ordered removed ..., and who enters or attempts to reenter the United States without being admitted is inadmissible." (ii) Exception: does not apply to "an alien seeking admission more than 10 years after the date of the alien's last departure from the United States if, prior to the alien's reembarkation ... the Secretary of Homeland Security has consented to the alien's reapplying for admission." (iii) Waiver: only "in the case of an alien who is a VAWA self-petitioner". [SOURCE: same]
A parent who crossed, accrued a year, left, and crossed again illegally (a common circular-migration pattern pre-2008) cannot get an IR-5 green card at all until 10 years outside the US. [INFERENCE]

**Provisional (stateside) waiver I-601A uses the same qualifying relatives.** 8 CFR 212.7(e)(3): eligible if the alien "(i) Is present in the United States ...; (iii) Upon departure, would be inadmissible only under section 212(a)(9)(B)(i) of the Act ...; (iv) Has a case pending with the Department of State, based on: (A) An approved immigrant visa petition ...; (v) Will depart from the United States to obtain the immigrant visa; and (vi) Meets the requirements for a waiver provided in section 212(a)(9)(B)(v) of the Act." [SOURCE: sources/law_8cfr212.7_cornell.txt; https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/8/212.7]
It shortens the time abroad but does not widen the qualifying relatives, and it cannot cure 212(a)(9)(C) or 212(a)(6)(C)(i) fraud. [INFERENCE: (e)(3)(iii) "inadmissible only under 212(a)(9)(B)(i)"]

**Fraud ground.** INA 212(a)(6)(C)(i): "Any alien who, by fraud or willfully misrepresenting a material fact, seeks to procure (or has sought to procure or has procured) a visa, other documentation, or admission into the United States or other benefit provided under this chapter is inadmissible." Its waiver (212(i)) is likewise keyed to a USC/LPR spouse or parent (`[GAP]` quote 212(i) text). [SOURCE: sources/law_8usc1182_ina212_cornell.txt]

### Consequence for the Mexican IR-5 flow [INFERENCE from the texts above]
| Parent's history | Where the IR-5 is granted | Bar |
|---|---|---|
| Inspected entry (visa, BCC), overstayed, never left | Adjusts inside the US (245(a); 245(c) IR exemption) | none triggered while inside |
| Entered without inspection, beneficiary of a petition filed by 30 Apr 2001 | Adjusts inside the US under 245(i), $1,000 | none |
| Entered without inspection, no 245(i), never re-entered illegally | Must leave for Ciudad Juárez; 10-year bar if ≥1 yr unlawful presence after age 18; waiver only via USC/LPR spouse or parent | 212(a)(9)(B)(i)(II) |
| Re-entered illegally after >1 yr unlawful presence | Ineligible until 10 years abroad | 212(a)(9)(C), no family waiver |
| Parent living in Mexico | Consular IR-5 | none |
So a rising in-US adjustment share among Mexican IR-5 is the pattern the statute predicts if the petitioning children were born in the US to parents who entered on visas or BCCs and stayed; it is not by itself a fraud signal.

## A. Measured fraud rates (appended per source)

### A1. GAO-06-259 (March 2006), the founding benefit-fraud review — no family/parent rate
[SOURCE: GAO-06-259, https://www.gao.gov/assets/a249232.html (curl 403; fetched via Exa); archived `sources/gao_06_259_benefit_fraud_2006.txt` (text truncated at ~120k chars, footnotes not captured)]
- The only *sampled* rate: FDNS religious-worker assessment, "about 33 percent of the 220 sampled applications resulted in a preliminary finding of potential fraud" (sample from a 6-month FY2004 window; ~660 of ~2,000). Method: "FDNS first selected a statistically valid sample of applications", then verified via database checks, interviews and site visits.
- FY2005 fraud *denials* (not a rate of fraud): "USCIS denied just over 20,000 applications because USCIS staff detected fraudulent application information"; of these "application for a spouse to immigrate (14 percent)". No breakdown for parent petitions.
- Consular returns: "out of 2,400 applications returned on average each month to USCIS by the National Visa Center ... about 900 involve fraud or suspected fraud". All family and employment categories pooled; no parent split; denominator is returns, not petitions.
- Coverage gap stated by GAO: "FDNS's current plan calls for assessing benefit types that represent only about 25 percent of the applications USCIS received in fiscal year 2004". USCIS's reply lists the chosen "high risk" types (marriage, religious workers, intra-company transferees); parents are not among them.

### A2. DHS OIG-13-97 (June 2013): counts of family-based I-130 fraud denials, no rate, no parent split
[SOURCE: https://www.oig.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/assets/Mgmt/2013/OIG_13-97_Jun13.pdf; `sources/dhs_oig_13-97_family_fraud_tracking_2013.txt`, p.3–4]
- "nationwide from fiscal year (FY) 2008 through FY 2011, USCIS denied or revoked 2,557 family-based I-130 petitions for fraud. Of these petitions, 622 were associated with an FDNS finding of fraud". Also "USCIS denied or rescinded 2,961 family-based I-485 applications for fraud".
- That is ~640 fraud denials/revocations per year across all family I-130 categories (spouses, children, parents, siblings). The report gives no denominator; USCIS adjudicates several hundred thousand I-130s a year (e.g. the FY2023 Q4 performance file lists 170,156 immediate-relative approvals in one quarter — `[GAP]` not archived, only seen in a search snippet), so detected-and-recorded fraud is well under 1% of I-130 decisions. [INFERENCE; a detection count, not a prevalence estimate — undetected fraud is by construction absent]
- OIG's finding is that recording was incomplete (49% of I-130 fraud findings lacked TECS records), not a fraud prevalence.

### A4. DNA: policy exists, published failure rates do not (except the refugee P-3 pilot)
- State and USCIS may *suggest* but "may not require" DNA; consular officers "may not request DNA testing in an attempt to disprove a relationship"; parent–child results must reach 99.5%. [SOURCE: 9 FAM 601.11-1(A)–(C), https://fam.state.gov/fam/09FAM/09FAM060111.html; `sources/state_9fam_601.11_dna_excerpt.txt`] The FAM refers to annual "DNA collection volume reports" between CA and USCIS; none is published. So DNA is used only where documents are missing, and **no IR-5 or I-130 DNA failure rate has been published** (searched State FAM, PRM, USCIS; none found).
- Closest measured rate, **refugees, not IR-5**: the 2008 PRM/USCIS DNA pilot of the Priority-3 refugee family-reunification program (~500 applicants in Nairobi, then ~3,000 in seven African countries, mostly Somali, Ethiopian, Liberian): "only able to confirm all claimed biological relationships in fewer than 20% of cases (family units). In the remaining cases, at least one negative result (fraudulent relationship) was identified, or the individuals refused to be tested." Tests were among applicants, "not between the applicants and the anchor relative in the United States." [SOURCE: State PRM fact sheets 2008-12-04 https://2001-2009.state.gov/g/prm/refadm/rls/fs/2008/112760.htm and 2009-02-03 https://2009-2017.state.gov/j/prm/releases/factsheets/2009/181066.htm, 2008 sheet re-read in full via Exa fetch; excerpts archived `sources/state_prm_p3_dna_fraud_factsheet_2008-12-04.txt`]. The <20% figure counts refusals and no-shows as failures, and State never released the split (Immigration Policy Center 2010 says refusals/no-shows were "the great majority" of negatives in the 3,000 sample — secondary). Its transfer to Mexican IR-5 is weak: a different population (refugees in camps without civil registries), a different mechanism (slot brokers), and Mexican parent–child links are documented by two civil registries.

### A5. Fabricated US birth records on the Texas border (the mechanism the operator suspects, one generation earlier)
- The State Department maintains a "Suspect Birth Attendants" (SBA) list of Texas midwives "convicted of birth certificate fraud and/or who the Department has a reasonable suspicion of having engaged in birth certificate fraud"; the settlement notice says "Over the years, a large number of midwives and birth attendants filed birth certificates in Texas for people who were not born in the United States." The government's Answer admits "a number of these midwives were convicted" and that some applicants' births "were registered both in the United States and in another country." [SOURCE: Castelano v. Clinton, S.D. Tex. 7:08-cv-00057, settlement Doc 89-2 (2009) and Answer; `sources/castelano_v_clinton_settlement_excerpts.txt`]
- **No count** of convicted midwives or of false certificates appears in the documents read; the SBA list itself is withheld ("will not be disseminated in any form to Class Counsel"). The Washington Post (2008-09-09) dates the forgeries "to the 1960s" (only the lead was retrievable). `[GAP]`: DOJ/INS press releases from the 1990s Rio Grande Valley midwife prosecutions were not located in this pass.
- Relevance to IR-5: a Mexico-born child with a false Texas birth certificate would be a "US citizen" petitioner at 21. The class covers passport applications from 2003 onward and births with non-institutional midwife registration, which were concentrated in earlier decades; the SBA screening has applied to passports since then. Whether USCIS applies the same scrutiny to the *petitioner's* citizenship evidence in I-130s is not documented in what was read. Size is unknown; the tell would be IR-5 petitioners born at home in Cameron/Hidalgo/Starr/Webb counties. [INFERENCE]

## B3. Parole in place for parents of military members (a third in-US route)
USCIS: "You may be eligible for parole in place ... if you are 1 of the following service members, or are the spouse, widow(er), parent, son, or daughter of 1 of the following service members: Active-duty member ...; Individual in the Selected Reserve ...; or Individual who ... previously served ... and was not dishonorably discharged." And: "Parole in place may be granted only to individuals who are present without admission". [SOURCE: https://www.uscis.gov/military/discretionary-options-for-military-members-enlistees-and-their-families; `sources/uscis_military_parole_in_place_excerpt.txt`]
A parole grant satisfies 245(a) ("inspected and admitted or paroled"), so an entrant-without-inspection parent of a US-born service member or veteran can adjust as IR-5 without leaving. [INFERENCE from 245(a) text] No count of military PIP grants to Mexican parents was found — `[GAP]`. The general 2024 "Keeping Families Together" parole-in-place for spouses was for spouses only and was vacated by a federal court (training knowledge, not verified here — `[UNVERIFIED]`); it does not bear on IR-5 parents.

## C. Disconfirmation, both directions
What would show fraud, and what the documents show:
- **Implausible parent ages.** The sister lane's DHS profiles give Mexican IR-5 counts but not parent age by class; the age columns in `ir5_flow.csv` are for all Mexican LPRs. No published source with IR-5 parent ages by country was found. `[GAP]` for the parent lane (the birth-cohort test is the stronger check).
- **DNA failure.** No IR-5 or I-130 DNA failure rate is published (A4). The only measured DNA rate is the African refugee P-3 pilot, which does not transfer.
- **OIG/GAO findings specific to IR-5.** None found. GAO-06-259 and OIG-13-97 treat family petitions as one bucket, and USCIS's assessment program chose marriage, religious worker and intracompany-transferee benefits as "high risk", not parents (A1, A2). The absence of an assessment is not evidence of low fraud; it means the rate is unmeasured.
- **Evidence against the fraud reading.** (1) The statute makes the in-US adjustment share a function of entry mode, not fraud: overstayers and BCC holders adjust; entrants without inspection mostly cannot (B). (2) Mexican IR-5 consular issuance is flat while adjustments surge after FY2022, the same years USCIS worked down its adjustment backlog; a fraud wave would not be confined to one processing channel's catch-up. [INFERENCE] (3) Adjustment requires USCIS to verify the lawful entry (I-94/CBP records) or the 2001-or-earlier 245(i) petition in its own files; a forged parent-child link alone does not open the adjustment door for a parent who entered without inspection. [INFERENCE from 245(a)/(i)]
- **Evidence for a fraud channel.** Texas midwife birth-certificate fraud is admitted in federal court, involved convictions, and produced dual US/Mexican birth registrations; it creates false *petitioners*, not false parent links (A5). Its scale is uncounted in the documents read. If it mattered for IR-5, petitioners would cluster among home births registered in Rio Grande Valley counties 1960s–1990s; for FY2019–24 petitioners (born ≤2003) that window overlaps. [INFERENCE]
