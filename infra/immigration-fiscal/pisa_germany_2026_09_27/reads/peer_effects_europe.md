claude-opus-5-5
**Verdict:** Only Brunello & Rocco identify from country-level change on a cross-year-linked absolute scale (PISA): −2.75% of natives' score, about −0.14 SD per 10 pp (SE ±0.135 on −0.275; t≈2). The seven within-school or within-institution designs find zero to −0.05 SD on relative scales, or −0.16 to −0.30 school-level SD in Ballatore et al.'s rule-based class-level IV. None of them can detect a system-wide level effect.

# European immigrant-peer-effect studies: design, outcome scale, effect on natives

Sections are in the brief's numbering. Two citation corrections: Tonello is *Empirical Economics* and Frattini & Meschi is *European Economic Review* (both Crossref-verified). Scope: eight studies (Brunello & Rocco 2013; Schneeweis 2015; Jensen & Rasmussen 2011; Ohinata & van Ours 2013; Geay, McNally & Telhaj 2013; Ballatore, Fort & Ichino 2018; Tonello 2016; Frattini & Meschi 2019). For each: data, design and comparison level, outcome scale (ABSOLUTE vs RELATIVE), headline native effect verbatim, conversion per 10 pp [CALCULATION], heterogeneity.

Retrieval date: 2026-09-27. Routes: working-paper PDFs via curl (browser UA) + pdftotext, pdfinfo Title checked.

## 1. Brunello & Rocco (Economics of Education Review 2013, 32(1):234–246; IZA DP 5479)

**Text read:** IZA DP 5479, February 2011, 27 pp., `https://docs.iza.org/dp5479.pdf` (pdfinfo Title matches: "The Effect of Immigration on the School Performance of Natives: Cross Country Evidence Using PISA Test Scores"). Page numbers below are the DP's printed page numbers. The journal version was not read; numbers could differ in the published version [GAP].

**Country and data.** 27 countries ("mainly from Europe and the Anglo-Saxon world"), PISA 2000, 2003, 2006, 2009, aggregated to country × wave × subject: "we obtain a final sample of 238 observations" (p. 10) [SOURCE: IZA DP 5479 p. 10]. Immigrant = "pupils born abroad from two foreign parents" (p. 8). Mean share "4.8 percent, with a standard deviation equal to 0.035. While two thirds of the total variation in this share occurs between countries, one third takes place within countries and over time" (p. 10). Germany's first-generation share is 0.065, 0.145 on the broader definition that adds the second generation (Table 1, p. 18).

**Design and level of comparison.** Between countries over time, with country fixed effects: the effect comes from within-country changes across PISA waves. "We address sorting within countries by aggregating at the country level … we control for between - country migration flows by conditioning on country fixed effects and on the stock of immigrants" (p. 3). The identifying assumption is that "conditional on this stock, the share of immigrant pupils … is mainly determined by demographic factors and is as good as random" (p. 6). Measurement error is handled with a split-sample IV (Aydemir–Borjas; "use the immigrant share in the second-half sample as instrument for the immigrant share of the first-half sample … repeated 500 times", p. 12). Standard errors cluster by country × time.

**Outcome: ABSOLUTE.** The outcome is the log of the country-mean PISA score for natives. The authors keep a domain only from the wave in which it was the major domain onward, to "enhance the comparability over time for each domain" (p. 8): reading 2000–2009, maths 2003–2009, science 2006–2009. This is the only study of the eight whose outcome sits on a scale linked across years.

**Headline effect on natives.** Table 2, col. (1), p. 19, dependent variable log test score: "Share of immigrant pupils (marginal effect) -0.275** (0.135)". By group: males −0.255* (0.145); females −0.326** (0.141); good parental background −0.171 (0.123); poor parental background −0.409*** (0.155). The text reads this as "a one percentage point increase in the share of immigrant students is expected to reduce the average test scores of natives by 0.275 percent in the full sample" (p. 11). The IV averages over 500 replications are −0.281 [0.153] for all natives and −0.422 [0.178] for poor-background natives (Table 4, p. 21). The corrected-share OLS is −0.238* (0.133) (Table 3, p. 20).

**Per 10 pp [CALCULATION].** Δln(score) = −0.275 × 0.10 = −0.0275, a fall of 2.75% (95% CI from ±1.96 × 0.135: −0.8% to −5.4%). At the sample's country-mean scores of roughly 455–549 (Table 1, p. 18), 2.75% is −12.5 to −15.1 PISA points; at 500 it is −13.8 points. The paper gives no SD. On the PISA reporting convention of an OECD SD of 100 points [TRAINING-DATA: PISA scale, OECD mean 500 / SD 100 in each domain's base year], −13.8 points ≈ **−0.14 SD**. For poor-background natives: −0.409 × 0.10 = −4.1%, about −20 points or about −0.20 SD. For good-background natives: −1.7%, not significant. Cross-check with the abstract: 0.275 × 4.8 = 1.32%, matching "reduce the test score of natives by 1.32" (abstract).

**Heterogeneity the authors stress.**
- The effect falls mostly on poor-background natives. The good-vs-poor difference has "p-value … equal to 0.000" (fn. 12, p. 11).
- The effect is larger where segregation is higher: "about 0.5 percent in Latvia, Czech Republic and Mexico … about 0.3 percent in Switzerland, Russia and New Zealand" per extra point (p. 13).
- Adding the second generation to the definition "changes only slightly the marginal effect" (p. 14).

**Caveats.**
- The identifying variation is within-country change across 4 waves (one-third of the share's variance), estimated from 238 cells. The SE is large: the all-natives t-statistic is ≈2.0.
- The authors test attenuation: "attenuation bias is a minor problem in the current context" (p. 12).
- Steel-man for a causal reading: country fixed effects remove the fixed differences between countries. Against it: a time-varying country shock that moves both immigrant inflow and scores (for example the business cycle or a school reform) survives, controlled only through GDP, GDP growth, spending and the migrant stock.
- IZA World of Labor's summary of this study calls the sample "a small, non-representative sample of 19 countries" [SOURCE: https://wol.iza.org/articles/immigrants-in-classroom-and-effects-on-native-children/long]. The DP says 27 countries. That reviewer summary may describe the journal version's sample [GAP: journal-version country count unverified].

## 2. Schneeweis (Labour Economics 2015, 35:63–76; IZA DP 7230 / NRN WP 1302)

**Text read:** IZA DP 7230, February 2013, 42 pp., `https://docs.iza.org/dp7230.pdf` (pdfinfo Title matches). Page numbers are the DP's printed page numbers. The econstor copy of NRN WP 1302 returned HTML, not a PDF. The journal version was not read [GAP]. The journal abstract (ScienceDirect, via Exa) confirms the same conclusion: "native students are not affected by the share of migrant students" [SOURCE: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927537115000305].

**Country and data.** Austria, one city: "Students in Linz" (p. 11). School register data cover "22 school cohorts" from 1980 to 2001. Track choice in grade 5 is observed for "21 cohorts in 47 schools", N = 29,979, of whom 27,082 are natives (Table 1, p. 31). Migration background comes from native language and citizenship (§7.1). The mean first-grade migrant share (Fshare) is 0.096 with SD 0.126 (Table 1). Natives attend schools with a mean share of 0.079 and migrants 0.263.

**Design and level of comparison.** Within school, across adjacent cohorts. The design uses school and cohort fixed effects plus school-specific linear, quadratic or cubic trends: "The identification strategy is based on variation in ethnic grade-composition of adjacent cohorts within schools" (p. 14). The peer group is the grade, not the class, because class assignment may be non-random (p. 14). A robustness check uses a Hoxby-style IV, "the deviations from these trends … to instrument for the share of foreign students" (p. 25–26; Table 7 Panel B).

**Outcome: RELATIVE.** The outcomes are high-track attendance in grades 5–8 and grade repetition, both linear probability models. They are attainment and placement outcomes, not test scores. Track attendance is rationed and relative to the local system. It cannot register a system-wide change in skill.

**Headline effect on natives.** Table 2 Panel A, last three columns, p. 32. "Native*Fshare" on grade-5 high track is −0.018 (0.058) with a linear trend, −0.045 (0.060) quadratic and −0.030 (0.062) cubic. On grade-8 high track (Panel B): 0.038 (0.070), −0.043 (0.071), −0.039 (0.073). Grade repetition (Table 3, p. 33) is 0.020 (0.024), 0.026 (0.026) and 0.011 (0.026) in primary, and 0.007 (0.036), 0.009 (0.039) and 0.011 (0.041) in secondary. The 2SLS grade-5 high-track estimate is −0.037 (0.065) (Table 7 Panel B, p. 37). The author's summary: "While there is no significant effect for native students, migrant students suffer from a higher share of foreign students in the grade" (p. 16).

**Per 10 pp [CALCULATION].**
- Grade-5 high track: coefficient × 0.10 = −0.2 to −0.45 pp for natives, against a native mean of 0.460 (Table 1). The lower 95% bound for the quadratic trend is (−0.045 − 1.96 × 0.060) × 0.10 = −1.6 pp.
- Primary repetition: +0.1 to +0.3 pp against a native mean of 0.037. The upper 95% bound is (0.026 + 1.96 × 0.026) × 0.10 = +0.77 pp.
- Migrant students, for contrast: −0.157 to −0.203 per unit share, which is −1.6 to −2.0 pp per 10 pp. The author's reading: "an increase in the share of foreign students by around 10 percentage-points reduces the probability of choosing a high track school type for migrant students in grade 5 by 1.6-2 percentage-points" (p. 16).
- No SD conversion applies to binary outcomes.

**Heterogeneity.** By gender: "native females are negatively affected by a higher share of foreign students with respect to grade repetition in primary school. An increase in the share of students with migration background by 0.1 (one standard deviation) increases the probability of repeating a grade by around 0.5 percentage-points for them" (p. 22). Native boys gain on grade-8 track from a larger foreign male peer group (p. 21–22), with gender differences "significant at the 10-percent level" (p. 22). Own-ethnic-group shares drive the migrant spillovers (§6.2). Panel B of Table 4 finds concave non-linearity for migrants, with no native effect.

**Caveats.** The city's migrant share rose from under 5% in the 1980s to about 22% by 2001 (p. 11). The school-specific trends absorb that secular rise, so the estimate uses only deviations around each school's trend. By construction it cannot see a city-wide level effect. Tracking in Linz is also partly capacity-constrained, so a relative placement outcome can hold constant while absolute skill moves [INFERENCE].

## 4. Ohinata & van Ours (Economic Journal 2013, 123(570):F308–F331; IZA DP 6212)

**Text read:** IZA DP 6212, December 2011, 35 pp., `https://docs.iza.org/dp6212.pdf` (pdfinfo Title matches). Also circulated as CentER DP 2011-136, CEPR DP 8718 and NORFACE DP 2012-12. **Citation correction:** IZA DP 5694 and 6111 are other papers (checked by pdfinfo). The EJ version was not read [GAP].

**Country and data.** Netherlands, 4th grade (age about 9–10). PIRLS 2001 and 2006 cover reading; TIMSS 1995 and 2007 cover maths and science (p. 11). Samples are small: "Number of schools 24 / 32 / 103 / 26" and 513, 810, 3,107 and 557 native students (Table 4, p. 28). Immigrant share counts first- and second-generation children (Table 2, Table 7).

**Design and level of comparison.** Within school, across classes of the same grade, with school fixed effects. There is no rule-based or random assignment; the paper assumes it. "The main identification assumption requires that once school specific characteristics are controlled for, students are allocated to each class randomly within a school" (p. 15). The authors test for it: in Table 3 (p. 27), class and teacher characteristics are regressed on the immigrant share, and they "do not find any evidence that teaching resources are allocated favorably to classes with high shares of immigrant students" (p. 15). Each survey year is estimated separately.

**Outcome.** The scale is linked across cycles, but the estimate is relative. The outcome is PIRLS/TIMSS plausible-value scores in scale points. Table 4's coefficients (for example "Books at home: More than 200 27.83***") are in points. The Table 5 note calls the dependent variables "standardized test scores". This clash sits inside the DP [GAP]. The IEA scales are linked across cycles [TRAINING-DATA]. Identification, however, compares classes within a school within one year, so the result carries no cross-year or cross-system comparison.

**Headline effect on natives.** Table 4 row "% of immigrants in class" (p. 28), with school FE, one regression per wave:

| Subject, year | Coefficient (points per 1 pp) | SE | Standardized coefficient (paper) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reading 2001 | 0.40 | (0.29) | 0.012 |
| Reading 2006 | −0.13 | (0.28) | −0.003 |
| Science 1995 | 0.24 | (0.38) | 0.003 |
| Science 2007 | −0.31 | (0.33) | −0.01 |
| Maths 1995 | 1.54*** | (0.58) | 0.023 |
| Maths 2007 | −0.23 | (0.47) | −0.007 |

The text: "1 percent increase in the proportion of immigrant students in class positively affects the average Dutch students' reading plausible score by 0.4 point (column (1)). This is approximately a 0.012 standard deviation increase for one standard deviation increase in the percentage of immigrant students" (p. 16). Without school FE (Table 5 row b), reading 2001 is −0.95*** (0.16). The negative cross-school correlation therefore disappears within schools.

**Per 10 pp [CALCULATION].** The coefficient × 10 gives: reading +4.0 (2001) and −1.3 (2006) points; science +2.4 and −3.1; maths +15.4 and −2.3. The widest adverse 95% bound is maths 2007: (−0.23 − 1.96 × 0.47) × 10 = −11.5 points. The most precise, reading 2006, spans (−0.13 ± 0.55) × 10 = −6.8 to +4.2 points. The paper gives no SD for scores. On the IEA reporting SD of 100 [TRAINING-DATA: PIRLS/TIMSS international metric mean 500, SD 100], the point estimates are **−0.03 to +0.15 SD per 10 pp**, mostly within ±0.04 SD. The paper's standardized coefficients refer to 1 SD of the class share, which the DP does not print. They cannot be converted to per-10-pp without that SD [GAP].

**Heterogeneity.**
- First-generation-only shares, and first-generation children who arrived after age 5, are all insignificant (Table 5 rows c–d).
- The non-Western share is insignificant (row e).
- Excluding schools that group classes by ability raises reading 2001 to 0.80*** (0.24) (row h), a positive coefficient.
- The spillover falls on immigrant peers in reading: −1.47* and −2.08** (row j), "Immigrant children themselves experience negative language spill-over effects" (abstract).

**Caveats.**
- Power is low: 24–32 schools for reading.
- Across-class variation within a school is small, and PIRLS/TIMSS sample only one or two classes per school.
- The authors expect the bias to be positive: "participating schools were allowed to exclude some students with severe linguistic problems … Our estimates are, therefore, likely to be positively biased" (p. 12–13).

## 5. Geay, McNally & Telhaj (Economic Journal 2013, 123(570):F281–F307; IZA DP 6451)

**Text read:** IZA DP 6451, March 2012, 43 pp., `https://docs.iza.org/dp6451.pdf` (pdfinfo Title matches). The EJ version was not read [GAP].

**Country and data.** England. National Pupil Database, census of state primary schools, Year 6 (age 11). Cohorts run 2003–2009; N ≈ 2.14 million native English speakers in 11,879 schools (Table 4 notes, p. 32). The treatment is language, not immigrant status: "'native English speaker' is defined by whether the person speaks English as a first language" (p. 8). The non-native share of pupils rose "from 8.7% in 2003 to 12.4% in 2009" (p. 8).

**Design and level of comparison.**
- **(a) Main estimate: within school, across adjacent cohorts** (Hoxby 2000). The specification carries school fixed effects, age-7 prior attainment, time-varying school controls and school-specific trends. "leaving only idiosyncratic variation in the percentage of non-native English speakers within the same school across cohorts" (p. 5). Natives are assigned to their age-7 school to avoid endogenous moves.
- **(b) IV:** Catholic school × post-2005 trend. This uses the 2004 EU enlargement shock, since Polish families favour Catholic schools. It instruments the share of *white* non-native speakers (eq. 2, p. 13). It is "a Local Average Treatment Effect … for native English speakers who attend Catholic schools" (p. 14).

**Outcome: RELATIVE.** The outcome is the Key Stage 2 "percentile score in reading, writing or maths" (p. 12). Natives' mean is 49.75 (SD 28.44) in reading (Table 2, p. 30), consistent with a national percentile rank. The DP does not state in words that ranks are computed within each year [INFERENCE: within-cohort rank, since the native mean is about 50 in every specification]. A percentile rank is relative by construction: a nationwide shift in achievement leaves it unchanged.

**Headline effect on natives.** Table 4 (p. 32), coefficient on the % non-native speakers in the year group, in percentile points per 1 pp:
- Raw, year dummies only (col. 1): reading −0.120* (0.005); writing −0.094* (0.005); maths −0.138* (0.005).
- With school FE (col. 3): reading 0.004 (0.007); writing −0.000 (0.008); maths 0.014* (0.007).
- Full specification (col. 7): reading 0.002 (0.008); writing 0.000 (0.010); maths 0.013 (0.007).
- IV maths second stage: "1.27 (se: 0.69) … a 10 percentage point increase in the percentage of white non-native speakers would increase the average attainment of native English speakers by about 1 percentile point (although this effect is only significant at the 10 per cent level)" (p. 23). The reading and writing reduced forms are "very imprecisely estimated … not informative" (p. 22).
- The abstract: "our results suggest zero effect and rule out negative effects".

**Per 10 pp [CALCULATION].**
- Raw: −0.120 × 10 = −1.2 percentile points in reading, matching the text "1.2, 0.9 and 1.4 percentile points" (p. 15).
- Within school (col. 7): reading +0.02 percentiles (95% CI (0.002 ± 1.96 × 0.008) × 10 = −0.14 to +0.18); maths +0.13 (CI −0.01 to +0.27).
- In SD: dividing by the percentile SD of 28.44 (Table 2) gives reading +0.001 "percentile-SD" (CI −0.005 to +0.006). Near the median, 1 percentile point is about 0.025 SD of a normal score [CALCULATION: 1/(100 × φ(0)) = 1/39.9]. So −0.14 percentiles is about **−0.004 SD at the adverse bound**, and the effect is statistically indistinguishable from zero at about ±0.005 SD per 10 pp.
- Raw association: −1.2 percentiles ≈ −0.03 SD per 10 pp.

**Heterogeneity.**
- Free-school-meal natives are "even less affected".
- Bottom-quintile age-7 natives: in the full specification, "a 10 percentage point increase in the percentage of non-white non-native speakers is associated with a reduction in the percentile score in reading and writing of about half of a percentile point. This is extremely small" (p. 18–19). That is ≈ −0.0125 SD [CALCULATION: 0.5 × 0.025].
- Non-linearity: the raw association is larger above 8% non-native, but "this falls away as soon as other controls are added" (p. 17).
- The number of languages is also null: "effects below about -0.05 can be ruled out" (p. 17).
- London (38% non-native): null with controls.

**Caveat.** This is the most precise null of the eight studies (2.1m pupils), but on a relative, within-cohort percentile scale. It rules out *relative* harm to natives within schools where the share rose faster than elsewhere. It says nothing about the national level of KS2 attainment.

## 6. Ballatore, Fort & Ichino (Journal of Labor Economics 2018, 36(4):885–921; IZA DP 8732 / CEPR DP 10341)

**Text read:** the authors' accepted-version PDF dated "February 22, 2018", 39 pp., `https://www.andreaichino.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/ichino_ballatore_fort.pdf`. It has no pdfinfo Title, but the first-page title and authors match. Its abstract matches the JOLE abstract on EconPapers ("sizable and negative (16% of a standard deviation) … first-generation … 30%") [SOURCE: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jlabec:doi:10.1086/697524]. Page numbers are the PDF's. IZA DP 8732 (Dec 2014, downloaded, Title matches) is an earlier version with different headline numbers ("-1.6% for language and math"). Cite the 2018 text.

**Country and data.** Italy, INVALSI 2009–10, "the first one in which all schools and students of the selected grades were required to take part". Grades 2 and 5 (ages about 7 and 10). Data are aggregated to school × grade: 16,100 schools in 5,749 institutions (Table 1, p. 23). Immigrant = "born from parents who are both non-Italian … regardless of whether they are born in Italy or not" (p. 5). Mean class size is **18.7** (SD 4.0), with 16.6 natives and **2.0 immigrants per class** (Table 1).

**Design and level of comparison.** Within institution (schools run by one principal), across schools, with institution × grade FE. The IV uses rule-based class formation: "rules of class formation … Maimonides-type rule in conjunction with the consequences of the interaction between February and September class splitting". Natives enrol in February and immigrants arrive later, so the number of immigrants per class is hump-shaped in predicted native class size (p. 2–3, Figs 1–2). The instruments are "a set of 15 dummies, one for each level of the theoretical number of natives in a class" (Table 2 notes). The estimand is the **Pure Ethnic Composition (PEC) effect**: swap one native for one immigrant at constant class size and constant student quality.

**Outcome: RELATIVE (non-linked).** The outcome is "the fraction of correct answers of natives in the language test", school average (Table 2 notes), from one cross-section. The paper's SD is the **school-level** SD of the native mean score, 0.10 for language and 0.11 for maths (Table 1). That is smaller than a pupil-level SD, so "% of a standard deviation" here is larger than a pupil-SD effect size would be.

**Headline effect on natives.** Table 2, p. 26, language, grades pooled:
- **Two endogenous (col. 1):** β (natives) −0.0019*** (0.0005); γ (immigrants) −0.0176** (0.0078); **PEC δ −0.0158** (0.0077)**, "roughly 16% of a standard deviation" (p. 12–13).
- **One endogenous (col. 2):** δ **−0.0085*** (0.0025)**, "about 9%" (p. 13).
- Maths PEC is −0.0176 to −0.0098, "-16% and -9%" (p. 13).
- First-generation immigrants: "between 22% and 30% of a standard deviation" (p. 17).
- Within-institution OLS benchmark, the design of Contini and Ohinata–van Ours: δ −0.0029 (p. 13–14). The authors read the gap as principals' compensating allocation.

**Instrument strength (flag).** In the preferred col. (1), "F stat (immigrants) 2.9252; SW F stat (immigrants) 2.7954" (Table 2). By the usual F > 10 rule this first stage is weak [INFERENCE]. Col. (2), the one-endogenous specification, has F = 18.0. So the precise −9% estimate is the better-identified one, and the headline −16% rests on a weak first stage.

**Per 10 pp [CALCULATION].** One swap at the mean class size of 18.7 moves the immigrant share by 1/18.7 = 5.35 pp, so 10 pp = 1.87 swaps (the paper's own mean class size).
- δ = −0.0158: −0.0158 × 1.87 = −0.0295, i.e. −3.0 points of fraction-correct = **−0.30 school-level SD** (÷ 0.10).
- δ = −0.0085: −0.0159 = **−0.16 school-level SD**.
- OLS within institution: −0.0029 × 1.87 = −0.0054 = −0.05 school-level SD.
- Linear extrapolation caveat: 10 pp here nearly doubles the mean of 2.0 immigrants per class.
- Pupil-level SD is not given in the main text [GAP]. A pupil-level effect size would be smaller, by the ratio of school-level to pupil-level SD [INFERENCE].

**Heterogeneity.** The effect is larger for first-generation immigrants, 22–30% SD per swap. Language and maths are similar in size, but maths is less precise.

**Caveat.** This design identifies a class-level composition effect *holding class size fixed*. The authors stress that the policy-relevant total effect includes principals' endogenous adjustments, which make observed effects "considerably smaller" (abstract). It does not measure a system-wide effect: with one cross-section, national shifts are absorbed.

## 7. Tonello (**Empirical Economics** 2016, 51(1):383–414)

**Citation correction.** The brief lists this paper as *Labour Economics* 2016. Crossref gives "Peer effects of non-native students on natives' educational outcomes: mechanisms and evidence", **Empirical Economics** 51, 383–414, DOI 10.1007/s00181-015-0995-y [SOURCE: api.crossref.org query].

## 8. Frattini & Meschi (**European Economic Review** 2019, 113:1–22; IZA DP 11027)

**Citation correction.** The brief lists this paper as *Labour Economics* 2019. It is in the **European Economic Review**, DOI 10.1016/j.euroecorev.2018.12.005 (ScienceDirect record S0014292118301995, found via Exa). Crossref confirms the title, journal, volume 113 and pages 1–22 [SOURCE: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2018.12.005].

**Text read:** IZA DP 11027, September 2017, 40 pp., `https://docs.iza.org/dp11027.pdf` (pdfinfo Title matches). The university repository copy of the "EER Revision 2" manuscript (air.unimi.it) returned HTML to curl. An Exa excerpt of that revision's conclusion reads: "a one standard deviation increase in classroom immigrant share (i.e. 15 percentage points) results in a 7.6% of a standard deviation reduction in mean maths scores". The DP says "(i.e. 16 percentage points) … 7.4%". The journal coefficients may differ slightly from the DP table below [GAP].

**Country and data.** Italy, Lombardy, regional **vocational** training institutions. Two cohorts entered in 2009 and 2010. "all 14,700 students (6,329 in the first and 8,365 in the second cohort)… about 18% of students are foreign born… 1,308 classes… a mean class size of 17.3, and a mean immigrant share per class of 19%" (p. 7). Immigrant = foreign-born; the second generation counts as native (p. 6).

**Design and level of comparison.** Within school, across classes and adjacent cohorts, with school FE and a value-added specification controlling for the entrance test. "Our identification strategy relies on the random variation of students across both classes and adjacent cohorts within schools" (p. 8). Balancing tests of entrance scores on class share appear in §4. There is no instrument.

**Outcome: RELATIVE.** End-of-third-year regional literacy and maths tests, "standardized to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one in each wave" (p. 7). The outcome is value-added, conditional on the standardized entrance score.

**Headline effect on natives.** Table 5.1 (p. 35), coefficient on the immigrant share (0–1) in SD units:
- Literacy: full sample 0.131 (0.164); low ability 0.052 (0.219); high ability 0.167 (0.209).
- **Maths: full sample −0.463** (0.203)**; **low ability −0.754*** (0.235)**; high ability −0.392 (0.257).
- Text: "a 10 percentage point increase in immigrant share (70% of a standard deviation) generating about a 4.6% of a standard deviation decrease in score" (p. 13). Low ability: "about a 7.5% of a standard deviation decrease" (p. 14).

**Per 10 pp [CALCULATION].** Maths: −0.463 × 0.10 = **−0.046 SD** (95% CI −0.086 to −0.007). Low-ability natives: −0.075 SD (CI −0.121 to −0.029). Literacy: +0.013 SD (CI −0.019 to +0.045). The authors' own figure is 4.6%.

**Heterogeneity and non-linearity.**
- In Table 5.2 (p. 36), only the top quintile of class immigrant share matters: maths 5th quintile −0.235*** (0.084); low-ability 4th −0.198** (0.083) and 5th −0.312*** (0.095). "negative peer effects arise only when the proportion of immigrants is high" (p. 15).
- The class share's interquartile range is "from 8% to almost 30%" (p. 14).
- Mechanism: the effect is driven by classes with high linguistic distance between immigrants and natives, and "ethnic diversity plays no role" (abstract, §5.1).

**Caveat.** Natives in vocational schools are "the lowest ability native students" (abstract), so this is an upper-end population for vulnerability. The within-wave standardization makes it blind to a region-wide level shift.

## Synthesis

- **Designs that could detect a system-wide effect.** Only Brunello & Rocco's country-level panel can. Its identifying variation is within-country change in the national immigrant share across PISA waves, measured on a scale linked across years, and it estimates about −0.14 SD per 10 pp with wide error (t ≈ 2, 238 cells). Aggregation also nets out the reallocation of students between schools.
- **Designs that cannot.** The other seven compare schools, classes or cohorts inside one system and absorb national, city or year shocks with fixed effects, school trends, or outcomes standardized within wave. They identify *relative* harm from local concentration, which ranges from zero (England, the Netherlands, Austria) to small (Italy, Denmark). Ballatore et al., who hold class size fixed, are the outlier at −0.16 to −0.30 school-level SD. None can see a level shift common to all schools [INFERENCE from the stated designs].
- **Absolute cross-year measurement of natives.** Only Brunello & Rocco measure natives on an absolute cross-year scale. Jensen & Rasmussen and Ohinata & van Ours use absolute-scale instruments (PISA, PIRLS/TIMSS), but identify from one cross-section or from within-school-year contrasts.
- **Pattern in the heterogeneity.** Harm concentrates on low-SES or low-ability natives (Brunello & Rocco, Frattini & Meschi, Geay et al.'s bottom quintile) and appears above thresholds of roughly 10–20% share (Tonello, Frattini & Meschi, Jensen & Rasmussen's >50% schools). Where tested, it tracks language distance or first-generation status more than immigrant status as such.
