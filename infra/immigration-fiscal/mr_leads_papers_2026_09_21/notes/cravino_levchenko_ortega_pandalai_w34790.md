**Verdict:** A calibrated GE simulation, not an empirical estimate — evidence level "structural model + assumed elasticities," with no standard errors, no identification, and no fiscal block whatsoever. What it establishes is an *accounting logic*: because the model is scale-free and capital fully adjusts, long-run aggregate real wages are invariant to the size of the unauthorized population (Corollary 1, p. 18), so the headline "native real wages fall 0.33%" is **pure redistribution** from natives to the immigrants who remain (+3.2% authorized, +12.2% unauthorized), not an aggregate income loss. The short-run +0.15% native gain is driven entirely by a capital share the authors themselves concede is overstated (footnote 10, p. 21: BEA data "imply a labor share of about 0.5" because self-employment income is unadjusted); at the conventional α≈0.35 the short-run native gain falls to roughly zero, and at Clemens–Lewis ϵ=1.3 it flips sign to −0.24% [INFERENCE, from their own equation p. 20 + Table 2]. For transfer to the Mexican-origin resident population the binding caveat is scope: the shock is 50% of **4.95M unauthorized workers** (Table A1, p. 50) — 3.2% of employment — while roughly half the unauthorized *population* is reclassified into the model's "authorized" category (Appendix A.1, p. 44), and the model's "natives" category **includes naturalized citizens** (p. 7), so most Mexican-origin US residents sit on the *losing* side of this simulation, not the removed side. The paper never reports a Mexican share of any quantity, and its conclusion explicitly disclaims fiscal relevance.

## Question, data, sample

Counterfactual question: what happens to US wages and relative prices if 50% of unauthorized workers are removed? No historical episode is studied.

- **Microdata:** 2023 ACS via IPUMS (Ruggles et al. 2015) — "At the time of writing, the 2023 ACS is the most recent available wave" (fn. 8, p. 7). Sample ages 16–67; wage-bill shares restrict further to employed with positive earnings, **excluding the self-employed and retirees** (p. 7).
- **Reweighting/projection:** re-weighted for 2020 Census undercount per Khubba, Heim & Hong (2022), "which enlarges the total immigrant population by about 1 million (to 47.1 million)" (p. 43); then weights adjusted upward using border-crossing, asylum and parole data to be representative of **fiscal-year-2024** (p. 44).
- **Production/trade data:** BEA Input-Output Make and Use tables for **2023**; Commodity Flow Survey for intra-US trade; Census state sectoral trade; BEA state GDP and PCE; OECD ICIO for US–ROW (p. 22).
- **Expenditure heterogeneity:** US Consumer Expenditure Survey, shares by 5% income ventile (p. 32).
- **Unit:** 48 regions (47 US "states" + rest of world; DE-NJ, DC-MD, AK-HI, NH-VT merged, fn. 12), 44 sectors (NAICS 3-digit, Table A2), 36 occupations (2018 Census classification, Table A1).

## Identification

**None — there is no identification section, no regression, no instrument, no first stage, no standard errors, no pre-trends, no placebos.** The shift-share literature the brief asks about (Goldsmith-Pinkham–Sorkin–Swift, Borusyak–Hull–Jaravel, Adão–Kolesár–Morales, Jaeger–Ruist–Stuhler) is **not cited and not relevant** to this design. All elasticities are imported from other papers (Table 1, p. 24). The only "validation" is internal: the analytical formulas of Propositions 1–2 reproduce the full model's numbers (e.g. fn. 6, p. 5: "0.25×0.2×0.5×ln (0.5) ≃ 0.017" vs. the model's 1.64%).

Solution method: Dekle–Eaton–Kortum (2008) exact-hat algebra, so results depend only on observed shares and assumed elasticities (p. 21).

## Heterogeneity and mechanisms

**Two opposing forces** (pp. 3–4, formalized pp. 19–21):
1. *Relative-supply / imperfect substitution* (ξᵣ ≈ 0.35–0.36): removing immigrants makes natives relatively more abundant → native relative wage falls. Operates at **all** horizons.
2. *Capital deepening* (αᵣ ≈ 0.5): a smaller labor force raises K/L → **all** wages rise. Operates **only** in the short run; capital is decumulated to restore the steady-state K/L.

Closed forms: short run `w^N_r1 − p_r1 = [ξᵣ − αᵣ] λᵁᵣ hᵁ₁` (p. 20); long run `w^N_r∞ − p_r∞ = ξᵣ λᵁᵣ ξˡᵣ hᵁ∞` (eq. 20, p. 18). The sign of the short-run native effect is **decided by whether ξᵣ ≶ αᵣ** — 0.36 vs 0.50 in the baseline.

Occupational reversal: natives *gain* in immigrant-intensive occupations when ϵ is high relative to θ and η̄ᵣ — "if natives and immigrants are close enough substitutes, but occupations are not, then a decline in the mass of immigrants can increase the demand for natives in occupations that initially use a lot of foreign workers" (p. 19).

Muted margins: labor-force participation and internal migration barely matter. "our calibration implies a range of ξˡᵣ from 0.95 to 0.97" (p. 20) — i.e. regional relative labor supply moves essentially one-for-one with the national shock.

Why prices move so little: the relative-price effect is the product of three small numbers — unauthorized share of the sector wage bill (0.25 in Farms) × labor share of gross output (0.2 in Farms) × macro elasticity ξᵣₛ (≈0.5). Trade across states and with ROW further muffles consumer prices relative to producer prices (p. 30).

## Threats the authors do not address

- [INFERENCE] **The short-run headline is not robust to the authors' own footnote.** With α=0.35 (a self-employment-adjusted labor share of 0.65) instead of 0.50, `ξᵣ − αᵣ = 0.36 − 0.35 ≈ 0.01`, and the +0.15% native gain collapses to roughly **−0.01%**. The entire "deportation raises native wages in the short run" result rests on a capital share footnote 10 says is overstated.
- [INFERENCE] **The short-run native gain flips sign under Clemens–Lewis.** At ϵ=1.3, Table 2 gives ξᵣ=0.75 > αᵣ=0.5, so `[ξᵣ − αᵣ]λᵁᵣhᵁ` ≈ **−0.24%**: natives lose in the short run *and* the long run. The paper flags the ϵ sensitivity only for the Farming occupation, never for the aggregate short-run number.
- [INFERENCE] **Implied sensitivity band of the headline long-run number**, scaling −0.33% by ξᵣ: ϵ=4.6 → **−0.23%**; ϵ=1.3 → **−0.69%**. A factor of 3.
- [INFERENCE] **"Housing congestion" is claimed but not modelled.** P. 5 says a contribution is understanding "how changes in the unauthorized population affects capital and housing congestion." There is no land, no fixed factor and no housing sector in the model — Real Estate (NAICS 531) is an ordinary produced, capital-intensive sector. The immigration-and-rents channel, which is where a native cost-of-living loss from immigration would actually show up, is absent by construction.
- [INFERENCE] **Capitalists' income is never reported.** Natives own the capital, and long-run decumulation cuts native capital income, yet only the wage side is shown. A native welfare number would need both; the paper offers no aggregate native real-income statistic at all.
- [INFERENCE] **No productivity, scale, agglomeration, innovation or entrepreneurship channel.** The model is scale-free by assumption (Corollary 1), which *forces* the long-run aggregate real wage to zero. Any positive-scale-effect literature is assumed away rather than tested.
- [INFERENCE] **Removal is uniform across regions, sectors and occupations** (p. 23). Actual enforcement is highly selective; a selective shock would produce far larger sectoral price effects than the 1–1.6% reported.
- [INFERENCE] **Zero labor-demand disruption, zero adjustment cost.** Firms reoptimize instantly; there is no firm exit, no hiring friction, no capital that is sector-specific.
- [INFERENCE] **The legal-status imputation is unvalidated.** It is a residual method (Warren–Warren / Passel–Cohn / Connor 2024) with no reported accuracy against any ground truth; the "narrower definition" moves ~1.8pp of employment (5% → 3.2%, fn. 3, p. 2) into the authorized category, which mechanically shrinks the shock.

## Answers to the repo's questions

**1. Model structure.** 48 regions (47 US + ROW), 44 NAICS-3 sectors, 36 Census occupations (pp. 21–22). Three US worker types — natives (US-born **or naturalized citizens**), authorized immigrants (LPR, H-1B/F-1/J-1 etc., **plus DACA, TPS, parole, asylum-seekers**), unauthorized (p. 7). No skill/education groups; heterogeneity is by occupation, not skill. Nesting: occupations combine via CES η, then each occupation nests natives vs. foreign via ϵ, and foreign nests authorized vs. unauthorized via σ (pp. 14–15). Elasticities (Table 1, p. 24): **η=1.6** (Burstein et al. 2020), **ϵ=3** (midpoint of Burstein 4.6 and Clemens–Lewis 1.3), **σ=14** (Borjas–Cassidy 2019), **θ=1** occupational supply (Burstein–Morales–Vogel 2019), **ν=1.5** migration (Fajgelbaum et al. 2019), **ψ=0.3** participation (Chetty et al. 2011), **χ=2** Armington (Boehm–Levchenko–Pandalai-Nayar 2023), β=0.95, δ=0.05. Capital: owned by a regional "capitalist" who invests; **fixed at t=1 (short run), fully endogenous and decumulated to restore the pre-shock K/L in the long run** (K_{rt+1}=I_rt+[1−δ]K_rt, p. 13). Trade: iceberg costs across states and with ROW, χ=2, built from CFS + gravity imputation for non-CFS tradeables; non-tradeable sectors have production = local purchases by assumption (pp. 22–23).

**2. The long-run mechanism — and the neutrality result is intact.** Your framing needs inverting: neutrality is **not broken**. Corollary 1 (p. 18) states aggregate real wages return exactly to the pre-shock level — "the economy is scale-free, and… following a deportation event capital gets decumulated until the capital-labor ratio returns to its pre-shock steady state level." What falls is the *native relative* wage, and the sole mechanism is **imperfect substitution between natives and immigrants (finite ϵ)**: "Following a decline in the mass of undocumented households (hᵁ∞ < 0), the long-run average real wages of natives w^N_r∞ − p_r∞ decrease, while average real wages of immigrants w^U_r∞ − p_r∞ increase in all regions. **The intuition is that the relative supply of natives increases, reducing their wage**" (p. 19). And explicitly: "if natives and immigrants are perfect substitutes (ϵ → ∞), removing immigrants has no impact on relative wages" (p. 19). Sector specificity, scale effects, housing and land play **no** role. [INFERENCE] Arithmetic confirmation that this is redistribution and not loss: with wage-bill weights λ^N=0.942, λ^A=0.044, λᵁ=0.014, the weighted sum 0.942(−0.33) + 0.044(3.21) + 0.014(12.24) ≈ 0.

**3. Counts (Table A1, p. 50, "All occupations" row, units = millions of workers).** Natives **138.87M**; foreign workers **15.55M**; foreign authorized **10.60M**; foreign unauthorized **4.95M**; foreign share 0.10. Checks: 4.95/154.42 = 3.2% ✓, 10.60/154.42 = 6.9% ✓. Total foreign-born population (incl. naturalized) after undercount reweighting = **47.1M** (p. 43); naturalized ≈ 25M (fn. 2). "in 2024 the protected population makes up nearly half of the total unauthorized population" (p. 44). Source chain: **2023 ACS via IPUMS**, Connor (2024) method built on Warren & Warren (2013) / Passel & Cohn (2014) / Borjas & Cassidy (2019), reweighted per Khubba–Heim–Hong (2022), projected to FY2024 with DHS border/asylum/parole data — **not** CMS, Pew or MPI directly (Pew is cited only for the growth claim). **Removed in the 50% scenario: 2.475M employed unauthorized workers, stated in the paper as "about 3.7 million" (pp. 4, 23) and "approximately 2.5 million" (p. 36).** Farming occupation detail: natives 0.529M, foreign 1.063M, of which unauthorized 0.741M (46.5% of that occupation). **Mexican share: NOT REPORTED anywhere.** The word "Mexican" appears exactly once outside the state name and references — in the DACA-assignment targeting step (p. 43).

**4. Dollar magnitudes: NONE.** No aggregate native income, no GDP, no GDP per capita, no income per state, no income by skill group — the paper reports only percentage changes. Verified by exhaustive grep for "million/billion/GDP/dollar/income"; the only dollar figure in the paper is the "$75 billion" ICE appropriation in the intro (p. 2). Distribution across states: −0.2% (ND/ME/SD/MI) to −0.49% (CA/WA/DE-NJ/TX) long run. Distribution across **skill** groups: not available — there are no skill groups. **Across occupations**, the closest proxy: the highest-unauthorized-intensity occupations gain (Farming +3.44%, Construction Roof/Steel +0.86%) while 23 of 36 occupations lose, worst being "Other Personal Services, Health, Social and Media, and Business, Finance, and Legal" (p. 26). So the *low-skill-proxy* occupations that directly compete with the removed workers gain, and the high-skill occupations lose — but this ranking is the elasticity-dependent result the authors warn about (p. 35).

**5. Government/fiscal block: NO.** Section 3.3 (p. 14) is the entire government: "There is a government that makes exogenous purchases/requisitions of the aggregate sectoral goods in each region G_rst. These purchases that are in zero world net supply in revenue terms, ∑ᵣ P_rst G_rst = 0 ∀s. As will be clear in our calibration section, the G_rst's are **wedges required to perfectly match the observed regional variation in sectoral absorption** in the data as well as the observed sectoral trade deficits and surpluses." No taxes, no transfers, no public services, no budget constraint, no financing. Only private incomes and prices. The conclusion says so: the analysis "does not speak to other important aspects of migration policies, such as their fiscal, political, and humanitarian consequences" (p. 36).

**6. Price effects by sector** — see the Main-estimates table. Long-run relative **consumer** prices: Farms **+1.17%**, Forestry & Fishing **+1.00%**, everything else **−0.23% to +0.37%**. Long-run relative **producer** prices: Farms +1.64%, Forestry & Fishing +1.42%, Construction and Food & Beverages Mfg reaching 0.8–0.9% *in some regions only*. Short-run outlier: Real Estate **−0.35%** (capital-intensive, so cheaper when K/L rises). Within-California consumer prices: Farms +3.01%, Forestry & Fishing +2.57%.

**7. Sensitivity of the headline numbers.** The paper reports sensitivity only of the *composite elasticities* (Table 2), not of the headline percentages. Applying its own linear formulas [INFERENCE]: long-run national native wage **−0.23% (ϵ=4.6) to −0.69% (ϵ=1.3)** vs. −0.33% baseline; short-run national native wage **+0.24% (ϵ=4.6) to −0.24% (ϵ=1.3)** — *the short-run sign is not determined by the data*. Sectoral relative prices scale with ξᵣₛ ∈ [0.31, 0.77] vs. 0.52 baseline, so Farms consumer prices span roughly **0.7%–1.7%**. θ is immaterial for ξᵣ and matters modestly for ξᵣₛ. ν and ψ are immaterial (ξˡᵣ ∈ [0.93, 1.00]). χ=7 vs 2 leaves wages unchanged and cuts Food prices from 1.2% to 1.0% (p. 35).

**8. Ingredients for "native income gain per unauthorized worker present."** Calibration year: **ACS 2023 projected to FY2024; BEA IO 2023**. Native workers **138.87M**. Workers removed: **2.475M** employed (or 3.7M working-age persons — pick one and state it). Native wage change: **−0.33%** long run, **+0.15%** short run. **The paper supplies no wage bill or GDP level**, so the dollar step needs an external input. [INFERENCE] Backing the wage-bill weights out of Corollary 1 gives λ^N ≈ **0.942**, λ^A ≈ 0.044, λᵁ ≈ **0.014** (the last cross-checks against the paper's own "λᵁᵣ − λᵁᵣₛ = −0.24" with λᵁ_Farms ≈ 0.25, p. 19). Formula: `Δ native labor income = 0.942 × (US compensation of employees) × (−0.0033)`. With BEA 2024 compensation ≈ $12.0tn **[external, not from the paper]**: native wage bill ≈ $11.3tn, long-run change ≈ **−$37bn/yr**, i.e. **≈ +$15,100 per employed unauthorized worker present per year**, or **≈ +$10,100** if the 3.7M denominator is used. Short run: ≈ +$17bn/yr, i.e. −$6,850 per worker removed. Two warnings before this number travels: (a) it is a **transfer**, fully offset by the remaining immigrants' gains, not an efficiency effect — the model's aggregate real wage is unchanged by construction; (b) it excludes native capital income, which also falls as capital is decumulated.
