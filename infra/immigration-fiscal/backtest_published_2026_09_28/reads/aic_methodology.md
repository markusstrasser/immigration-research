## Q1. Population unit

- S2 (and S1, identical wording): "In 2019 alone, Mexican undocumented households earned almost [result redacted] in household income and contributed [result redacted] in federal, state, and local taxes, even assuming conservative estimates that only half of all undocumented households filed taxes. This is in addition to the [result redacted] in contributions to Social Security and [result redacted] to the Medicare Trust Fund that were made by or on behalf of employers of Mexican undocumented immigrants. After taxes, Mexican undocumented immigrants held more than [result redacted] in spending power, money that often goes back into local economies as they spend on housing, consumer goods, and services." [SOURCE: S2 https://research.newamericaneconomy.org/report/contributions-of-undocumented-immigrants-by-country/ ; S1 https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/report/contributions-of-undocumented-immigrants-by-country/]
- S2 table titles: "Table 4: Economic Contributions of Mexican Undocumented Immigrants by State, 2019" and "Table 5: Economic Contributions for the Top 5 Countries of Origin Among Undocumented Immigrants, 2019". [SOURCE: S2]
- S3: "Using the ACS 1-year microdata sample, we estimate the aggregate household income, tax contributions, and spending power of foreign-born households. A household is defined as a foreign-born household if the household head is foreign-born." [SOURCE: S3 https://www.newamericaneconomy.org/methodology/ (capture 2021-02-27)]
- S3: "The remainder of the foreign-born population that do not meet these criteria are reclassified as undocumented. Estimates regarding the economic contribution of undocumented immigrants and the role they play in various industries are made using the same methods used to capture this information for the broader immigrant population in the broader brief." [SOURCE: S3]
- S3: "Except where otherwise noted (e.g., “undocumented immigrant” or “DACA-eligible”), we define an immigrant as anyone born outside the country to non-U.S. citizen parents who is resident in the United States. This includes naturalized citizens, green card holders, temporary visa holders, refugees, asylees, and undocumented immigrants, among others." [SOURCE: S3]
- NAE's standard for payroll contributions: "When estimating Social Security and Medicare contributions, we include all individual wage earners in the households and aggregate the amount paid by state." [SOURCE: S5 Methodology Appendix, printed p. 41 (PDF page 43); same text in S6 printed p. 31 (PDF page 33)]

**Reading.**
- **Unit.** The published quantities are for households, called "Mexican undocumented households". The report switches to "Mexican undocumented immigrants" for spending power and for the payroll contributions.
- **Whose income.** Income is total household income, counting all members ("total annual household income", S3). A household enters through its head (S3); that "Mexican undocumented" refers to the household head is [INFERENCE] from S3's household rule and its "same methods" sentence. The report never defines it.
- **Status.** Naturalized citizens and immigrants coded as legal residents are excluded. The population is only the foreign-born whom the Borjas residual method codes as undocumented (criteria under Q6).
- **Payroll unit.** Under NAE's standard rule, the Social Security and Medicare columns count all wage earners in the households. That this carried over to the 2021 report is [INFERENCE].

## Q3. Federal taxes

- Table 4 columns (S2; header cells joined with " | "): "States | Household Income, in M$ | Federal income taxes, in M$ | State and Local taxes, in M$ | Spending Power, in M$ | Social Security contributions, in M$ | Medicare contributions, in M$". Table 5 columns: "Country of Origin | Total Household Income (in Millions $) | Federal Income Taxes (in Millions $) | State & Local Taxes (in Millions $) | Spending Power (in Millions $)". [SOURCE: S2]
- "For federal income tax rate estimates, we use data released by the Congressional Budget Office and calculate federal tax contributions using federal tax bracket determined with adjusted household income. [3]" Reference [3]: "Congressional Budget Office. 2020. “The Distribution of Household Income and Federal Taxes, 2017.”" [SOURCE: S3]
- The same page's DACA and TPS sections: "For federal tax rate estimates, we use data released by the Congressional Budget Office and calculate the federal tax contributions based on the CBO estimates for household federal tax incidence rates by income quintile." [SOURCE: S3]
- NAE's standard definition (S3 cites S5 for "spending power"): "As for federal tax, we used the data released along with a report from the Congressional Budget Office in 2014 and calculated the federal tax based on the federal tax bucket that they are in." [SOURCE: S5 Methodology Appendix, printed p. 41 (PDF page 43)]
- S5 body text: "As described above, the federal taxes that Asian Americans pay include contributions to Social Security and Medicare." [SOURCE: S5 PDF page 11]
- S5 appendix on payroll: "Social Security and Medicare contributions are drawn from taxes on an individual’s wage earnings. This is far different from a household’s overall income, which may include other revenue streams such as rental income and returns on investments. To account for this difference between overall federal taxes and Social Security and Medicare contributions, we estimate Medicare and Social Security contributions based on wage and salary data provided at the individual level in the ACS. For selfemployed individuals, we used the self-employment income as the income base. The amount of earnings that can be taxed by the Social Security program is capped at $117,000, while there is no such limit for the Medicare program. We used a flat tax rate of 12.4 percent to estimate Social Security contributions and 2.9 percent to capture Medicare contributions. This estimates the total amount that immigrants and their employers contributed in 2015. It is also worth noting that half of the amount contributed to Social Security and Medicare (6.4 percent [sic] of Social Security tax rate and 1.45 percent of Medicare tax rate) comes from individual workers, while the other half comes directly from their employers." (Footnote markers 47–49 omitted. Method parameters: statutory rates and the 2015 wage cap.) [SOURCE: S5 printed p. 41 (PDF page 43); same in S6 printed p. 31 (PDF page 33)]
- CBO's definitions (S8): "In this analysis, federal taxes consist of individual income taxes, payroll taxes, corporate income taxes, and excise taxes." "Income groups are created by ranking households by income before transfers and taxes, adjusted for household size." "adjusted household income is calculated as household income divided by the square root of the number of people in the household." "CBO allocates the individual income taxes and the employee’s share of payroll taxes to the households paying those taxes directly. CBO also allocates the employer’s share of payroll taxes to employees because employers appear to pass on their share of payroll taxes to employees by paying lower wages than they otherwise would." [SOURCE: S8]

**Reading.**
- **Rate source and application.** Federal taxes are CBO average rates, taken from CBO's distribution report on 2017 incomes (published 2020). Each household is placed in a CBO income group (bracket or quintile) by "adjusted household income", and that group's rate is applied to its household income. The rates vary by income group only, not by state. That "adjusted" means CBO's square-root household-size adjustment is [INFERENCE].
- **Which CBO components.** This is not stated. The column is labelled "Federal income taxes", and the report presents the federal, state and local taxes as separate from the Social Security and Medicare contributions ("This is in addition to the [result redacted] in contributions to Social Security and [result redacted] to the Medicare Trust Fund"). NAE's own standard (S5), however, says its "federal taxes" include Social Security and Medicare, which points to CBO's total federal rate: individual income, payroll (employee plus employer), corporate and excise. The sources conflict and do not settle the question.
- **Payroll columns.** Social Security and Medicare are reported separately. Under NAE's standard method they are the statutory combined employee-plus-employer rates (12.4% to the wage cap, and 2.9%) applied to wage and self-employment income. S2's wording "made by or on behalf of employers" is consistent with including the employer share. Carrying the S5 method over to the 2021 report is [INFERENCE].
- **Headline total.** [CALCULATION on S2's cached table cells; no figure reproduced] The headline "federal, state, and local taxes" total equals Table 4's "Federal income taxes" plus "State and Local taxes" in the United States row, to rounding. It excludes the Social Security and Medicare columns.

## Q4. State and local taxes

- "We estimate state and local taxes using the tax rates estimates produced by Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy (ITEP) by state income quintiles. [2]" Reference [2]: "Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy. 2018. “Who Pays? A Distributional Analysis of the Tax Systems in All 50 States (6th edition).”" [SOURCE: S3]
- NAE's standard: "First, we aggregated the household income by state as well as by income quintile, and then calculated the state and local taxes based on the tax rates estimate by the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy." [SOURCE: S5 Methodology Appendix, printed p. 41 (PDF page 43)]
- ITEP's scope (S7): "The 6th edition of Who Pays?, unless otherwise noted, shows the impact of permanent tax laws on non-elderly taxpayers, including the impact of all tax changes enacted through September 10, 2018, at 2015 income levels." "In each distributional chart, the non-elderly population is divided into income quintiles (groups of 20 percent of the population). The highest-income quintile is further subdivided into three groups: the top one percent, the next highest four percent, and the next 15 percent." "Three smaller charts appear below it and show the distribution of each state’s sales and excise, personal income, and property taxes by income group." Row labels of each state's detail table (sub-rows in parentheses, separated by semicolons): "SALES & EXCISE TAXES (General Sales—Individuals; Other Sales & Excise—Ind.; Sales & Excise on Business) / PROPERTY TAXES (Home, Rent, Car— Individuals; Other Property Taxes) / INCOME TAXES (Personal Income Tax; Corporate Income Tax) / TOTAL TAXES". The totals are no longer shown "post-federal offset". [SOURCE: S7 PDF page 5 (scope footnote), PDF page 35 (state-page roadmap) and PDF page 37 (Alabama detail table)]
- Undocumented: "When estimating the tax contributions of undocumented immigrants, we follow the methodology detailed by ITEP and discount the total amount by half, considering the fact that about 50 percent of undocumented immigrants pay taxes using false Social Security or Individual Tax Identification numbers." (Method parameter: a 0.5 multiplier.) [SOURCE: S3]

**Reading.**
- **Rate source and application.** The rates are ITEP's Who Pays 6th edition (2018), applied by state and income quintile. NAE says "quintiles"; whether the three top-quintile subgroups were used is not stated.
- **Which taxes.** NAE does not list them. ITEP's "total taxes" covers sales and excise (including those on business), property (including other property taxes), and personal and corporate income taxes. Whether NAE applied the total rate is [INFERENCE] from the phrase "state and local taxes".
- **ITEP's undocumented method.** For undocumented households NAE follows "the methodology detailed by ITEP" but does not cite which ITEP document. S3's reference [7] (Hill and Wiehe 2017, young undocumented immigrants) is attached to the DACA section, not this one.

## Q5. Spending power

- "Consistent with past NAE research, we use the term “spending power.” [1] Here as elsewhere, we define spending power as the disposable income leftover after subtracting federal income, state, and local taxes from total annual household income." [SOURCE: S3]
- "In this report, we defined spending power as the disposable income in hands of the households after extracting federal, state, and local taxes from household incomes." [SOURCE: S5 Methodology Appendix, printed p. 41 (PDF page 43)]
- [CALCULATION on S2's cached tables; script `check_sp_identity.py` in the session scratchpad; no figure reproduced] Spending power = household income − "Federal income taxes" − "State and Local taxes". This holds to within 1 M$ of rounding in every state row and the United States row of Table 4, and in every row of Table 5. No row also nets out the Social Security and Medicare columns.

**Reading.** Spending power is household income minus the two published tax columns. The separately reported Social Security and Medicare contributions are not subtracted, so if the "federal income taxes" column excludes payroll, employee payroll tax stays inside spending power.

## Q6. Special treatment of undocumented immigrants and of the Mexico-born

- Status imputation in force in 2021: "Using data from the ACS, we apply the methodological approach outlined by Harvard University economist George Borjas to arrive at an estimate of the undocumented immigrant population in the overall United States and individual states. [6] The foreign-born population is adjusted for misreporting in two ways. Foreign-born individuals who reported naturalization are reclassified as non-naturalized if the individual had resided in the United States for less than six years (as of 2019) or, if married to a U.S. citizen, for less than three years. We use the following criteria to code foreign-born individuals as legal U.S. residents: Arrived in the U.S. before 1980; Citizens and children less than 18 years old reporting that at least one U.S.-born parent; Recipients of Social Security benefits, Supplemental Security Income, Medicaid, Medicare, military insurance, or public assistance; Households with at least one citizen that received SNAP benefits; People in the Armed Forces and veterans; Refugees; Working in occupations requiring a license; Working in occupations that immigrants are likely to be on H-1B or other visas, including computer scientists, professors, engineers, and life scientists; Government employees, and people working in the public administration sector; Any of the above conditions applies to the householder’s spouse." (The bullets are joined with semicolons.) [SOURCE: S3]
- Tax discount: "we follow the methodology detailed by ITEP and discount the total amount by half, considering the fact that about 50 percent of undocumented immigrants pay taxes using false Social Security or Individual Tax Identification numbers." (Method parameter.) [SOURCE: S3] The report's own wording: "even assuming conservative estimates that only half of all undocumented households filed taxes." [SOURCE: S1, S2]
- Mexico-born: the 2021 method has no Mexico-specific rule. The report applies the general undocumented method to each country of birth. [SOURCE: S3, by the absence of such a rule]

**Current AIC method, for reference only (not the method behind the 2021 figures).**
- S4 (2023 data): "Foreign-born individuals who reported naturalization are reclassified as non-naturalized if the individual had resided in the United States for less than five years; or if married to a U.S. citizen, for less than three years; or if they reported their country of origin as Mexico." [SOURCE: S4 https://map.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/methodology/]
- S4: "Following the ITEP methodology, when calculating income and payroll taxes, the total amount paid is reduced by 40 percent to reflect a 60 percent contribution rate. This takes into account lower tax compliance rates among undocumented immigrants, but also the fact that some undocumented immigrants pay more income tax via withholding than they owe. When calculating sales and excise taxes, we recalculate the income brackets that undocumented households fall into, as it is recognized that approximately 15 percent of the income earned by undocumented immigrants goes towards remittances, leaving less to spend in their local communities. Corporate income tax rates are also recalculated to reflect lower levels of capital ownership." (Method parameters from ITEP, July 2024.) [SOURCE: S4]
- S9 (2022 data): "In part to adjust for misreporting in the ACS data noted above around reporting of naturalized citizens whose country of origin is Mexico, the make-up of the sample is then reweighted based on the country of origin to reflect the demographic make-up of undocumented immigrants based on DHS estimates, which are based on administrative records." [SOURCE: S9, Methodological Appendix, printed p. 28 (PDF page 31)]
- S4's current sources are "Who Pays? ... (7th edition)" (ITEP 2024) and "The Distribution of Household Income and Federal Taxes, 2019" (CBO 2022). The rest of the household and spending-power definitions are worded as in S3. [SOURCE: S4]

## Unanswered

1. How a "Mexican undocumented household" is defined: an undocumented Mexico-born head (inferred), or any undocumented Mexico-born member. Also whether each household's income is counted once.
2. The dollar year and any inflation adjustment of ACS 2019 household income.
3. Which CBO components make up "Federal income taxes": individual income tax only, or CBO's total federal rate (individual income, employee and employer payroll, corporate, excise). NAE's 2017 standard (S5) says its "federal taxes" include Social Security and Medicare; the 2021 report presents the taxes as "in addition to" the payroll contributions. This conflict is unresolved.
4. Whether "adjusted household income" is CBO's square-root size adjustment, and whether NAE used CBO quintiles or finer CBO groups.
5. Whether the one-half discount covers all taxes (federal, state and local sales, excise and property) or only income taxes, and whether it also covers the Social Security and Medicare columns.
6. Which ITEP undocumented-immigrant methodology document NAE followed. It is not cited.
7. Whether ITEP's "total taxes" rates, or a subset, were applied, and whether the three top-quintile subgroups were used.
8. For the payroll columns: whether the wage earners counted are all earners in the household (NAE's standard) or only the undocumented individuals, and which year's Social Security taxable maximum was used.
9. How group quarters were treated. They are excluded if the household-based method was applied, but the report does not say.
10. No AIC publication was found giving taxes or spending power for all Mexico-born immigrants, or for any year after 2019.
