claude-opus-5[1m]

**Verdict:** Indian Americans remain the most Democratic-leaning large national-origin group in the
US, but the margin has eroded on a consistent trajectory across three Carnegie waves: the
presidential Democratic share among Indian American citizens ran 68% (2020) → 62% (2024) → 57%
(2026 hypothetical rerun), with Trump at 22 → 30 → 25 and the residual moving into third-party and
non-voting rather than to the Republicans. Party ID moved 52/15 D/R (2020) → 46/19 (2026) with
independents up six points. The erosion runs through **US-born men under 40**, not the naturalized
first generation — the opposite of the usual assimilation story.

**The mandatory disconfirmation largely succeeds, and the mechanism is education, not income.** In
GSS 2021–2024 data, US-born non-Hispanic white two-party voters split 46.8% for Biden overall but
**69.7% among postgraduates**, against 75.6% for Indian Americans: a graduate-degree restriction
alone closes about four-fifths of a 28.8-point raw gap. Adding top-quintile income and large-metro
residence closes none of the remainder, and the matched cell (n=86, SE 7.2 pp) cannot distinguish
its 10.9-point residual from the postgraduate-only 5.9. Income runs the *other* way inside the
white graduate group: Biden share rises 62.7 → 70.5 → 75.7 across the bottom three income
quartiles, and the third-quartile cell (median family income $101,280) sits at **75.7% against the
Indian American 75.6%** — an exact reproduction. Income is not what predicts the white Democratic
lean; the degree is. What is genuinely distinctive about Indian Americans is the
**absence of an internal education gradient** — Carnegie finds "little difference between
respondents with and without a college degree," where the general population polarises sharply.

Two instrument facts qualify the "rich but votes left" premise itself: the IAAS 2026 sample's
median household income is **$80,000–$99,999 against an ACS 2024 Asian Indian median of $161,520**,
and GSS tops family income out at **$158,425**, so neither instrument contains white households as
affluent as the median Indian American one. The income leg of the match is the weak leg, tagged
[GAP].

On giving, the headline "4–5% of income" comes from a non-representative donor-community survey
(n=280) whose own Figure 4 shows Indian American donors below $100k giving **2.5% of income against
a US average of 9.5%**, and at $100–200k **2.9% against 6.4%**. Remittances: India received
**$118.7bn** in FY2023-24 with the US the largest source at **27.7%**. Organised influence is
officeholder- and donor-heavy but institutionally thin: 6 of 6 Indian-origin House members are
Democrats, individual giving ran roughly **$46.6m D vs $16.3m R** in 2020, while the four main
ethnic advocacy bodies report about **$7.4m** combined annual revenue and the two ethnic PACs
raised **$18k and $219k** in the 2024 cycle.

## Findings — epoch 2: giving, remittances, and what the giving report actually says

### The "giving gap" claim, verified against the primary, does not say what the headline says

Fetched: *From Closing the Gap to Setting the Standard: The State of Philanthropic Giving in the
Indian American Diaspora*, Dalberg Advisors (Swetha Totapally, Girish Pendse) with Indiaspora and
the India Philanthropy Alliance, published November 2025.
[SOURCE: https://dalberg.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/2025-10_IPA-Report.pdf → local `raw/ipa_giving_2025.pdf`]

**Instrument.** The 2024/2025 Indian American Community Engagement Survey, 400+ respondents,
online, disseminated "through extensive outreach efforts led by IPA, Indiaspora, and countless
partner organizations." The report states its own limitation verbatim: *"As a result, rather than
being a representative sample of the broader Indian American Diaspora, the survey represents the
donor community."* Working N for the giving-share estimates is **280**; N for the passion-area
questions is **433** (188 women, 233 men). The report is financed by the Sehgal Foundation, the
Neera and Deepak Raj Family Foundation, the Ujala Foundation and the Indiaspora donor community —
i.e. by the community it measures.

**What the headline says:** giving rose from 1–2% of income (2018) to 4–5% (2024); total giving
$4–5bn in 2024, roughly tripled since 2018; the gap narrowed from $2–3bn to $1bn.

**What the report's own Figure 4 says, verbatim:** *"IAD donors earning less than $100,000 gave
2.5% of their income compared to the US average of 9.5%. Similarly, those earning $100,000 –
$200,000 contributed 2.9% versus the national average of 6.4%."* Giving as a share of income
**declined** for Indian American donors earning under $200,000. The aggregate rise is carried
entirely by high-income donors, who now exceed the US average. So the group-level "we give 4–5% of
income" and the band-level "we give a third of what comparable Americans give below $200k" are both
in the same report, and only the first is in the press release.

**Three methodological cautions the report itself supplies:** (1) the percentages are for **donor
households only** and exclude non-donors; (2) the top giving band changed between waves ($10K+ in
2018 → $50K+/$500K+ in 2024), so the 1–2% → 4–5% trend is partly an instrument change, which the
authors acknowledge and claim survives adjustment; (3) "Given the 2024 IAD sample was
disproportionately wealthy, we applied population weights across income bands" — the raw sample is
skewed rich, unlike the Carnegie IAAS sample which is skewed poor. The two headline instruments for
this community lean in opposite directions on income.

**The $1bn "gap" is a modelled residual, not an observation.** Its construction, verbatim from the
notes to Figure 1: Donation capability = IAD households (ACS) × mean gross IAD household income
(ACS) × donor rate (Lilly School Philanthropy Panel Study 2021) × share of gross income donated by
*American* donor households (IRS SOI); Actual donations = the same first two terms × an IAD donor
rate **assumed equal to the US donor rate** × the share donated by IAD donors from the survey. The
entire gap therefore reduces to one survey-measured ratio (IAD share of income donated, n=280)
against one IRS-derived ratio, with the donor *rate* assumed identical by construction. It is not
evidence that Indian Americans donate less often, only that sampled donors report donating a
different share.

**Volunteering.** 2024 finding: respondents averaged **200 volunteer hours a year against a
national average of 70** — "nearly three times." The report states in the same passage that the
national figure fell from 96 hours (2017) to 70 (2023), and that IAD volunteering itself *declined*
slightly since 2018. The ratio grew partly because the denominator collapsed. The 2018 study's
comparable claim was "nearly twice the US average." [SOURCE: `raw/ipa.txt` lines 199–203, citing
US Census Bureau volunteerism releases 2024]

**Population and income anchors used by the report:** IAD 5.2 million, up over 1 million since
2018; median household income $110,000 (2015–16 ACS) → $151,000 (2021–23 ACS); 12 Indian-origin
billionaires in the US. [SOURCE: same, citing Census tabulations of ACS via IPUMS and Forbes 2025]

The report asserts: *"despite making up only 1.5% of the U.S. population, Indian Americans
contribute 5-6% of all US tax revenues."* Its footnote 4 traces to **Indiaspora, "Small Community,
Big Contributions, Boundless Horizons: The Indian Diaspora in the US" (2024 Indiaspora Impact

## Findings — epoch 3: the mandatory disconfirmation, remittances, organised influence

### The matched-white comparison: education removes most of the gap, income removes none of it

Script `matched_white.py`, outputs `derived/matched_white_gss.csv` and
`derived/matched_white_gss_partyid.csv`. Instrument: GSS 1972–2024 cumulative, Stata release R3a,
the 2021/2022/2024 waves, which carry 2020 presidential vote recall (`pres20`), `degree`, `coninc`
(family income in constant dollars) and `srcbelt` (SRC beltcode: 1–4 = central cities and suburbs
of the 100 largest SMSAs). Weights `wtssps` with `wtssnrps` fallback; standard errors use the Kish
effective-n from the weights. Universe: US-born non-Hispanic white two-party voters, n=2,967
unweighted. Benchmark: Carnegie IAAS 2020, Indian American US citizens, 68% Biden / 22% Trump →
**two-party Biden share 75.6%**.

| Cell | n | n_eff | Biden two-party % | SE | Gap to Indian Americans |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| US-born NH white, all | 2,967 | 1,324 | 46.8 | 1.4 | **28.8 pp** |
| + BA or higher | 1,380 | 625 | 61.6 | 1.9 | 14.0 pp |
| + postgraduate degree | 553 | 256 | **69.7** | 2.9 | **5.9 pp** |
| + BA+ and top income quintile | 451 | 236 | 66.1 | 3.1 | 9.5 pp |
| + postgrad and top income quintile | 212 | 106 | 70.5 | 4.4 | 5.1 pp |
| + BA+, top quintile, top-100 metro | 170 | 92 | 63.3 | 5.0 | 12.3 pp |
| **+ postgrad, top quintile, top-100 metro [matched]** | **86** | **45** | **64.7** | **7.2** | **10.9 pp** |
| + postgrad, top income decile, top-100 metro [strict] | 63 | 33 | 66.3 | 8.2 | 9.3 pp |

**The answer to the brief's question is mostly yes, and the mechanism is education, not income.**
The raw 28.8-point gap between Indian Americans and US-born whites falls to 5.9 points once the
white comparison is restricted to postgraduates — roughly **four-fifths of the gap is a degree
composition effect.** Adding income moves the white Democratic share by essentially nothing (69.7 →
70.5 for postgraduates entering the top quintile) and adding metro residence moves it *down*
(70.5 → 64.7), which is a small-cell artefact more than a finding: the matched cell holds 86
respondents with an effective n of 45 and a standard error of 7.2 points, so the residual 10.9-point
gap sits about 1.5 standard errors from zero and **is not distinguishable from the 5.9-point
postgraduate-only residual.**

The same pattern appears in party identification, which does not depend on vote recall and has
larger cells: US-born NH whites overall are 36.1% Democratic / 45.3% Republican including leaners;
postgraduates are 58.6/30.4; the matched postgrad + top-quintile + metro cell is 62.1/31.5 (n=219).
Compare Indian Americans at 47/21 D/R with 26% independent (IAAS 2024) — on party ID the matched
white cell is if anything *more* Democratic than Indian Americans are, because Indian Americans
park a much larger share in the independent column.

**So the "rich but votes left" framing survives badly.** For US-born whites, income is not what
predicts the Democratic lean; the graduate degree is, and the Indian American population is
roughly 75–78% college-educated with a very high postgraduate share. The distinctive Indian
American fact is not that a high-income group votes left — postgraduate high-income metro whites
also vote left — it is that Indian Americans show **almost no internal education gradient** (IAAS
2024, claim 6), so the group leans Democratic at both ends of the education distribution where
whites do not.

**Limits of this test, stated plainly.** (1) It is a **cross-instrument** comparison: GSS is a
probability sample with in-person/phone/web modes, IAAS is a sample-matched online panel. Mode
effects are not adjusted and could plausibly be several points. (2) **The income match cannot
reach the relevant range.** GSS `coninc` is top-coded at **$158,425**, with 330 of 9,550 valid
2021+ cases piled at that maximum, and the p90 cut is $139,024. The Asian Indian median household
income is $151,000–$161,520. The "top decile" white cell is therefore a *top-coded* cell, not a cell
of people as rich as the median Indian American household. A proper income match needs CES or ACS-
linked data. [GAP] (3) `pres20` is vote recall collected one to four years after the fact, which
inflates the winner's share. (4) The GSS comparison is against the **2020** IAAS benchmark, because
`pres24` is not in the R3a release; the 2024 comparison is not run. [GAP]

close the residual gap and the top-coding limitation is not load-bearing for the conclusion.
Script `income_gradient.py`, output `derived/income_gradient_white.csv`. Same universe and weights.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| BA or higher | Q1 lowest | 339 | $28,485 | 58.2 |
| BA or higher | Q2 | 343 | $52,222 | 55.3 |
| BA or higher | Q3 | 444 | $90,080 | 67.7 |
| BA or higher | Q4 highest | 162 | $158,425 | 63.8 |
| Postgraduate | Q1 lowest | 150 | $34,815 | 62.7 |
| Postgraduate | Q2 | 111 | $63,300 | 70.5 |
| Postgraduate | Q3 | 183 | $101,280 | **75.7** |
| Postgraduate | Q4 highest | 76 | $158,425 | 64.3 |

Within white postgraduates the Democratic share **rises** with income through the third quartile —
62.7 → 70.5 → 75.7 — and the Q3 cell, at a median family income of $101,280, sits at **75.7%
Biden against the Indian American 75.6%**. At a real, non-top-coded income level the matched white

evidence of a rightward turn at the top: Q4's median *equals* the top-code ($158,425), so it is the
censored pile rather than a measured income band, and it holds 76 respondents.

That settles the direction question. Money does not push white graduates right over the observable
range, so the inability to match Indian Americans' actual income level weakens the precision of the
matched cell without threatening the conclusion. **Indian Americans vote like highly-educated
metropolitan white Americans of comparable income, and the raw "rich but votes left" gap is
composition.**

**Implied US→India flow ≈ $32.9bn for FY2023-24** — but this is an [INFERENCE], not an RBI figure,
and it carries a denominator mismatch the RBI states in its own note to Table 1: *"For 2023-24,
shares are derived based on two major components of inward remittances – (a) transfers for family
maintenance and savings; (b) local withdrawals from non-resident deposit accounts."* The 27.7%
share is computed on those two components, not on the full $118.7bn balance-of-payments total.
Applying it to the total is an approximation whose error is unquantified here. The
`../remit_leak_2026_09_16/` lane's correction — that an accounting quotient is not a measured
sending propensity — applies with full force: **27.7% is a source-country share of receipts, not a
rate at which Indian Americans remit.** Nothing here identifies what fraction of Indian American
income leaves the country.

The RBI article also attributes the rising advanced-economy share to "a shift in migration pattern
towards skilled Indian diaspora" and notes 13.4 lakh (1.34m) Indian students abroad as of January
2024, of whom 25.3% are in the US.

> **Indian Americans vote Democratic at rates that a matched white comparison largely reproduces.**
> Across three Carnegie waves the Democratic presidential share among Indian American citizens ran

> column [SOURCE: Carnegie IAAS, N=714 citizens 2024 and N=870 citizens 2026, YouGov online panel,

> multi-racial respondents, so the endpoints are not strictly comparable.
>
> The apparent anomaly — a group with roughly double the national median household income voting
> two-to-one Democratic — is mostly a composition fact about graduate degrees. In GSS 2021–2024
> data, US-born non-Hispanic white two-party voters split 46.8% for Biden overall, but 69.7% among
> postgraduates, against an Indian American two-party Biden share of 75.6% in 2020. Education alone

> weak one: GSS top-codes family income at $158,425, below the Asian Indian median household income
> of about $151,000–$161,520, so no GSS cell contains white households as affluent as the median
> Indian American one.
>

> "little difference between respondents with and without a college degree" in Indian American vote

> been identical (22%) across both groups in 2020.
>
