Summary

  • Other US residents pay about {{q:case.main|mid}} a year ({{q:case.main|range}}). That is about {{q:case.per_member|mid_range}} per member of a lineage of {{q:headcount.lineage|value}}: immigrants, their children and later descendants. Only 2024 is measured.
  • The cost comes from lower taxes, not from heavier use. Against as many average residents, lower taxes at the same ages make up {{q:decomp.taxes_share|range_unit}} of the group's extra cost. With pensions counted when earned, its taxes about equal its benefits. Schools and other public services make the cost.
  • Who migrates matters most. Under the same rules, Indian-origin residents benefit others by about {{q:indian.per_member|mid_range}} per member. Children's schooling follows their parents' place in the home country's schooling range.
  • Legal status explains little. Imputed unauthorized and legal Mexico-born adults have similar fiscal gaps.
  • The college gap stops closing after the second generation. All three generations cost others. The second generation closes 31% of the college gap, and the third keeps about 86% of what is left. Descendants who stop reporting Mexican origin are too few to explain the stall. The total counts them.
  • State and local taxpayers pay most. About one other resident in six comes out ahead, mostly landlords and high earners. Renters and workers with less schooling lose most.
  • Costs outside the budget raise the total to about {{q:pairing.total|mid_range}}. These are pollution, road crashes, crime victims, traffic and fear, net of gains from a larger economy.
  • Immigrants offend less than natives, and their US-born sons are held at about twice the white rate. Parental income explains most of the sons' gap.
  • The frame matters. The group gains much by living here. With every person weighted equally, the world gains about {{q:world.total_equal|value}} in the year measured. Other US residents lose at any weight. Counting the group as US residents, the US comes out behind when the group's welfare counts for less than {{q:world.breakeven_us|value}} of other residents'.
  • Of the budget assumptions, the horizon moves the size most. With first-year budget responses the total is about {{q:case.first_year|mid_range}}. Counting descendants by their share of Mexican ancestry gives about {{q:case.ancestry_share|mid_range}}. Sampling noise is about ±{{q:noise.sampling_95|value}}.

One year

The account covers 2024 only. It is the only year with a full measured account. It takes the people who live here now and asks what their presence costs others in that year. Economists call this an annual cross-section.

All governments, not the federal budget

The total covers federal, state and local budgets together. About {{q:incidence.state_local_share|range_unit}} falls on state and local budgets, mostly through schools, police and local services. The pension promises are federal. Counting pensions when paid, the state and local part is {{q:incidence.state_local_share_cash|range_unit}}.

Deficit

States and cities must balance their budgets. Their part of the cost shows up as higher taxes or fewer services in the same year. Only the federal part can become debt. So the total is not a deficit number.

Interest

If the federal part of past gaps was borrowed, taxpayers pay about {{q:debt.interest|mid_range}} a year in interest on it. This is a legacy cost. Removing the group in 2024 does not remove the old debt. So the interest stays beside the total.

Return on public capital

Schools, roads and public buildings tie up capital that could earn a return in another use. National accounts charge only wear, so they set the return to zero. The total charges 2–3% a year. Economists call this the opportunity cost of capital. It is a resource cost, not a payment. It never enters the deficit or the debt.

Pensions: when paid or when earned

On a cash basis, a worker's payroll tax counts as income in the year it is paid. But the tax also buys a claim on future benefits. The members who report Mexican origin are young, and they pay about $65bn more payroll tax than they draw (63–68). The main estimate counts the promise when it is earned, at the benefits current law can pay on the Trustees' 2026 projections. Current law keeps the retirement and disability funds apart. In place of the year's pension cash flows, that adds about {{q:pension.accrual|mid_range}} to the total. Counting pensions when paid gives {{q:case.cash_set|mid_range}}.

Earlier years

Years before 2024 come from a model. It carries the 2024 position back on each year's national data. Do not multiply the annual number by a number of years.

Lifetimes

Lifetime numbers come from a separate ledger. They compare the group with whites and discount future years at 3%. They agree in direction with the annual total. Never scale one onto the other.

How to read this page

Section headings state the claim. Numbers show a rounded value first and the range in brackets: "{{q:case.main|mid_range}}". The range is the spread over the account's choices, not a confidence interval, unless the text says so.

Under each finding, two words say how the number was obtained: where the input comes from, and what the analysis does with it. The two are separate. Records read off directly are strong. Records carried to another decade are weaker. A sample survey read off directly can be stronger than records put through a model.

{{LEGEND}}

A "known bias" line appears only where the error has a known direction. Random error in an outcome shrinks at these sample sizes. Random error in a group label does not vanish: it pulls gaps between groups toward zero. The decennial census counts everyone, but people report their own ethnicity. The ACS and CPS are sample surveys.

Open "Evidence" under a finding for the reasoning, what would change the number, the data and the sources.
