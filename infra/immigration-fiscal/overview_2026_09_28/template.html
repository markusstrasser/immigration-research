Evidence Map

What the Mexican-origin population costs other US residents, and on what evidence. Fiscal year 2024. {{N_FINDINGS}} findings in 15 groups, drawn from {{N_ENTRIES}} analyses.

The big strokes

  • One year is measured: 2024. In that year the Mexican-origin population (40.9M people, three generations) cost all other US residents $322–387bn, or $7.9–9.5k per member.
  • Services set the sign. The group pays about $60–70bn more in taxes than it gets in cash benefits. Public services reverse the sign. Schooling is the largest line, about $214–227bn 250. If less than 3–14% of service costs grow with population, the account turns positive 239.
  • The range is choices, not noise. Sampling noise is about ±$21bn. Single choices move the number by $25–85bn. The second chart ranks them.
  • Two accounting choices move it most. If property taxes follow people like the capital they fund, it falls to about $290–356bn. If pensions count when earned, not when paid, it rises by about $75bn 253, 257.
  • Outside the budgets the group adds crime-victim, pollution, road-crash, traffic and fear costs, net of scale gains. With them, the total is {{SOCIAL_TOTAL}} a year 195, 260, 264.
  • State and local budgets pay about 85%. About one other resident in six comes out ahead, mostly landlords and the top income tenth 226.
  • All three generations are net costs. The second generation closes most of the school-completion gap. Progress then stalls: about 0.84 of the college gap stays in the third-plus generation 224, 178, 232.
  • Against an equal number of whites the group costs about $315–330bn a year more, or $406–412bn with local whites by state 263.
  • Frame matters. Other US residents lose. The group itself gains much by living here. At equal weights the world total is positive 250 [framing-sensitive].

Snapshot

The account covers one year, 2024. It is the only year with a full measured account. It takes the people who live here now and asks what their presence costs others in that year. Economists call this an annual cross-section or a static account. A snapshot is unfair to a group with many children, who cost now and pay later. For this group the young age mix works the other way: at a common age mix the per-person gap is larger, $7,049 against $4,093 raw 161.

Budget

The headline covers all governments together: federal, state and local. It is not the federal budget. About 85% of the cost falls on state and local budgets, mostly through schools, police and local services 226. The federal part is 13–19% of the 2024 cash gap 207. Federal payroll taxes run ahead of Social Security and Medicare; federal income tax runs behind federal Medicaid, credits and food aid.

Deficit

States and cities must balance their budgets. Their part of the cost therefore shows up as higher taxes or fewer services in the same year. It does not become debt. Only the federal part can become deficit. So the headline is not a deficit number. CBO's often-quoted $897bn lower federal deficit over 2024–2034 is a different object again: federal only, new arrivals of all origins, a projection that includes growth effects and leaves out most discretionary spending 55.

Interest

If the federal part of past gaps was borrowed, it left $0.96–1.29tn of debt entering FY2024. In 2024 taxpayers pay $31–42bn of interest on it, about $756–1,018 per member 207. This is a real cost, but it is a legacy cost. Removing the group in 2024 does not remove the old debt. The account therefore holds existing interest at a response of zero and reports the legacy interest beside the headline. Adding it would count a past decision as a present effect of presence.

Return on public capital

Schools, roads and public buildings tie up capital. That capital could earn a return elsewhere. National accounts charge only depreciation, so they value the return at zero. The main case charges 2% real at the low end and 3% at the high end: $34–56bn 238, 239. Economists call this the opportunity cost of capital or user cost. It is a resource cost, not a payment. It never enters a deficit, debt or interest figure. At a private 7% it would be $406–462bn in total, reported beside.

Cash and accrual

On a cash basis a worker's payroll tax counts as income in the year it is paid. But the tax also buys a claim on future Social Security and Medicare benefits. A young group pays much more payroll tax than it draws: this group's 2024 surplus is $63–68bn 257. Cash books that surplus as a gain. Accrual books the benefit promise earned in 2024 instead. Under current law, with benefits cut to what the trust funds can pay after their reserves run out, accrual adds $77.3 / $73.6bn. With scheduled benefits paid in full, as CBO's baseline assumes by statute, it adds $106–112bn. Accrual replaces the cash pension lines; it is a swap, not an extra line.

2024 and earlier years

Years before 2024 are a model, not a measurement. The back-cast carries the 2024 position back on national series for each year: receipts, programmes, prices, population and the group's relative income. Over 2015–2024 it gives $2.8–3.7tn in 2024 dollars, without interest 162. Do not multiply the annual number by years; the back-cast already does the year-by-year work.

Lifetimes and lineages

The lifetime numbers come from a separate ledger. They compare the group with a white reference group and discount future years at 3%. Per founder, the Mexican lineage runs $1.29M behind the white lineage undiscounted, $513k at 3% 159. That is a gap against a reference. The headline is a cost of removal with no reference group. They agree in direction. Never scale one onto the other.
