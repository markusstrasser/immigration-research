# Housing competition & deportation-rent stories — probe

**Verdict:** The story is real and the operator remembers it correctly — Doral, Florida, reported by the Wall Street Journal in late September 2025 and syndicated by The Real Deal on 2025-10-01. **It does not hold up quantitatively.** Doral's repeat-rent index was $2,864.6 in October 2025, *above* five earlier months in 2023-24; the "three-year low" is not reproducible at the date it was published, and Doral's worst reading (January 2026, $2,837) is a 14-month low that underperforms the national by about 2 percentage points. Nothing in the Zillow metro panel shows a deportation-timed rent collapse anywhere. The metros with the largest declines (Austin −10.8%, San Antonio −3.9%) peaked in **August 2022** and fell on construction completions, years before the enforcement surge, and the heavy-enforcement metros of 2025-26 (Chicago +10.5%, New York +9.9%, Minneapolis +7.6% since January 2025) beat the national +4.8%. San Diego, cited by the White House at −5.7%, is at an **all-time high**.

**The defensible per-1%-of-population rent elasticity is about +1.0%, range 0.8-2.2%**, anchored on Saiz 2007 (JUE, shift-share IV, +1.0%) and best-identified in its modern form by Cabral & Steingress (European Economic Review 2026, +2.2% rents / +3.8% prices, ancestry-composition IV), which splits into +6 to +8% in permit-restrictive counties receiving educated immigrants and **0 to −2%** in permissive counties receiving low-education immigrants. Incidence lands on incumbent renters at the bottom of the quality distribution; incumbent owners capitalize a gain.

**Running that elasticity backwards to price deportation is the central error.** Howard, Wang & Zhang (November 2025) measure both arms of the same program: Secure Communities cut homebuilding 5.7%, raised new-construction prices 4.5%, and moved existing-stock prices **+3.4% in the least-undocumented tracts and −4.1% in the most**, with zero effect on average and homeownership down 93 basis points. Removal is not arrival with a minus sign, because it strikes construction labor and housing demand at once.

### The defensible number [INFERENCE]

**Per 1% of metro population arriving, rents rise about 1%, with a credible range of 0.8-2.2% and a short-run spike up to 3.75%.** Saiz's 1.0% is the anchor; Cabral & Steingress's 2.2% is the best-identified modern replication and the right number to use when the destination is supply-constrained; the 6-8% upper tail applies only to restrictive-permit counties receiving high-education immigrants, and the elasticity goes to **zero or negative** in permissive counties receiving low-education immigrants. Incidence falls on incumbent **renters**, concentrated in the bottom of the quality distribution; incumbent **owners gain** through capitalization.

**Running that elasticity in reverse to price deportation is wrong**, and this is the load-bearing result of the whole probe. The removal shock is not the arrival shock with a minus sign, because it hits construction labor at the same time it hits housing demand. Howard, Wang & Zhang measure both arms on the same program and find the supply arm dominates everywhere except the most heavily undocumented tracts. The policy claim "mass deportations lower housing costs" is therefore true only in the specific neighborhoods immigrants actually live in (−4.1% there), and false on average (+3.4% in the whitest-collar tracts, zero overall, homeownership down 93 bp). Konczal's arithmetic — removing one million renters saves the average tenant **$4.40 per month** — is the same conclusion from the demand side alone. [SOURCE: https://www.stacker.news/items/1407724]

## APPEND 4 — verified negative + one bonus source [DATA] [SOURCE]

**[VERIFIED NEGATIVE] "Howard, Liebersohn & Ozimek 2023, *The short-run effects of the immigration surge on housing*, JUE" does not appear to exist.** Two targeted Exa passes (one on the title, one on Ozimek + EIG + immigration surge + housing) returned nothing matching. What exists is Howard & Liebersohn, JUE 124 (2021) 103369, on location demand and supply-inelastic cities, which is not an immigration paper, and Ozimek's EIG housing writing, which is about zoning and supply, not a rent elasticity. Treat the citation as a composite memory until a DOI is produced, and do not carry it into a memo.

**Bonus, and it strengthens the repo's growth-margin rent null (−0.17):** Cheremukhin, Restrepo-Echavarria, Wright et al. (2026), summarized at CEPR/VoxEU, calibrate a multi-household-type macro model to the 2021-24 surge and find the disinflationary supply channel and the inflationary demand channel **approximately cancel**, with a peak annualized inflation response of about **5 basis points** and output up ~0.5% over the medium run. Immigrants in the surge behave as hand-to-mouth consumers: consumption-to-income 83% versus 66% for natives, 63% hand-to-mouth versus 38%. [SOURCE: https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/why-post-pandemic-us-immigration-surge-barely-moved-inflation]

**Saiz's own welfare benchmark, worth quoting directly:** raising a city's foreign-born share by 1 percentage point needs an inflow of 1.15% of initial population, which raises rents ~1.15%; since renters spend about a quarter of income on rent, that is **0.28% of initial renter income** — an order of magnitude larger than the relative labor-market effect for the same renters. [SOURCE: https://docs.iza.org/dp2189.pdf]
