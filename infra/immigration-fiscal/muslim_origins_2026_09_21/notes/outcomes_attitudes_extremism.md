claude-opus-5[1m]

**Verdict:** Four primary documents were fetched and checked line-by-line. Pew 2017 (n=1,001 Muslim
adults) establishes that US Muslims are 58% foreign-born, match the general public on college
degrees (31% vs 31%) and on incomes ≥$100k (24% vs 23%), but are over-represented below $30k (40%
vs 32%) and own homes far less (37% vs 57%) — a bimodal distribution, not a uniformly poor or
uniformly elite one. On the attitude items the picture is genuinely mixed and is usually reported
dishonestly in both directions: 12% of US Muslims say killing civilians can often/sometimes be
justified versus 14% of the general public, and *net* rejection is identical (84% vs 83%) — the
much-quoted "76% vs 59% say never" gap exists only because the general public splits into "rarely"
(24% vs 8%). On homosexuality US Muslims are distinctly more conservative (52% say society should
accept vs 63% public) but moved +25 points in a decade. Cato PA 991 gives hard denominators: 237
foreign-born terrorists, 3,046 murders over 50 years, 0.30% of all US murders, 1 in 4.56 million
per year — and simultaneously that foreign-born Islamists account for 99.4% of all terror murders
on US soil, a figure that is 97.8% 9/11. Cato's route split is the load-bearing detail: 81 LPRs,
44 tourists (the hijackers), 29 refugees, 9 illegal entrants with **zero** murders in 50 years.
The Europe contrast is strong for concern *in Europe* (Koopmans: 44% of European Muslims hold
consistent fundamentalist attitudes, 54% see the West as an enemy, 26% hostile to all three
out-groups) but Koopmans himself argues in the same paper that this does not transfer to the US,
attributing the gap to the US Muslim population being "predominantly middle class and highly
educated". What is thin: I did not reach the New Immigrant Survey, CES religion item, ISPU poll,
New America, or GWU Program on Extremism (budget). One structural caveat matters more than any
number here: **Cato counts only the foreign-born, explicitly excluding native-born terrorists**, so
it cannot speak to the second generation — which is exactly where the repository's generational
framing would want evidence. And the Danish MENAPT account, though genuinely negative
(−13bn kr immigrants, −11bn kr descendants in 2019), is a *country grouping*, not a religion
variable, and Denmark's own ministry attributes the entire immigrant gap to employment rates.
Where it is best measured, the US evidence points markedly less alarming than the European
evidence, and the US-vs-Europe divergence is itself the most robust finding on this axis.

## Datasets with religion observed
- **Pew 2017 Survey of U.S. Muslims** — n=1,001 Muslim adults, fielded Jan 23–May 2 2017, RDD
  landline+cell, case-level microdata downloadable after free account registration. Variables:
  nativity, country of birth, education, household income bands, homeownership, employment,
  religiosity, the violence-against-civilians item, homosexuality acceptance, discrimination
  experience, partisanship. Earlier waves 2011 and 2007 exist in the same series (attitude trend
  in B11 is built from all three). **This is the best US religion×economics instrument found.**
- **Koopmans 2015 six-country survey** — Turkish- and Moroccan-origin Muslims + native Christians
  in DE, FR, NL, BE, AT, SE; religion, denomination (Alevite vs Sunni distinguished), fundamentalism
  battery, out-group hostility battery. European only; not a general Muslim sample.
- **Danish Finance Ministry register account** — full-population register data, but religion is
  NOT observed; MENAPT is a birthplace grouping (B28). Same measurement basis as this repository's
  own ACS/CPS work, so it is a comparison case, not a religion-observing dataset.
- `[GAP]` Not reached: New Immigrant Survey 2003 public-use files (religion + earnings + visa
  route — potentially the single most valuable US source for this repo, since it observes visa
  class *and* religion), Cooperative Election Study religion item, ISPU American Muslim Poll,
  New America "Terrorism in America After 9/11" database, GWU Program on Extremism tracker.
