# Burstein, Hanson, Tian, Vogel — "Tradability and the Labor-Market Impact of Immigration: Theory and Evidence from the United States", *Econometrica* 88(3), May 2020, 1071–1112. DOI 10.3982/ECTA16196

Paper: `/Users/alien/Projects/corpus/doi_10_3982_ecta16196/parsed.pymupdf4llm@0.3.4+cfg-99914b93/page.md`

**Verdict:** A Card/shift-share 2SLS design over 722 US commuting zones × 50 occupations, 1980–2012, showing that immigrant inflows crowd natives out of *more* relative to *less* immigrant-intensive **nontradable** occupations (about −0.30 to −0.37) but have a precisely estimated **zero** effect across tradable occupations; the design is strong for these *relative, within-region* contrasts and says nothing about aggregate or average native wage levels, which come only from the calibrated model.

## Population, period, unit
- **Unit of observation:** commuting zone × occupation (722 CZs × 50 occupations); regressions run separately for low- and high-education natives. "Observations are for CZ–occupation pairs (722 CZs×50 occupations)."
- **Period:** 1980–2012 long difference. Sensitivity uses 1990 start and 2007 end.
- **Data:** IPUMS — 5% Census 1980 and 1990; pooled 2011/2012/2013 1% ACS for "2012". Ages 16–64 in the year preceding the survey; group-quarters residents dropped.
- **Outcome measures:** employment = total hours worked by native-born in full-time-equivalent units; labor payments = total wages and salaries in a CZ–occupation.
- **Immigrant definition:** born outside the US and not born to US citizens; undocumented included to the extent the surveys capture them. "share of immigrants in hours worked in our sample rises from" 6.6% in 1980 to 16.8% in 2012
- **Education cells:** natives split college vs. less than college; immigrants split three ways (less than high school, HS/some college, college), with the extra bottom cell created "given the preponderance of undocumented immigrants in this group".
- **Tradability:** Blinder–Krueger (2013) offshorability, split at the median into 25 tradable and 25 nontradable occupations. Most tradable: fabricators, financial-record processors, mathematicians/computer scientists, textile-machine operators. Least tradable: firefighters, health assessors, therapists, vehicle mechanics.

## Design and identification
- **Shock:** `x_ro` = percentage growth in working-age immigrants in education cell e in CZ r, weighted by the initial share of foreign-born in that education cell in occupation o's earnings in r, summed over e (eq. 23, p. 1085).
- **Instrument:** a Card/Altonji–Card shift–share (eq. 26, p. 1088) — national change in immigrants from source c with education e *excluding region r*, allocated by region r's historical share of immigrants from c with education e, then routed into occupations by initial immigrant intensity. 12 source regions; the sum is disaggregated so there are **three instruments per endogenous variable**.
- **Controls:** occupation dummies and CZ × occupation-group (tradable/nontradable) dummies. Weighted by start-of-period CZ–occupation–education native population. **SEs clustered on state.**
- **First stage (Angrist–Pischke F):** Panel A 136.10 (low ed.) and 105.67 (high ed.); Panel B 102.77 and 75.21 (low ed.), 65.90 and 48.48 (high ed.). Strong.
- **Identifying restrictions** stated explicitly (p. 1088): predicted inflow uncorrelated with (i) non-immigration-induced native supply change and (ii) the weighted regional productivity shock; and (iii) initial region–occupation immigrant intensity `S^I_reo` uncorrelated with the region–occupation productivity shock.
- **Robustness run:** dropping the largest immigrant-receiving CZs; the modified instrument of eq. (27) using out-of-state occupation intensities; a placebo regressing 1950–1980 native occupational employment change on the 1980–2012 shock (opposite sign, so no continuing-trend artifact); alternative tradability cutoffs; industries instead of occupations; dropping routine- and communication-intensive jobs; 2007 end year. Changing the start year to 1990 weakens the low-education nontradable effect but not the high-education one.

## What it says about

- **Native wages by skill/education.** Not identified in the reduced form. The authors say explicitly that measuring occupation-level wage changes "is not straightforward because changes in observable wages reflect both changes in the occupation wage per efficiency unit of labor and self-selection of workers across occupations according to unobserved worker productivity", so they "analyze immigration impacts on occupational labor allocations and labor payments in this section and address wage changes in our quantitative exercises." Wage numbers therefore come from the **calibrated model**, not from a wage regression. In the model, the education wage premium falls for both a Latin American *reduction* and a college-immigrant *increase*, and the authors stress that this is a Roy-model composition result, not within-occupation substitution: "Our Roy model, in which education groups are perfect substitutes *within* occupations, endogenously generates *aggregate* patterns of imperfect substitutability between education groups."
- **Native employment / crowd-out.** The core result. Average crowd-out across all occupations (−0.15 low ed., −0.23 high ed.), decomposing into ≈0 within tradables and ≈−0.30/−0.37 within nontradables. This is a *relative* statement across occupations within a CZ: "Because these results on greater crowding out of natives by immigrants within nontradables (compared to tradables) involve comparisons across native workers within a region, they do not imply that native workers in immigrant-intensive jobs within nontradables must lose from immigration."
- **Housing prices, rents.** Not studied. Saiz (2007) is cited once in a footnote as prior work on land rents; there is no housing outcome and no housing sector in the model.
- **Fiscal: taxes, transfers, public services, schooling.** Not studied. No fiscal variable appears anywhere. Worker education is taken as given: "Whereas we allow occupational choice to respond to immigration, we take worker education as given" (fn. 2, paraphrase of "While we allow occupational choice to respond to immigration, we take worker education as given").
- **Firms, production, investment, profits.** No firm-level data; perfect competition, constant returns, no capital. Production enters only as occupation output `Q_ro` in the CES of eq. (1). Labor payments are the model's proxy for occupational revenue: the labor-payments regression is the *mechanism test* — tradables adjust through quantity, nontradables through price. Agglomeration is a sensitivity only (λ = 0.05), not a baseline.
- **Mechanism the authors claim.** Crowding out happens iff the native–immigrant substitution elasticity ρ exceeds the elasticity of local occupation output to local price ε_rg: "native-born workers are crowded out by an inflow of immigrants if and only if the elasticity of substitution between native and immigrant labor within each occupation is greater than the elasticity of local occupation output to local prices." Tradability raises ε_rg (local prices are pinned down by outside markets), so tradables absorb inflows through output expansion and nontradables through price declines. The labor-payments signs (γ > 0, γ_N < 0) confirm it, and the authors note this matches Cortes (2008), who found immigrant inflows cut prices of immigrant-intensive nontraded services but not of tradables.

## Elasticities or parameters a model could transport

**The 4.6 the parent is chasing is ρ.** Precisely:

> ρ = the **elasticity of substitution between immigrant and domestic labor *within* a single occupation**, in the CES occupation production function `Q_ro = A_ro [ (A^I_ro L^I_ro)^((ρ−1)/ρ) + (A^D_ro L^D_ro)^((ρ−1)/ρ) ]^(ρ/(ρ−1))`, **equation (1), p. 1076**. Reported value **ρ = 4.6, Table III, p. 1098**.

Four qualifications that matter for transport, all in the paper:

1. **It is calibrated, not estimated.** "Since estimates of the elasticity of substitution between occupations, η, and the elasticity of substitution between native and immigrant workers within occupations, ρ, are not available from existing research, we calibrate them." The targets are the two reduced-form coefficients (β^D = −0.01, β^D_N = −0.34), matched by running the same 2SLS regression on model-generated data. There is no standard error and no confidence interval for ρ anywhere in the paper.
2. **It is a within-occupation object, at the CZ × occupation level**, estimated on US natives vs. all foreign-born, 722 CZs, 50 occupations, 1980–2012, two native education groups and three immigrant education groups. Within an occupation, **education groups are perfect substitutes** — imperfect native/immigrant substitution lives entirely in the nativity dimension inside an occupation cell, and aggregate education-level imperfect substitutability is an *emergent* Roy-sorting result, not a parameter.
3. **ρ = 4.6 is pinned by the near-zero tradable coefficient, i.e. by ρ ≈ ε_rT ≈ α = 7.** "targeting β [D] ≈ 0 in the employment-allocation regression (no crowding in or out within tradables for natives) requires that the elasticity of regional output to the regional producer price within tradables, ϵrT, equals the elasticity of substitution between native- and foreign-born workers within each occupation, ρ. Thus, ρ must be closer to α than to η, yielding ρ = 4�6." So ρ inherits the trade elasticity's calibration, which is an assumption (α = 7) from Head–Mayer, not a US immigration estimate. Under free trade within tradables the same moments give **ρ = 6.8** (fn. 38).
4. **The authors say explicitly that this is NOT the aggregate native–immigrant substitution elasticity of Borjas–Grogger–Hanson or Ottaviano–Peri, and that the aggregate version is about twice as large.** Footnote 42, p. 1101: "Unlike the elasticity of substitution between immigrant and domestic workers within occupations ρ, the aggregate substitution elasticity is not a structural parameter in our model. When we estimate it using model-generated data, it is roughly twice as high as our assumed value of ρ; see Appendix I.2." **[UNVERIFIED — parent's judgement call]** If the parent's removal model uses an ε between natives and foreign-born *inside an education cell* (i.e. an aggregate, occupation-pooled nativity elasticity), the transportable number from this paper is the implied **aggregate ≈ 9**, not 4.6. Using 4.6 in an education-cell nest imports a within-occupation elasticity into a cross-occupation aggregate and will overstate imperfect substitutability, hence overstate the native surplus/loss from a removal. Tripling to ρ = 13.8 breaks the paper's own tradable moment (β^D = −0.14), so 4.6–13.8 brackets the range in which this paper's mechanism still works, with ~9 as the authors' own aggregate mapping.

Other transportable parameters, all on the same US CZ × occupation 1980–2012 sample: **α = 7** (substitution across regional origins for a given occupation, i.e. trade elasticity 6, assumed from Head–Mayer and Donaldson); **η = 1.65** (substitution across occupations in the final good, calibrated to β^D_N); **θ = 1**, so **θ + 1 = 2** is the elasticity of occupational employment shares to occupational wages (assumed from Burstein–Morales–Vogel and Hsieh et al.); **ν = 1.5**, the elasticity of native and immigrant spatial allocation to regional real wages (assumed from Fajgelbaum et al.); **(1 − α)δ = −1.29**, the CZ-level distance elasticity of trade, from Monte–Redding–Rossi-Hansberg on the Commodity Flow Survey. Only ρ and η are calibrated to this paper's own moments; α, θ, ν and the distance elasticity are imported.

## Authors' stated limitations and external-validity notes

- **No aggregate statement.** "These results do not address how immigration affects tradables or nontradables *in the aggregate*, which is the focus of previous literature." The reduced-form estimates are within-region, across-occupation contrasts only.
- **No reduced-form wage estimate**, for the self-selection reason quoted above; wage results are model output.
- **Tradability is measured by national offshorability, not by intra-US trade.** "Given limited data on intracountry trade flows in occupation services, we use measures of offshorability at the national level to capture tradability at the regional level, a correspondence which is imperfect."
- **Bilateral CZ trade by occupation is assumed, not observed.** Trade costs are common across tradable occupations and parameterized by a gravity function in distance, because of "the absence of bilateral cross-CZ trade data by occupation".
- **Closed national economy.** Trade with the rest of the world is abstracted away: "By abstracting away from trade with the rest of the world, we may understate differences between T and N."
- **Shift–share identification caveats** are engaged directly (Adao–Kolesár–Morales, Borusyak–Hull–Jaravel, Goldsmith-Pinkham–Sorkin–Swift) with the sufficient conditions spelled out, plus the Borjas–Freeman–Katz persistence critique tested by placebo and the Jaeger–Ruist–Stuhler short-vs-long-run critique addressed by the three-decade window.
- **Model fit is too good.** "The R2s in the 2SLS regressions are high, suggesting that our reduced-form regressions have a good fit. In order to match the lower R [2] in the data, we would have to introduce random changes in productivities by occupation and regions".
- **Education is exogenous** (fn. 2), and spatial resorting turns out to matter little: "labor reallocation across regions is minor relative to the large initial shock."
- **1990 start year weakens the low-education result.** "When we alternatively change the start year from 1980 to 1990, the differential crowding-out effect for low-education workers in nontradables weakens, but remains strong for high-education workers in nontradables".
