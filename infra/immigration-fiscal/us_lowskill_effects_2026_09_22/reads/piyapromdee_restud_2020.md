**Verdict:** A structurally estimated spatial-equilibrium model of 114 US metro areas (Census 1980/1990/2000 + 2005-7 ACS) in which eight worker types (skill × gender × nativity) choose cities; substitution elasticities are IV-estimated with a shift-share enclave instrument and housing supply with Diamond-style demand-shock × regulation interactions, then the whole equilibrium is re-solved under counterfactual immigration policies — strong on internal consistency and on jointly pricing wages, rents and location choice, weak on identification in the usual shift-share sense (no experiment; the enclave instrument and the Katz–Murphy industry-mix shocks carry all the weight), and it is a **structural simulation**, not a reduced-form causal estimate, for every counterfactual number below.

## Population, period, unit

- **Unit of analysis:** metropolitan statistical area ("city"), 2000 Census MSA definitions, matched back to 1980/1990. "The Census includes 218 MSAs consistently across the three rounds. I focus on the 114 MSAs which have at least 200 full-time and non self-employed of each type of immigrants" (p.6); remaining areas are collapsed into a single outside option, giving 115 locations.
- **Period:** 1980, 1990, 2000 five-percent Census samples plus the pooled 2005-7 ACS (called "the 2007 sample period"), from IPUMS (Ruggles et al. 2010) (p.6).
- **Workers:** "individuals over the age of 18 with 1 to 40 years of potential experience who report positive earnings and worked at least one week in the previous year and not currently enrolled in schools" (p.6). Wage sample further drops the self-employed and anyone below 35 hours/week and 40 weeks/year.
- **Types:** 8 = skill (high = 1-3 years of college or more; low = high-school graduates and dropouts) × gender × nativity. "Immigrants are defined as individuals born abroad" (p.6). Immigrant origin enters through 22 country groups used for the network term.
- **Counterfactual scale:** skill-selective policy raises the high-skill immigrant/native ratio from 0.17 to 0.25, "roughly 46 percent, or around 3.6 million workers in 2007" (p.29). Stock counterfactual is "roughly a 25 percent increase in the stock of immigrants in 2007, or 1.5 million new high skill immigrants and 2.1 million new low skill immigrants" (p.34).

## Design and identification

**Production side.** Three-level nested CES inside a Cobb-Douglas aggregate `Y = A L^α K^(1-α)`, capital perfectly elastic at a common national price. Nest order is **skill (top) → gender → nativity (bottom)**, i.e. natives and immigrants are substituted *within* a skill-gender cell. Skill has two cells only (high/low), justified by Card (2009). The nativity elasticity is allowed to differ by skill level. Estimated iteratively from the bottom nest up, Card–Lemieux (2001) style, because a one-shot nonlinear system "generates numerical difficulties" (p.17).

**Instrument for relative labor supply (labor demand step).** The predicted inflow rate of immigrants built from historical country-specific enclave shares (Altonji and Card, 1991): "I instrument for the relative labor supply ... using the predicted inflow rate of immigrants" (p.17). Controls in the relative-productivity term: gender, education and year fixed effects, log 1980 city population, 1980 mean wage residual, and a Katz–Murphy industry-mix demand index `KM_egct` built from three-digit-industry national hours changes excluding the own city and own state (p.17-18).

**First stage (Table A.5, p.77).** t-statistics on the predicted-inflow coefficient: 11.20 (skill nest), 11.97 (gender nest), −26.45 (high-skill nativity), −16.44 (low-skill nativity); first-stage R² = 0.325 / 0.275 / 0.638 / 0.411. OLS gives wrong-signed or very different elasticities (−8.264 and −15.922 in the top two nests), so the IV moves the estimates a long way.

**Labor supply (location choice).** Two-step BLP/Berry-Levinsohn-Pakes-style discrete choice (McFadden 1973; Berry et al. 1995, 2004; Bayer et al. 2007; Diamond 2016). Step 1 recovers city mean utilities by maximum likelihood over observed location choices with birthplace-state, distance-to-birth-state and co-national-network terms; step 2 regresses first-differenced mean utilities on local real wage and rent using **KM industry-mix shocks** as instruments for local real wages. Hansen J p-value 0.070 in the baseline (Table 1, p.45) — marginal, and the two alternative specifications in the sensitivity analysis are rejected at p<0.05.

**Housing supply.** Land + materials Cobb-Douglas, land price convex in the housing stock; inverse supply elasticity is parameterized as `γ_c = γ^geo · x_c^geo + γ^regu · ln(x_c^regu)` (Saiz 2010 geographic constraint, Gyourko et al. 2008 regulatory index). Identified with **KM shocks interacted with the regulatory constraint**; the geographic-constraint interaction was dropped because over-identification was rejected with it in (p.22, footnote 33). Hansen J p-value 0.207.

**Equilibrium.** 920 fixed points (115 cities × 8 types); welfare from simulated indirect utility for a random draw of 240,000 individuals.

**Out-of-sample validation.** Mariel boatlift replication: +20% Cuban immigrants in Miami, "a 14.7 percent increase of total labor force in Miami in the fixed migration scenario"; after relocation the extra workers fall to 0.93 percent and "the rent increase falls from 14 percent to slightly less than one percent" (p.25), matching Card (1990) on wages and Saiz (2007) on rents.

## What it says about

- **Native wages by skill/education.** Studied, and this is the paper's core. Direction depends entirely on the skill mix of the inflow. A high-skill-selective inflow raises low-skill native wages a lot (+4.3% in gateway cities, +1.2-1.4% elsewhere) and barely moves high-skill native wages (+0.2 to +0.4% gateway, −0.1% elsewhere). A composition-neutral 25% stock increase reverses this: high-skill native wages +1.2%, low-skill native wages −0.4% to −0.1%. The conclusion states flatly: "the results indicate that a large increase in the stock of immigrants has little impact on the wages of natives. The impacts are more highly concentrated on previous immigrants." (p.39) The asymmetry is mechanical in the model — it comes from σ_M being finite and much larger for low-skill (17.9) than high-skill (6.9) cells.
- **Native employment / crowd-out.** **Not studied.** Labor supply is inelastic on the extensive and intensive margin by assumption: "A worker of type z inelastically supplies one unit of labor" (p.11), and "the long run effects on the extensive and intensive margins are likely to be modest (Beerli and Peri, 2016) and so, for tractability, I abstract from labor supply decisions" (p.11-12). Card (2001) and Dustmann et al. (2016b) employment effects are cited in a footnote only. What *is* modelled is **geographic** crowd-out: natives and prior immigrants relocate across cities.
- **Housing prices, rents.** Central. Rents are an equilibrium object with a city-specific inverse supply elasticity averaging 0.683 (range 0.039-1.183). The headline pass-through is roughly unit-elastic: 1.24/1.14 percent rent per percent population under the high-skill counterfactual, 0.89/0.84 under the stock counterfactual, the latter lower because low-skill arrivals push less on housing demand. The paper notes the model's rent response only covers the average house: "The immigration effect on housing, in this case, should be interpreted as the city's average effect." (p.13-14) Rising housing cost is the dominant welfare channel: "Most welfare losses come from rising housing costs." (p.39)
- **Fiscal: taxes, transfers, public services, schooling.** **Not studied.** There is no government in the model — no taxes, no transfers, no public goods, no school costs. The only fiscal sentence in the paper is a policy suggestion in the conclusion: "an appropriate tax scheme on rental income and housing regulations would be an important consideration if policymakers want to redistribute gains/losses more evenly." (p.39)
- **Firms, production, investment, profits.** Firms are perfectly competitive with constant returns and zero profit; there is one tradeable good; capital is "perfectly elastically supplied at a common price κt" (p.10), so the capital stock adjusts fully and the capital share earns a fixed return. Investment, entry, and firm heterogeneity are not modelled. Cities differ only through a scalar productivity `A_ct` and the CES share parameters. **Landlords are the one non-labor claimant that is modelled**, and their gains are what flip the aggregate welfare sign from negative to positive in both counterfactuals.
- **Mechanism the authors claim.** Three superposed CES terms (own-cell competition, gender-cell complementarity, skill-cell complementarity) determine the wage incidence, and imperfect native-immigrant substitution is why incumbent **immigrants**, not natives, absorb most of the loss. Layered on top, internal migration by incumbents partially equalises impacts across cities but transmits them: "an out-migration of workers of a given type may raise the local wages for workers of that type, while reducing the local wages of workers with complementary characteristics." (abstract, p.1) Out-migration is strongest where housing supply is inelastic, productivity and amenities are low, and where the incumbent stock is mobile (natives already outside their birth state; immigrants whose co-national network is dispersed across many cities). A worked example: Atlanta (inverse elasticity 0.47, 48% of workers born out of state) sheds 3% of high-skill male natives while Santa Barbara (inverse elasticity 1.38, 24% out-of-state) sheds 0.7% (p.31).

## Elasticities or parameters a model could transport

Every parameter below is estimated at the **MSA (city) level** on pooled 1980/1990/2000 Census + 2005-7 ACS microdata for workers aged 18+ with 1-40 years of potential experience, with the enclave shift-share or Katz–Murphy industry-mix instruments. That matters for the parent's production term: these are *not* national-level elasticities, and the author explicitly warns they are not comparable to Ottaviano–Peri's.

1. **Elasticity of substitution between natives and foreign-born within a high-skill gender cell, MSA level: σ = 6.925 (SE 0.154).** Estimated on high-skill (some college or more) male and female cells across 114 MSAs × 3 periods.
2. **Elasticity of substitution between natives and foreign-born within a low-skill gender cell, MSA level: σ = 17.870 (SE 0.819).** Low skill = high-school graduates and dropouts. Nearly-perfect substitution; the paper reads this as education quality and English mattering less at the bottom.
3. **Elasticity of substitution between high- and low-skill labor, MSA level: σ_E = 2.193 (SE 0.109).** Two education cells only. The author places this "between the range of estimates at the MSA level provided by Diamond (2016) and Card (2009)" and notes national-level estimates are typically smaller (p.23).
4. **Elasticity of substitution between men and women within a skill cell, unconditional on occupation, MSA level: σ_G = 1.973 (SE 0.167).** Benchmarked against Johnson and Keane (2013)'s unconditional 1.85-2.20.
5. **Nest ordering:** skill above gender above nativity. Reversing skill and gender (Table A.7 col 5) leaves the nativity elasticities identical (17.870, 6.925) and moves σ_E from 2.193 to 3.183 and σ_G from 1.973 to 2.115 — so the parent's nativity-nest number is robust to that reordering, but the skill elasticity is not.
6. **Labor share α** enters the wage equation as `(1/α)·ln A_ct` but is not separately reported as a number in the parsed text.
7. **Inverse housing supply elasticity by MSA:** `γ_c = 0.909·x_c^geo + 0.526·ln(x_c^regu)`, predicted mean 0.683, SD 0.303, min 0.039, max 1.183. Inputs are Saiz (2010) geographic constraints and the Gyourko et al. (2008) regulatory index.
8. **Rent pass-through of population:** 0.84-1.24 percent rent per percent city population, the low end for low-skill-weighted inflows and the free-migration case, the high end for high-skill-weighted inflows with locations fixed. Consistent with Saiz (2007)'s unit elasticity.
9. **Location-choice elasticity w.r.t. local real wage** (elasticity of a small city's population of that type): HS male native 2.086, HS female native 1.018, LS male native 1.323, LS female native 1.725; HS male immigrant 3.839, HS female immigrant 3.825, LS male immigrant 1.228, LS female immigrant 2.964. Implied rent elasticities are these multiplied by λ^r, i.e. −0.305 to −0.626 for natives and −0.442 to −1.305 for immigrants. **Caution:** Table A.7 Panel II shows these collapse by roughly half to two-thirds under two alternative specifications (e.g. HS male native 2.086 → 1.035 → 0.668), and those alternatives fail the Hansen J test, which is the author's stated reason for preferring the larger baseline values.
10. **Housing expenditure share λ^r (calibrated from 2005-7 ACS, not estimated):** 0.30 high-skill natives, 0.30 low-skill natives, 0.34 high-skill immigrants, 0.36 low-skill immigrants. Restricted to vary only by skill × nativity.
11. **Birthplace attachment (natives, 2007):** own-birth-state MSAs are 2.73-2.91× more likely for high-skill natives and 3.41-3.63× for low-skill natives; distance decay −0.578 to −0.662 per 1,000 miles. **Network attachment (immigrants, 2007):** 1.034-1.377 per million prior co-nationals in the MSA, declining steadily from 1990 values of 2.245-2.844.
12. **Landlord composition (2005-7 ACS):** 71 / 18.5 / 8.5 / 2 percent across high-skill natives, low-skill natives, high-skill immigrants, low-skill immigrants. This is the distributional key that turns an aggregate welfare loss into an aggregate gain, and it is a **proxy**: landlords are imputed as sample workers reporting positive rental income and positive house value, since "the actual number of landlords who own rental property is not available" (p.26-27).

## Authors' stated limitations and external-validity notes

- **No labor supply margin.** Employment and hours are fixed by assumption; the author justifies this by the long-run evidence in Beerli and Peri (2016) but acknowledges Card (2001) and Dustmann et al. (2016b) find short-run employment effects in high-density areas (p.11-12, fn.19).
- **No occupations, no age structure.** Occupational substitution is dropped "to keep the number of worker types computationally manageable" (p.9, fn.12); age is dropped because the model is a static long-run equilibrium (p.8, fn.13). Gender is used as a stand-in for occupational segregation.
- **Skill downgrading of immigrants is acknowledged but not modelled**: assigning skill by education may mismeasure immigrants, "e.g. an immigrant with a Bachelor degree may be working in a low skill occupation" (p.9, fn.15).
- **No joint household location decisions** — "the role of joint location decisions for couples is important, this requires estimating parameters for many possible types of households. Therefore, I leave this to future work." (p.11, fn.18)
- **Legal status is unobserved.** The border-wall counterfactual imputes the undocumented inflow from Passel (2005) and its skill/gender mix from Borjas (2017); illegal immigrants' own location choice is "beyond the scope of the paper" (p.38, fn.51). The wall's effect on the *stock* is called ambiguous because of reduced circular migration (p.38, fn.50).
- **Homogeneous housing.** Quality-distribution effects documented by Saiz (2003) are outside the model; rent results are city averages.
- **Instrument validity is only partly clean.** The baseline labor-supply Hansen J p-value is 0.070; the two alternative price specifications are rejected at p<0.05. The geographic-constraint instrument for housing supply had to be dropped after over-identification rejection.
- **Own elasticities are not the national ones and should not be swapped in.** "the elasticities of substitution obtained from the national approach in Ottaviano and Peri (2012), and in this paper measure different relative wage effects (see Dustmann et al. (2016a) for further discussion)" (p.23). Section 6.6 re-runs the counterfactuals with Ottaviano–Peri's national elasticities: "The results from (i) are quite different." (p.39) The author argues the city-level estimates are the internally consistent choice, not that they are the right national numbers.
- **Omitted upside channels.** Footnote 45 (p.33) concedes the skill-selective policy's benefits "could be larger if, for example, there are positive externalities from high skill workers", and that tourism exports and ethnic-restaurant variety are additional unmodelled channels.
