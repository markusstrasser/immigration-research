**Verdict:** A state or local government spends 25 cents to about $1 of each grant dollar, against the 5–10 cents
theory predicts for a lump-sum grant. Two cases there are closer to a released cost than a grant: Missouri school
districts expanded operating budgets when debt retired, leaving tax rates unchanged; and states kept $40 of each $100
of extra income-tax revenue that the 1986 federal base broadening handed them.

Used for: the re-spent share s of a released state-local dollar in arm 4 of `beside_extra.py` (central 0.60, the
midpoint of the grant range; 0.40 from Ladd as a variant).

## Source

James R. Hines, Jr., and Richard H. Thaler, "Anomalies: The Flypaper Effect", Journal of Economic Perspectives 9(4),
Fall 1995, pp. 217–226. URL: https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/pdfplus/10.1257/jep.9.4.217. Read through Exa's crawler on

## Quotes

1. p. 218: a lump-sum grant's share spent on government goods "should be equivalent to the marginal propensity of
   local governments to spend out of income: for states this would be on the order of 5–10 cents on the dollar."
2. p. 218: "For unrestricted block grants, the estimated effects are often closer to 100 percent than to 5 or 10
   percent."
3. p. 219: "The low-end estimates are that a $1 grant increases the spending of the local agency by 25 cents; the
   high-end estimates are clustered around unity."
4. pp. 219–220, Olmsted, Denzau and Roberts (1993) on Missouri school districts' debt retirements: "The authors find
   that operating budgets expand at the same time that debt issues are retired, leaving tax rates unchanged, which
   seems to imply flypaper at work."
5. pp. 219–220, Ladd (1993): "for every $100 of higher state taxes generated by the changing federal definitions of
   income in the 1986 Tax Reform Act, state income tax collections rose by $40."
6. pp. 219–220, Strumpf (1995): "towns have spending elasticities of 0.41 out of tax collections from nonresidents,
   but only 0.27 out of tax collections from residents."
