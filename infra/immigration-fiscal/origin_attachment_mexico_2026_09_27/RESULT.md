# Attachment to the origin state — Mexico vs India/Philippines/China

**Verdict:** Mexico's legal tie to the diaspora is strong and, since the 17 May 2021 reform of Art. 30, unlimited by generation; India's is cut at naturalization. Measured uptake of that tie is small: Mexican votes from abroad reached 184,326 worldwide in 2024, under 2% of the 10.7M Mexico-born adults in the US, and 76,174 votes came from the US in 2018. Among Mexico-born LPRs eligible to naturalize, 62% of the eligible pool has naturalized (ACS 2024 / OHSS), against 74.5% for all origins and 83–85% for India, the Philippines and Vietnam. In held Pew surveys no noncitizen gave "keeping origin citizenship" as the reason for not naturalizing. Identity and contact with Mexico fall steeply by generation; the third generation calls itself "American" about as often as other Hispanics do. Not measured here: US-born persons registered as Mexican nationals, matrícula issuance (SRE site blocked), 2024 US-only vote counts, and the Q5 literature. Lane stopped at the 40-turn cap with these gaps.

## 1. Law

### Mexico (current text, quoted from official sources)

- **Art. 30 A (nationality by birth)** — "II.- Los que nazcan en el extranjero, hijos de padres mexicanos, de madre mexicana o de padre mexicano;" marked "(REFORMADA, D.O.F. 17 DE MAYO DE 2021)". The 1997 text it replaced read "hijos de padres mexicanos nacidos en territorio nacional, de padre mexicano nacido en territorio nacional, o de madre mexicana nacida en territorio nacional". Fraction III (added 20 March 1997) covers children born abroad of Mexicans by naturalization. [SOURCE: https://www.scjn.gob.mx/sites/default/files/cpeum/documento/2021-05/CPEUM-030.pdf, SCJN article history, pp. 3–4; archived `sources/scjn_cpeum_art30.pdf`] Same text at INALI official constitution site [SOURCE: https://constitucionenlenguas.inali.gob.mx/articulos/030, `sources/inali_cpeum_art30.html`].
  - Consequence [INFERENCE from the text]: before May 2021 a US-born child of a Mexico-born parent was Mexican by birth, but that child's own US-born child (grandchild of the migrant) was not, because the parent was not "nacido en territorio nacional". Since the 2021 reform any child born abroad of a Mexican parent is Mexican by birth, so nationality by descent now runs without a generational limit, provided each link is documented.
- **Art. 37 A** — "Ningún mexicano por nacimiento podrá ser privado de su nacionalidad." B: naturalized Mexicans lose nationality "Por adquisición voluntaria de una nacionalidad extranjera … por usar un pasaporte extranjero" or five years' continuous residence abroad. [SOURCE: https://constitucionenlenguas.inali.gob.mx/articulos/037, `sources/inali_cpeum_art37.txt`] This is the 1997 "no pérdida" rule: a Mexico-born migrant who naturalizes in the US keeps Mexican nationality.
- **Art. 32** — "La Ley regulará el ejercicio de los derechos que la legislación mexicana otorga a los mexicanos que posean otra nacionalidad y establecerá normas para evitar conflictos por doble nacionalidad. El ejercicio de los cargos y funciones para los cuales … se requiera ser mexicano por nacimiento, se reserva a quienes tengan esa calidad y no adquieran otra nacionalidad." Active service in the Army in peacetime, and the Navy/Air Force at all times, requires Mexican-by-birth status. [SOURCE: https://constitucionenlenguas.inali.gob.mx/articulos/032, `sources/inali_cpeum_art32.txt`]

### Comparison origins

- **India — Citizenship Act 1955 s.9(1)**: "Any citizen of India who by naturalisation, registration otherwise voluntarily acquires … the citizenship of another country shall, upon such acquisition … cease to be a citizen of India". s.4 (descent): birth abroad must be registered at a consulate, and "no such birth shall be registered unless the parents … declare … that the minor does not hold the passport of another country"; s.4(1A): a minor citizen by descent who also holds another nationality "shall cease to be a citizen of India if he does not renounce the citizenship or nationality of another country" [within six months of turning 18 — continuation not captured in extract]. s.7B(2): an OCI cardholder is not entitled to rights under "section 16 of the Representation of the People Act, 1950 … in regard to registration as a voter", election as President/VP, legislature membership, public employment. [SOURCE: https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/1522/1/a1955-57.pdf, ss.4, 7B, 9, via Exa fetch; archived `sources/in_citizenship_act_1955_exa.txt`]
- **Philippines — RA 9225 (2003)**: s.2 "all Philippine citizens of another country shall be deemed not to have lost their Philippine citizenship under the conditions of this Act"; s.3 retention/re-acquisition by oath; s.5 full civil and political rights incl. overseas absentee voting under RA 9189; candidates for elective office must renounce foreign citizenship. [SOURCE: https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2003/ra_9225_2003.html, ss.2–5; `sources/ph_ra9225.txt`] Unlike Mexico, retention requires an affirmative oath, and it does not create an unlimited descent chain [INFERENCE: RA 9225 text concerns natural-born citizens who naturalized abroad and their minor children].
- **China — Nationality Law 1980**: [GAP] official English text at npc.gov.cn not retrieved (Wayback 429, Exa returned nothing). From the brief's list only; not quoted.

Reading: on every identity and contact item, Mexican-origin attachment falls steeply from the 1st to the 3rd+ generation. Mexicans start higher than other Hispanics in the 1st generation (country-of-origin label 66–71% vs 52–59%; "American" 3–7% vs 8–16%) and, in three of four surveys, remain slightly more origin-identified in the 3rd+ generation (21–32% vs 11–27%; "American" 46–55% vs 51–72%), but the within-generation differences at 2nd/3rd+ are inside sampling error for single surveys. The Mexican 2nd generation has visited Mexico more often (93%) than the immigrants themselves (86%, which includes those who cannot travel). Mexico's legality of dual nationality is known to 70% of Mexican immigrants, yet no noncitizen in 2012 cited loss of origin citizenship as the reason for not naturalizing. [INFERENCE]

## 5. Disconfirmation

Evidence in hand, both directions:
- Against "dual nationality blocks US integration": no Pew noncitizen respondent (2012, n=415) named keeping origin citizenship as the reason not to naturalize; 93–94% of Mexican noncitizen immigrants say they would naturalize if they could; Indian-born, who must give up Indian citizenship, naturalize at a higher rate than Mexican-born, who keep theirs. Identity shifts to "American" by the third generation (46–55%).
- For "origin ties persist": Mexican immigrants report more origin identity than other Hispanic immigrants; 21.5% of Mexican noncitizen immigrants intend to return later in life (NSL2015 q64); 62% vs 74.5% naturalization gap to all origins; by law, citizenship now runs down the generations.
- [GAP] The literature (Mazzolari 2009 on dual-citizenship laws and naturalization; Naujoks; Jones-Correa 2001) was not read — turn cap reached. Next query: `research` MCP search_papers "dual citizenship naturalization Latin America Mazzolari" then fetch_paper and record page-cited findings.

- How many US-born people of Mexican descent hold or have registered Mexican nationality: no published count of consular or Registro Civil birth registrations was found; Pew files carry no item on holding Mexican nationality.
- Voting in Mexican elections by US generation: INE does not report voters by birthplace; any US-born dual national who votes is inside the totals, not separated.
- Remittances by generation: not in the held Pew files.
- Motive for low naturalization beyond self-report; OHSS eligibility omits English and civics requirements.
