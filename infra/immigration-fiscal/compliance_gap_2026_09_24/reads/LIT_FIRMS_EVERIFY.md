**Verdict:** One study tests the operator's mechanism directly. In Georgia, 1995–2005, firms that reported undocumented workers on payroll exited less often (0.5–0.8 points a quarter against a base near 2%), and rivals' undocumented hiring raised exit. That rival effect is insignificant in agriculture, construction and hospitality, and the firms observed are on-books taxpayers, not cash-economy operators (S1). The E-Verify studies show enforcement moving work off the books and toward small, less-compliant firms: Arizona's likely-unauthorized self-employment doubled, and mandates cut the number of large establishments by 2.7% (SE 1.3) (S3, S8). Low-skilled natives gained little or nothing (S4, S8; S5 and S7 disagree). Police-based enforcement lowered establishment and business counts (−2.9% and −6%), mainly by deterring entry (S9, S10). So the literature supports a private cost edge from noncompliance, and it shows that unevenly enforced rules shift activity toward the noncompliant segment. It does not show noncompliant firms driving honest firms out of business in aggregate: removing unauthorized labour reduced business counts, and compliant firms did not fill the gap. Documented coworkers' wage effects are under 1% either way (S2).

## Disconfirmation and grading (both sides, same standard)

**For the operator's claim** (noncompliant hiring gives a cost edge that hurts honest rivals):
1. S1: own-firm survival advantage from reporting undocumented workers (every sector but education and health significant); rival hiring raises exit, with coefficients significant in 4 of 12 sectors. Observational, no instrument for rivals' behaviour; firms observed are on-books payers.
2. S3 and S8: enforcement pushes work toward self-employment (Arizona) and toward small, less-compliant firms, with fewer large establishments (−2.7%, SE 1.3). This shows that uneven compliance moves activity to the noncompliant segment. Difference in differences with few treated states; status inferred.
3. A misclassification bid advantage of 4.9% of project cost (Belman, Bilginsoy, Ormiston and Wenz, ICERES research brief, June 2025, p. 3), which is not immigrant-specific. Read in full in `LIT_COMPLIANCE_COSTS.md` §3a. Corrected 2026-09-25: this line first named the authors as Ormiston, Belman and Hinkel, copying `informal_channel_2026_09_16/RESULT.md` §2a; the brief's own author list is the four above, and Hinkel appears only as a cited reference.

**Against it:**
1. S1 itself: the rival coefficient is insignificant in agriculture, construction and leisure and hospitality, the sectors where the claim is usually made.
2. S9–S11: removing likely-unauthorized workers **lowers** business counts and entry (Secure Communities −2.9% establishments, 287(g) −6% businesses per capita, BDS dynamism down); compliant firms do not fill the gap in aggregate. The same weaknesses apply as on the other side: police enforcement rather than E-Verify, a pre-trend failure in some 287(g) panels, and S11 read only as an abstract.
3. S4 and S8: native low-skilled workers did not gain from mandates (Arizona −4.4 points for white low-skilled men; QWI declines for young, male, less-educated natives). S5 and S7 find some native or competing-worker gains, so the worker-level sign is contested.
4. Repo, not re-read here: the H-2B cut-off natural experiment (lawful guest workers) finds no spillover onto non-participant firms in the same county and industry (`us_lowskill_effects_2026_09_22/reads/amuedo_dorantes_iza_2023.md`, Table 6). This is a lawful channel, so it bounds the labour-cost effect, not the rule-breaking one.

**[GAP]** No US study was found that compares formal and informal (cash) firms competing in the same market, and no study separates compliant from noncompliant firms' outcomes after an enforcement change. S1 is the only firm-level test of the mechanism, and it sees only on-books employers.
