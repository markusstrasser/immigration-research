## [EPOCH 2 verdict, retained] Three findings survive verification against primary records, and they
do not line up the way either side of this argument expects.

**(1) The clustering is real, large, and administratively documented.** 71.0% of FY2024 H-1B
approvals were India-born (USCIS, primary), rising with tenure in status; Gujarati-speaking
Indians are 84× more concentrated in motel management than other immigrant groups (Kerr–Mandorff,
full text). These are not contested numbers.

**(2) The mechanism evidence runs *against* taste-based favouritism, and the single best
discriminating test runs against it hard.** Hegde–Tumlinson tested the thing that actually
separates favouritism from information — whether co-ethnic VC deals *underperform* — across
almost all US venture deals 1991–2010. Co-ethnic pairs exit successfully **more** often (+2.5 to
+3.1pp), and IV/Heckman corrections make the effect *larger*, not smaller. Åslund–Hensvik–Skans
find a 7× co-ethnic hiring gradient in Swedish administrative data and then attribute it to
profit-maximisation, not preference. Banerjee et al. find no caste penalty in Delhi software
callbacks. `[⚠ FRAMING-SENSITIVE]` Against this, Giuliano–Levine–Leonard find US retail manager
own-race hiring effects that they *cannot* explain by networks or efficiency and that they read as
racial preference — so the literature is not unanimous, and the US-specific study is the one that
finds taste.

**(3) The native cost that is actually established is a *visa-program* effect, not a *co-ethnic
network* effect.** Doran–Gelber–Isen, using the H-1B lotteries matched to IRS microdata, find one
additional H-1B crowds out ~1.5 other workers at the firm with a precise zero on patenting.
Bound–Braga–Golden–Khanna's calibration puts US computer-scientist wages 2.8–3.8% higher and
American CS employment 7.0–13.6% higher under a 1994-level foreign-hiring counterfactual — while
total CS employment and output would be lower. That is a real distributional cost to native
computer scientists. It is caused by the *quantity and price of the visa*, and none of these
designs identifies co-ethnic favouritism as the channel.

**(4) The litigation record is split, and the split is about measurement.** A C.D. Cal. jury found
Cognizant liable for a pattern or practice of discrimination against non-South-Asian employees
(Oct 2024). The Seventh Circuit in July 2026 affirmed summary judgment *for* Infosys on a
near-identical theory — because the plaintiffs' expert (David Neumark) inferred ethnicity from
surnames and admitted under deposition that he had no expertise in identifying South Asian names.
Both cases turn on a statistical expert; one expert was admitted and one was excluded. Anyone
citing the Cognizant "8.4×" should know it is the same species of evidence that was thrown out in
`Koehler`, and it has not yet been traced to the trial record here.

**Net:** the coordination is measured; the *favouritism* interpretation is currently
unsupported by the tests designed to detect it; the native cost is real but attaches to the visa
program rather than to ethnic networks. Caste in the US diaspora is measured at 5% self-reported
annual incidence in the one weighted-panel survey, and the widely-cited alternative is a snowball
sample.

# Co-ethnic coordination among Indian immigrants in the US — what is actually measured

Lane: `infra/immigration-fiscal/indian_coordination_2026_09_18/`

## Scope
Is there evidence that Indian immigrants in the US coordinate along ethnic lines (hiring,
promotion, contracting, visa sponsorship, entrepreneurship, caste) beyond what skill and
occupation mix predict, and what does it cost or gain natives? Literature and administrative
records only; no adjudication of motives.

## Steel-man of the benign network account (written before the evidence)

The strongest benign case, stated as its proponents would state it:

1. **Selection, not coordination, explains most of the clustering.** The US admits Indians
   through a channel (H-1B → employment green card) that is occupationally narrow — computer
   occupations dominate. A group admitted through a computing pipeline will be concentrated in
   computing firms, and computing firms staffed from that pipeline will look Indian. No
   preference for co-ethnics is needed to generate the observed shares; the visa category does
   the work. Any group admitted the same way would look the same.

2. **Referral hiring is the cheapest screening technology that exists, and it is ethnically
   autocorrelated by construction.** Employers cannot observe productivity ex ante. A referral
   from an incumbent employee is a costly, reputation-backed signal. Because social networks are
   homophilous everywhere and for everyone, referral-based hiring mechanically reproduces the
   incumbent composition — this is true of Irish police departments, Italian construction firms
   and Mormon finance networks. Calling it favouritism when the group is Indian and
   "social capital" when the group is anything else is the asymmetry, not the finding.

3. **Trust substitutes for contracts where contracts are expensive.** The classic
   Greif/Landa/Rauch argument: co-ethnic trading networks exist because informal sanction within
   a bounded community enforces agreements that courts enforce badly or slowly. This is why
   diaspora networks raise bilateral trade and FDI with the origin country — a *positive*
   externality for the host economy, not a transfer from natives.

4. **Niche entrepreneurship is a human-capital transmission mechanism, not a cartel.** A first
   mover in motels or dry cleaning teaches the business to co-ethnics at near-zero cost; the
   co-ethnic entrant's comparative advantage in that sector is real, not conferred. Kerr and
   Mandorff's model is explicitly of *this* kind: small group + strong network → specialisation
   in a sector where network knowledge matters most. The equilibrium is efficient.

5. **Where co-ethnic matching has been measured with credible identification, it has looked like
   information, not taste.** If co-ethnic hires were favouritism, they would underperform.
   Testing that is the discriminating test, and the null result is the benign account's prediction.

6. **Caste in the US is mostly asserted, rarely measured.** The headline prevalence numbers come
   from a self-selected online convenience sample with no probability frame. The one
   probability-sampled alternative finds far less.

Everything below is the test of this account.

## What the evidence does and does not support so far

**Supported.** The *magnitude* of the concentration is not in dispute and comes from
administrative data: 71% of H-1B approvals are India-born, and the share is higher among
continuing (i.e. renewing, longer-tenured) beneficiaries than among initial ones. Any account of
US tech labour markets that treats national-origin composition as roughly balanced is wrong on
the arithmetic.

**Supported, with one adjudicated instance.** At least one large employer has been found by a
jury, on statistical evidence, to have run a termination process that fell disproportionately on
non-Indian workers. That is a finding of intentional discrimination by an employer, at one firm,
subject to appeal — not a finding about Indian immigrants as a class.

**Not supported by the current evidence: the taste-based mechanism.** The two best-identified
studies in the lane so far both point the other way. Åslund–Hensvik–Skans find a *seven-fold*
co-ethnic hiring gradient — as large as anything alleged in the US tech cases — and then find
that the wage and separation advantages vanish into unobserved worker characteristics, concluding
profit-maximisation rather than preference-based discrimination. Banerjee et al. find no caste
penalty in software-sector callbacks in Delhi itself. `[⚠ FRAMING-SENSITIVE]` The same gradient
supports "networks route information efficiently" and "networks exclude outsiders"; the
discriminating test is *performance of the co-ethnic hire*, and that test has not yet been run in
this lane for the US–India case.

**Not addressed at all yet: the cost to natives.** Nothing above measures a native wage,
employment or displacement effect. The Doran–Gelber–Isen lottery design, Bound–Khanna–Morales,
Glennon and Mayda et al. are located but not yet read.

## What epoch 2 changes

The lane now has the test that actually discriminates between the two accounts, and it was run on
a sample that includes Indians specifically. Hegde–Tumlinson asked whether co-ethnic business
relationships are a costly indulgence or a productive use of social capital, and answered that
co-ethnic VC–founder pairs exit successfully *more* often, with the causal corrections pushing the
estimate up rather than down. If co-ethnic selection in US high-skill markets were rent-seeking,
that is the coefficient that should have been negative. It was not.

The counterweight is Giuliano–Levine–Leonard, which is US, is well identified by manager turnover
within store, and explicitly fails to explain its own results by networks or efficiency. Its
own-race effects are about black/white/Hispanic hiring in retail, not Indians in tech, so
transferring it is an inference — but it is the reason the mechanism question cannot be closed.

On native cost, the lane can now say something concrete and it is not about ethnicity. The H-1B
lottery evidence says the marginal H-1B displaces roughly 1.5 co-workers and buys no measurable
innovation; the calibrated model says the program costs US computer scientists a few percent of
wages and a meaningful share of jobs; the offshoring evidence says restricting it recovers well
under half of those jobs, because the work leaves. These three are in tension about policy and
consistent about incidence: native computer scientists bear a real cost, and the cost is a
function of visa quantity and price, not of who the visa-holders' co-ethnics are.

`Koehler` is the mandatory disconfirmation and it is a sharp one, because it does not merely
report a loss — it explains why the statistical case failed. The entire class of claims in this
area rests on inferring national origin from surnames, and a federal appellate court has now held
that a labour economist doing so without demonstrated expertise or a validated error rate is
inadmissible. That reaches the Cognizant 8.4× figure too, which remains untraced.

## What epoch 3 changes — the central correction

The Cognizant case is the strongest single piece of evidence anyone has that Indian-origin
concentration in US tech imposed a measurable cost on identifiable American workers. Having read
the court's findings rather than the coverage, the mechanism it establishes is **not co-ethnic
solidarity**. It is a firm-level visa-arbitrage strategy, documented in the employer's own
presentations, in which a workforce of stockpiled H-1B holders — 99% of them Indian — was
systematically preferred for deployment because they were cheaper and more mobile, while
higher-graded incumbents were rotated off and benched to termination. The court held the policy
facially neutral *precisely because* it keyed on visa status, and found for the plaintiffs on
disparate impact because visa status is ~99% collinear with Indian national origin at this firm.

The decision-maker in that story is an American multinational with over 40,000 US employees
optimising labour cost, not a network of Indian managers helping their countrymen. Cognizant's own
rotation deck says the quiet part: replace people high on the "value chain" with visa holders "with
a lower grade and experience". A memo that cites Cognizant as proof of ethnic coordination is
citing the right case for the wrong proposition. It is proof that the **visa program** creates a
protected-class-correlated cost advantage that a firm will exploit to the point of illegality.

Two further things in the record cut against the ethnic-network reading. First, the disparity is
not Indian-vs-white: the class representative's father was of Hispanic national origin, and the
trial record shows 72% of Black employees involuntarily terminated against under 1% of Asian
employees — the policy fell on everyone without a visa. Second, the jury made no express findings
at all, so the 8.4× was credited implicitly, and it has never been tested on appeal.

Set against `Koehler`, where a near-identical theory died because the expert inferred ethnicity
from surnames, the litigation record now reads: **when plaintiffs have the employer's own visa
documents and a properly qualified statistician, they win; when they have surname matching, they
lose.**

## Draft memo section

> ### Co-ethnic coordination among Indian immigrants: what the records show
>
> Indian nationals are the overwhelming majority of the US skilled-visa pipeline. USCIS reports
> that **71.0% of H-1B petitions approved in FY2024 (283,397 of 399,395) were for beneficiaries
> born in India**, against 11.7% for China; the India-plus-China share is higher among continuing
> (89%) than initial (71%) employment, so the concentration deepens with tenure in status
> [SOURCE: USCIS, *Characteristics of H-1B Specialty Occupation Workers FY2024*]. In
> self-employment the clustering is sharper still: Gujarati-speaking Indians are **84 times** more
> concentrated in motel management than other immigrant groups, a ratio comparable to Korean dry
> cleaners (34×) [SOURCE: Kerr & Mandorff, HBS WP 16-042]. The commonly repeated claim that
> Indians own a majority of US motels does not appear in that literature; Kerr and Mandorff put it
> at **one-third** as of 2016, citing *The Economist* [UNVERIFIED at the underlying source].
>
> The question is whether this reflects coordination that redistributes from natives, or selection
> plus ordinary network economics. Three lines of evidence bear on it, and they do not agree.
>
> **The test built to detect favouritism does not detect it.** Hegde and Tumlinson examined nearly
> all US venture-backed deals from 1991 to 2010 — 22,000 VC partners, 85,000 startup executives —
> and asked whether co-ethnic VC–founder pairs, which are significantly more likely to form for
> Indian, Chinese, Jewish and South European pairs, subsequently *underperform*. They do not:
> shared ethnicity raises the probability of a successful exit by 3.1 percentage points, 2.5 with
> VC fixed effects, and IV and Heckman corrections make the estimate larger, not smaller. Post-IPO
> market capitalisation and net income are also higher [SOURCE: Hegde & Tumlinson, *Management
> Science*; author summary in *CESifo DICE Report* 2016/3]. Åslund, Hensvik and Nordström Skans
> find a seven-fold co-ethnic hiring gradient in Swedish administrative data covering 70,000
> establishments — immigrant managers hire 43% immigrants against 6% for native managers — and
> conclude that the associated wage and separation advantages vanish into unobserved worker
> quality, so that "the sorting patterns are more likely to be explained by profit-maximizing
> concerns than by preference-based discrimination" [SOURCE: *Journal of Labor Economics* 32(3)].
> In India itself, a 3,160-résumé audit found **no** caste penalty in software-sector callbacks
> [SOURCE: Banerjee, Bertrand, Datta & Mullainathan, *JCE* 37(1)].
>
> **But the best US-based study finds taste, not information.** Giuliano, Levine and Leonard,
> exploiting manager turnover within stores at a large US retail chain, find substantial own-group
> hiring effects and explicitly report that they cannot explain them by social networks or
> efficiency, reading them instead as the racial preferences of managers *and* of job-seekers
> [SOURCE: *JLE* 27(4):589–631]. Its setting is retail and its groups are not Indians, so
> transferring it is an inference — but it is why the mechanism question is not closed.
>
> **The demonstrated cost to natives is a visa-program effect, not a network effect.** Doran,
> Gelber and Isen matched H-1B lottery outcomes to IRS firm microdata and found that one additional
> H-1B **crowds out about 1.5 other workers at the firm**, with a precisely estimated null on
> patenting; their reading is that marginal H-1Bs "crowd out other workers, are paid less than
> alternative workers, and increase the firm's profits" [SOURCE: *JPE* 130(10):2501–2533]. A
> calibrated model puts US computer-scientist wages 2.8–3.8% higher and American CS employment
> 7.0–13.6% higher under a 1994-level foreign-hiring counterfactual, at the cost of 3.8–9.0% lower
> total CS employment [SOURCE: Bound, Braga, Golden & Khanna, *JLE* 33(S1)]. And restriction
> recovers less than half of it, because the work leaves: each visa rejection produces about 0.4
> foreign-affiliate hires, 0.9 at the most globalised firms [SOURCE: Glennon, *Management Science*
> 70(2):907–930].
>
> **The litigation record says the same thing.** In *Palmer v. Cognizant*, a jury found a pattern
> or practice of intentional discrimination against non-South-Asian and non-Indian employees on
> 4 October 2024, and on 5 December 2025 Chief Judge Dolly Gee found disparate impact. The expert
> evidence is now traceable: Dr. Phillip Johnson found non-South-Asian and non-Indian employees
> **8.4 times more likely to be terminated from the bench**, a 75.12-standard-deviation disparity
> [SOURCE: Findings of Fact ¶27, No. CV 17-6848-DMG]. What the court identified as the cause,
> though, was Cognizant's "Visa Readiness" and "Visa Utilization" policies, which it held
> **facially neutral because visa status is not a protected trait** — they bite on national origin
> only because 99% of the firm's visa holders were Indian. Cognizant's own rotation deck directs
> that staff high on the "value chain" be replaced by visa holders "with a lower grade and
> experience"; its own figures show a 30% bench-termination rate for non-visa employees against 3%
> for visa holders [SOURCE: same, Conclusions ¶8, TX-30, TX-38]. This is an American firm
> arbitraging a wage gap the visa creates, and the disparity fell on everyone without a visa —
> including, on the trial record, 72% of the firm's Black employees against under 1% of its Asian
> employees.
>
> **And the same theory loses without the documents.** In *Koehler v. Infosys*, the Seventh
> Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Infosys on 13 July 2026 because the plaintiffs' expert,
> David Neumark, inferred South Asian identity from surnames and conceded he had no expertise in
> doing so: "This alone is fatal to Neumark's qualifications" [SOURCE: No. 25-2272, at 13]. The
> court's example — "D'Souza", a common surname in formerly Portuguese Goa, sitting on Neumark's
> list of "western" names — is a general warning about every surname-based ethnicity statistic in
> this debate.
>
> On caste, the one weighted-panel survey of Indian Americans (n=1,200, YouGov, 2020) finds **5%**
> reporting caste discrimination in the past year, against 30% for skin colour; among Hindus who
> claim a caste identity, 83% report General/upper caste [SOURCE: Carnegie Endowment, *Social
> Realities of Indian Americans*]. The survey's authors caution that this subsample is too small
> for subgroup analysis, note that respondents attribute caste discrimination roughly equally to
> Indians and non-Indians (which they call "a genuine puzzle"), and state that the widely cited
> 2018 alternative "relied on a nonrepresentative snowball sampling method". The Cisco litigation
> that made caste salient is, on the face of the operative complaint, not pleaded as caste at all
> but as religion, ancestry, national origin and race/color, and it remains untried.
>
> [⚠ FRAMING-SENSITIVE] The honest summary is that the concentration is large and documented, the
> favouritism interpretation fails the tests designed to detect it, the native cost is real and
> attaches to visa policy rather than to ethnic networks, and the single adjudicated case of
> illegal discrimination describes a cost-minimising employer rather than an ethnic lobby.

**Correction to the lane verdict — it overreads the Cognizant order in the benign direction.** The December 2025 order decides the *disparate impact* claim, where a facially neutral practice is the legal premise. The same order records that the Phase I jury found a pattern or practice of *intentional* discrimination by race and national origin, that the disparity was "unexplainable on grounds other than race or national origin", and that the court, bound by that finding and on its own reading of the trial evidence, found **no business necessity** for the visa policies (Conclusions ¶13). So "visa-cost arbitrage, not co-ethnic coordination" is not what the record establishes. What it establishes: (a) the instrument was visa policy; (b) a jury found the intent discriminatory; (c) the court rejected cost as a justification; (d) the findings do not identify the decision-makers or their ethnicity, so the record neither shows nor rules out co-ethnic preference by managers.
