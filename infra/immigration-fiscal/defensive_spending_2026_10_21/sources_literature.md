## A. Bowles & Jayadev, guard labor

**Jayadev, A. & Bowles, S. (2006). Guard labor. *Journal of Development Economics* 79(2):328–348. doi:10.1016/j.jdeveco.2006.01.009.**
Read in full from the SFI working-paper version 05-07-030 (July 5th, 2005), which carries the same tables.
[SOURCE: https://sfi-edu.s3.amazonaws.com/sfi-edu/production/uploads/sfi-com/dev/uploads/filer/46/7a/467a0eb5-187b-4c7f-9037-c5be670f3199/05-07-030.pdf]
Methods appendix: Jayadev, "Estimating Guard Labor" [SOURCE: https://repec.umb.edu/RePEc/files/jayadev-egl.pdf].

Definition (quoted): "supervisory labor, private guards, police, judicial and prison employees, military and civilian
employees of the department of defense (and those producing military equipment), the unemployed, and prisoners."
"Ideally we would also include those producing guns for private use, locks, security systems and the like, but we are
not able to do so because of the lack of data." Measured at business-cycle peaks; 2002 uses the 2000 unemployment rate.

Table 1, % of US labor force (table cells, WP version):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Supervisors | 0.8 | 1.4 | 9.8 | 9.9 | 11.7 | 13.2 | 15.7 |
| Guards (police, corrections, judicial, private security) | 0.3 | 0.7 | 0.8 | 0.9 | 1.7 | 1.8 | 2.2 |
| Military | 0.3 | 0.8 | 3.3 | 5.4 | 3.0 | 3.1 | 1.8 |
| Prisoners | 0.4 | 0.2 | 0.2 | 0.3 | 0.3 | 0.5 | 1.5 |
| Unemployed | 4.2 | 3.8 | 4.7 | 4.5 | 6.7 | 6.3 | 4.8 |
| Total | 6.0 | 6.9 | 18.9 | 20.9 | 23.4 | 24.9 | 26.1 |

"About half of those classified as guards (47 percent) were privately employed in 2002, up from 28 percent in 1890."

Table 2, 18 advanced economies, 2002, % of labor force (supervisors / unemployed / military / prisoners / total).
**Guards (police and private security) are NOT in the cross-country table**: "we were able to estimate the numbers of
all types of guard labor except the category of guards (police and private security personnel) for eighteen" countries.
**Japan is not in the sample.** Selected cells: Switzerland 5.8/2.7/1.0/0.1/9.7; Sweden 4.4/5.3/1.1/0.1/10.9;
Italy 2.9/9.7/1.5/0.2/14.3; Canada 8.5/7.2/0.4/0.2/16.3; UK 13.4/5.5/0.7/0.2/19.9; **US 14.9/4.8/1.0/1.4/22.2**;
Greece 9.1/10.3/4.5/0.2/24.0 (highest). US is second-highest of 18; its lead is mainly supervisors (ISCO-88 Major
Group 1 "Legislators, Senior Officials and Managers") and prisoners.

Correlates, 18 countries (quoted): political conflict index (ethno-linguistic fragmentation 1960 + general strikes +
riots 1960–98) r = 0.61; political legitimacy index r = −0.57; union density r = −0.45 (p = .13); post-tax Gini
(Rëhme, smaller sample) r = 0.48 (p = .15); Figure 1 panel label "Gini Goefficient r=0.40". "Per capita GDP, trust
(from Knack and Keefer), and a standard measure of corruption ... yielded low negative correlations (-0.22, -0.14 and
-0.20 respectively)".

Identification (quoted): "None of the statistical associations we have presented are properly identified causal
relationships ... our suggestion that the extent of guard labor be a consequence of the degree of underlying economic
inequality and social conflict is simply a plausible speculation that has not yet been adequately tested."
Holds fixed: nothing; bivariate correlations, n = 18. Note the conflict index bundles ethno-linguistic fragmentation
with strikes and riots, so it cannot separate ethnic division from class conflict.

Welfare argument (quoted, load-bearing for the operator's point): "Suppose for example, that many members of a society
convert to a religion that condones theft, and that in response to the ensuing spread of lawlessness, individuals
purchase more private security services, thereby reallocating some workers from the production of consumption goods to
guard labor. Because their activities would be counted as output whether they were producing consumer goods or safer
streets ... there would be no direct effect of the reallocation of labor effect on measured gross output. But welfare
certainly would be lower as a result of the change." And: "roughly one in five workers are performing guard labor (not
counting prisoners and the unemployed), something like double the number of those producing the investment goods making
up the depreciation of the capital stock." They call the NNP adjustment "more conceptual than practical".
[INFERENCE] The "one in five" is dominated by supervisors (15.7 of ~19.7 pp); police, corrections and private guards
are 2.2 pp. The supervisor share is a DOT/Census "managers and administrators" proxy, so most of the broad number is a
definitional choice, not measured defensive work.

**Bowles, S. & Jayadev, A. (2007). Garrison America. *The Economists' Voice* 4(2):1–7. doi:10.2202/1553-3832.1253.**
[SOURCE: https://sites.santafe.edu/~bowles/GarrisonAmerica2007.pdf, read via Exa highlights]
Quoted: "roughly one in four in the United States economy is now engaged in guard labor ... Since 1890 the guard labor
fraction of the United States labor force has increased four-fold. And in Sweden today the guard labor fraction is less
than half that of the United States." Cross-country range "from less than a tenth of the labor force in Switzerland to a
fifth or more in Spain, the United States, the U.K., and Greece" (excluding police and private security). Gini r = 0.40.
**US states (Figure 4): "Protective Service Occupations as a Percentage of Labor Force by State vs. Economic Inequality
(Gini coefficient for family income). r = 0.51."** Bivariate, no controls; states labelled include New York, California,
Mississippi (high) and North Dakota, Vermont, Iowa (low).
Two disconfirming passages in their own text: "the long term growth in guard labor in the United States cannot be
explained by inequality trends ... because income inequality fell over most of the last century before rising during the
past three decades"; and "the maintenance of order has become a more specialized function over time ... In many
countries, the job of getting people to abide by the rules is not left up to the specialists ... it's not the police who
you have to worry about, but your (equally formidable) fellow pedestrians ... the social norms associated with the term
'social capital' are not strongly associated with guard labor (in our cross national data set the correlation with a
standard measure of trust is negative, but very small: -0.14)."
[INFERENCE] The specialization point matters for the Japan comparison: informal enforcement by neighbours is guard labor
that national accounts and occupation counts do not see, so a low measured guard share can partly reflect unpaid
enforcement rather than less enforcement.
## B. Nordhaus–Tobin regrettable necessities; Leipert; NNW

**Nordhaus, W. & Tobin, J. (1972). Is Growth Obsolete? In *Economic Research: Retrospect and Prospect, Vol. 5:
Economic Growth*, NBER (PDF title verified "Is Growth Obsolete?"; volume page range [TRAINING-DATA: pp. 1–80, not checked]).**
[SOURCE: https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c3621/c3621.pdf — page numbers 509–564 in the reprint pagination]

What counts (quoted): "Some consumer outlays are only instrumental, for example, the costs of commuting to work. Some
government 'purchases' are also of this nature —for example, police services, sanitation services, road maintenance,
national defense. Expenditures on these items are among the necessary overhead costs of a complex industrial
nation-state ... We are making no judgments on such issues in classifying these outlays as intermediate rather than
final uses of resources."
On the welfare reading: "The only judgment we make is that these expenditures yield no direct satisfactions. Even if the
'regrettable' outlays are rational responses to unfavorable shifts in the environment of economic activity, we believe
that a welfare measure, perhaps unlike a production measure, should record such environmental change."
Size (quoted): "We estimate that overhead and regrettable expenses, so far as we have been able to define and measure
them, rose from 8 per cent to 16 per cent of GNP over the period 1929—65 (Table 2, line 4)."
Table 2 cells (billions of 1958 dollars): GNP 1929 203.6, 1965 617.8; line 4a government regrettables 1929 −6.7, 1965
−63.2; line 4b private −10.3, −30.9. [CALCULATION: (6.7+10.3)/203.6 = 8.3%; (63.2+30.9)/617.8 = 15.2%]. Line 5c
"Disamenities" of urbanization, a separate deduction, −12.5 and −34.6 (5.6% of 1965 GNP).
Identification: accounting reclassification, not an estimate of any cause. Defense is the bulk of the government line.

**Leipert, C. (1989). Social Costs of the Economic Process and National Accounts: The Example of Defensive Expenditures.
*Journal of Interdisciplinary Economics* 3(1):27–46. doi:10.1177/02601079X8900300104.**
[SOURCE: https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/jinter/v3y1989i1p27-46.html — abstract only] Quoted: "The ratio of defensive
expenditures in the GNP between 1970 and 1985 increased from 5% to at least 10%." (Federal Republic of Germany.)
**Leipert & Pulselli (2008), "The origins of research on defensive expenditures: a dialog with Christian Leipert",
*Int. J. Design & Nature and Ecodynamics* 3(2):150–161.** [SOURCE: https://iieta.org/download/file/fid/46090, Exa text]
Quoted: "the proportion of all defensive costs in the five categories increased – in relation to the GDP – in the time
period 1970–88 from 6.8% to 11.6% (p. 133) ... 21% of the entire economic growth consists of the increase in defensive
costs." Environmental defensive costs alone "from 1.5% in 1970 to 3.4% in 1988 ... (These figures do not represent
genuine percentages of GDP, but rather a ratio, since certain cases of double accounting could not be avoided.)" The six
spheres: "the environment, transport, housing, security, health, and work." The security sphere's own share is in
Leipert's 1989 book (Die heimlichen Kosten des Fortschritts, pp. 131–309) [GAP: not obtained; security sub-share unknown].
Identification: accounting classification by function; "defensive" is a judgment about purpose, not an estimate of
what the economy would produce without the underlying damage.

## C. Allocation of talent

**Murphy, K. M., Shleifer, A. & Vishny, R. W. (1991). The Allocation of Talent: Implications for Growth. *QJE* 106(2):503–530.
doi:10.2307/2937945.** Read from NBER WP 3530 (1990) [SOURCE: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w3530/w3530.pdf];
WP cells may differ slightly from the QJE tables [UNVERIFIED against QJE].
Design: cross-section, growth of real GDP per capita 1970–85 on the 1970 share of college enrollment in law and in
engineering (UNESCO), 91 countries and the 55 with more than 10,000 college students.
WP Table III (controls: 1960 GDP only), coefficient (SE): Engineering 0.054 (0.027) all / 0.125 (0.037) large-enrollment;
Law −0.031 (0.025) / −0.065 (0.049); R² 0.09 / 0.23. Table IV (adds investment, primary enrollment, government
consumption, revolutions and coups): Engineering −0.010 (0.023) / 0.054 (0.034); Law −0.024 (0.020) / **−0.078 (0.040)**;
R² 0.47 / 0.56.
Quoted: "If an extra 10 percent of enrollment was in engineering ... the growth rate would rise 0.5 percent per year.
If an extra 10 percent were in law ... growth would fall 0.3 percent per year." "Of course, we cannot interpret these
relationships as structural, since law and engineering enrollments might be correlated with other sources of growth."
Identified? **No.** Cross-country correlation; the law coefficient is insignificant in three of four columns.

**Baumol, W. J. (1990). Entrepreneurship: Productive, Unproductive, and Destructive. *JPE* 98(5):893–921.**
[SOURCE: https://devf21.classes.ryansafner.com/readings/Baumol-1990.pdf]
Claim (abstract): "the productive contribution of the society's entrepreneurial activities varies much more because of
their allocation between productive activities such as innovation and largely unproductive activities such as rent seeking
or organized crime. This allocation is heavily influenced by the relative payoffs society offers". Evidence: "Historical
evidence from ancient Rome, early China, and the Middle Ages and Renaissance in Europe". Self-assessment: "none of the
material approaches anything that constitutes a formal test of a hypothesis, much less a rigorous 'proof.'"
Japan passage (quoted): "The fact that Japan has far fewer lawyers relative to population and far fewer lawsuits on
economic issues is often cited as a distinct advantage ... The difference is often ascribed to national character ...
But closer inspection reveals that there are also other influences. While in the United States legal institutions such
as trebled damages provide a rich incentive for one firm to sue another ... in Japan ... any firm undertaking to sue
another on antitrust grounds must first apply for permission from the Japan Fair Trade Commission. But such permission is
rarely given". Identified? No; historical illustration. Its Japan point is that rules, not culture, set litigation.

**Hsieh, C.-T., Hurst, E., Jones, C. I. & Klenow, P. J. (2019). The Allocation of Talent and U.S. Economic Growth.
*Econometrica* 87(5):1439–1474. doi:10.3982/ECTA11427.** [SOURCE: https://doi.org/10.3982/ecta11427 abstract;
NBER WP 18693 (2013) read in full, https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w18693/w18693.pdf]
Published abstract (quoted): "In 1960, 94 percent of doctors and lawyers were white men. By 2010, the fraction was just
62 percent ... Across our various specifications, between 20% and 40% of growth in aggregate market output per person can
be explained by the improved allocation of talent." The 2013 WP said "15 to 20 percent of growth in aggregate output per
worker since 1960" (WP Table 2: 20.4% τ_h case, 15.9% τ_w case).
Identification: **model-imputed**. A Roy model is calibrated so that occupational frictions (τ) are backed out from
observed occupation and wage gaps under the assumption that "the innate talent for these professions is unlikely to have
changed differently across groups"; the growth share is a counterfactual "holding the A's ... φ's ... and q's ... constant
over time and letting the τ's change." Nothing about rent-seeking vs productive occupations is estimated; the mechanism is
misallocation from barriers to women and Black men.

**Guard-labor opportunity cost.** Bowles–Jayadev (§A) give the conceptual case: guard labor is counted as output, so
GDP does not fall when workers move from goods to guarding, while welfare does. Their measure of the cost is the labor
itself (a headcount share), i.e. implicitly the wage bill; they propose no externality wedge beyond it.

**Yamagishi, T. & Yamagishi, M. (1994). Trust and commitment in the United States and Japan. *Motivation and Emotion*
18(2):129–166 [TRAINING-DATA: volume/pages]. doi:10.1007/BF02249397.** [SOURCE: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02249397, abstract]
Quoted: "A distinction is proposed between trust as a cognitive bias in the evaluation of incomplete information about the
(potential) interaction partner and assurance as a perception of the incentive structure that leads the interaction partner
to act cooperatively ... The results of a cross-national questionnaire survey (with 1,136 Japanese and 501 American
respondents) ... indicate that, in comparison to Japanese respondents, American respondents are more trusting of other
people in general, consider reputation more important, and consider themselves more honest and fair. In contrast, Japanese
respondents see more utility in dealing with others through personal relations." Design: survey comparison, not causal.
[INFERENCE] Japanese order rests on assurance (monitoring, sanctions, committed relations), which is itself a form of
enforcement, much of it unpaid and so invisible to occupation counts.

**(ii) Lost property.** **West, M. D. (2003). Losers: Recovering Lost Property in Japan and the United States.
*Law & Society Review* 37(2):369–424 [TRAINING-DATA: pages]. doi:10.1111/1540-5893.3702007** [SOURCE: abstract https://doi.org/10.1111/1540-5893.3702007;
SSRN 316119]. Quoted: "Folk legend attributes Japanese success to honesty and other-regarding preferences ... relying in part
on analysis of official data, history, and statutes, as well as surveys, interviews, and 'lost-wallet' experiments conducted
in New York and Tokyo, I discuss another possible reason ...: legal institutions that efficiently and predictably allocate
and enforce possessory rights. These recognized, centuries-old rules mesh with norms, institutional structures, and economic
incentives ... and yield more lost-property recovery than altruism alone." Mechanism as summarised by a secondary review
[UNVERIFIED, mutantfrog.com 2009 quoting a review]: finders must hand items to police (koban) or face prosecution; the
owner pays the finder a fee (5–20% of value under the Lost Property Law); unclaimed items go to the finder after six months
(since shortened to three). [GAP: West's wallet/phone counts for Tokyo vs New York not obtained.]

## E. Bove–Elia–Ferraresi 2021 and US analogues

**Bove, V., Elia, L. & Ferraresi, M. (2023). Immigration, Fear of Crime, and Public Spending on Security. *JLEO* 39(1):235–280.
doi:10.1093/jleo/ewab021 (online 2021).** [SOURCE: https://academic.oup.com/jleo/article/39/1/235/6423595; working-paper text
via Exa] Confirmed passage: "We find that immigration increases spending on police protection, such as enhanced police
presence and surveillance. On average, the amount of spending allocated to local security increases by 0.12-0.30 percentage
points for one point increase in the share of immigrants. This is a very large effect, as municipalities spend on average
about 4.3% of their budget on security". Design: 7,000+ Italian municipalities 2003–2015; shift-share on EU-enlargement
inflows plus Fascist-era migration instrument. Abstract adds: "immigration is associated to fear of future crimes rather than
the actual probability of being victim of a crime", and culturally distant immigration "corresponds to a deterioration in
civic cooperation and interpersonal trust". Holds fixed: municipality and year effects; outcome is a budget share, not
dollars per capita. [INFERENCE] The mechanism it identifies runs through fear, not crime, which the account's fear row
(φ share of WTP excess) already prices for crime-driven fear.

**US analogues.**
