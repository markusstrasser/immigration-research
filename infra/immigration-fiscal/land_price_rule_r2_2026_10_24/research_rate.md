### F3. OECD *Measuring Capital* manual (2009, 2nd ed.) — land user cost = real rate × P with expected real holding gain set to ZERO
[SOURCE: OECD (2009) Measuring Capital, ISBN 978-92-64-02563-9; cached `land_price_rule_r2_2026_10_24/_cache/sources/oecd_measuring_capital_2009.pdf` (from unstats.un.org/unsd/nationalaccount/docs/oecd-capital-e.pdf)]
- p.157, §18.1.2 "User costs of land": "Because land is a non-produced asset, there is no depreciation element in its user costs. User costs of land are thus composed of two elements: the real return to capital and real holding gains or losses. However, land is special insofar as land markets, in particular for residential land, are not free from speculative bubbles".
- p.158–159: "Steep upward movements in land prices that are simply extrapolated from past observations will tend to generate negative user cost expressions unless an explicit risk premium is considered … In sum, both conceptual and practical considerations lead us to suggest that, if there is no possibility to estimate a land-specific risk premium, the expected real holding gains and losses for land should be set to equal zero when user costs for land are calculated – and this is true whether endogenous, mixed or exogenous methods are applied to estimate the rate of return."
- p.159, eq. (30): "The user cost expression for land has a fairly simple form because there is no depreciation component and, if one follows the conclusion above, real holding gains and losses are set to zero." U_L,t = (1+ρt) r*_t P_L,t W_L,t (r* = real rate; ρ = inflation timing adjustment).
- p.145, ch.16 note 2: "there is one particular asset, land, for which it is always recommended to set real holding gains to zero or to some long-run value rather than using the ex-post movements of real land prices. … there are both practical and conceptual reasons to stay away from estimating asset-specific expected holding gains in the case of land."
- p.141–142, §16.3 (government): imputing a return on government capital is "for analytical purposes only"; scope "would limit the scope of government assets for which a net return is estimated, to produced assets (including inventories) plus land associated with structures used by government"; real rate justified "by an opportunity cost argument". p.144: OECD SRTP table, US 2.4% (preferred w=0.5, e=1), range 1.4–3.0%; Moore–Boardman et al. central 3.5% (2.0–5.0%).
Reading: the national-accounts/productivity standard for land is exactly r*·P in real terms with expected real revaluation = 0 (or "some long-run value"). This is the r1 construct (P × 2.081% real, no growth term). The reviewer's claim that r·P with no growth term "matches neither consistent construct" is contradicted by the OECD manual: it is the OECD-recommended user cost in real terms. It also matches the case's structure rule (r × current-cost stock, no relative-price term).

### F4. BLS multifactor productivity — land in capital services with a smoothed ex-post nominal revaluation term
[SOURCE: BLS Handbook of Methods, "Office of Productivity and Technology: Calculation", https://www.bls.gov/opub/hom/opt/calculation.htm (2025-09-30), text retrieved via Exa crawl]
- "The rental price of capital, or the user cost of capital, is the opportunity cost of holding and using capital assets for a period of time. … The equilibrium rental price is equal to the foregone earnings invested in the asset plus the decline in the asset's value."
- "The asset price term is the product of the asset price (P) from the previous period and the industry-specific rate of return on capital plus the product of the asset price from the previous period and the average rate of economic depreciation of the asset in a particular industry minus the change in the 3-year moving average of the asset price."
- Asset groups: "equipment, structures, intellectual property products, rental residential capital, land".
[SOURCE: BLS Handbook of Methods, "Major sector productivity: Calculation", https://www.bls.gov/opub/hom/msp/calculation.htm] eq.(19) Rental price = [P·R + P·D − Δp]·(1−uz−k)/(1−u), "R = internal rate of return … ∆p = capital gain term representing the price change of the asset over the prior three years".
[SOURCE: BLS "Overview of Capital Inputs for the BLS MFP Measures", 2017-06-02 (bls.gov/productivity/technical-notes/methods-capital-inputs-total-factor-productivity-2017.pdf; curl got an HTML stub, text via Exa crawl)] "The BLS rental price estimates take account of the rate of return to capital, the rate of economic depreciation, the rate of nominal appreciation of assets and also the tax treatment of assets. … weights did not change much except for land, which does not have a depreciation term." Land data: farm land from USDA, nonfarm land from IRS book values (prod3 technical note).
Reading [INFERENCE]: BLS land rental = P·(i_nominal − smoothed realised nominal land inflation) = P·(r_real − g_real,realised-smoothed). So BLS is the (r−g) form with g = 3-yr average realised price change — but r is an ENDOGENOUS internal rate (capital income residual), which absorbs the revaluation: a high ΔP raises the IRR that clears capital income. The BLS construct is a production-weighting device, not an exogenous-rate opportunity cost. The OECD manual (F3) explicitly criticises extrapolated ex-post land gains (negative user costs) and recommends zero.
[GAP] BLS does not publish a land-specific discussion of why ex-post smoothing is used; Harper (1999) Int. Stat. Rev. is the cited methods paper — not opened.

### F5. Diewert (2005), "Issues in the Measurement of Capital Services, Depreciation, Asset Price Changes, and Interest Rates" (NBER CRIW vol. *Measuring Capital in the New Economy*, ch.12, pp.479–556)
[SOURCE: https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c10628/c10628.pdf, cached `_cache/sources/diewert_2005_nber_c10628.pdf`]
- p.491, three ways to set the rate: "Use the ex post rate of return that will just make the sum of the user costs exhaust the gross operating surplus … This approach (incorrectly in our view) transforms pure profits (or losses) into a change in the opportunity cost of financial capital." / "Take a fixed real interest rate and add to it actual ex post consumer price inflation or anticipated consumer price inflation." Diewert picks the third: "choose the real rate of return to be 4 percent per annum" (ABS practice; "long-run observed economywide real rates of return for most … OECD countries, which fall in the 3 to 5 percent range").
- p.493: for anticipated asset price change, "we will simply smooth the observed ex post new asset rates of price change and use these smoothed rates as our estimates of anticipated rates"; fn 26: "different analysts may choose different smoothing methods, so there may be a problem of a lack of reproducibility".
- p.509 (model 2): "we assume that producers use the current consumer price index (CPI) inflation rate as estimators of anticipated asset inflation rates. This model turns out to be equivalent to the constant real interest rate model that is frequently used by statistical agencies." p.510: "while model 2 is acceptable, we prefer model 3 [smoothed asset inflation], since this model includes the effects of anticipated obsolescence".
Reading: the user cost of a non-depreciating asset is P·(r* − expected real asset price change). Expected change options: realised (model 1, volatile), CPI = zero real gain (model 2, "frequently used by statistical agencies"), or smoothed asset-specific (model 3). Diewert's reason to prefer model 3 is *obsolescence of machinery* (systematic real price declines) — not land. Chapter does not treat land. ### F6. UK appraisal practice — land at market value up front, residual value at the end; usual assumption: land "unaltered in real terms"
[SOURCE: HEFCE 2003/17c investment appraisal guide, Annex C "Technical issues" (built on HM Treasury Green Book 2003), https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/id/eprint/11522/2/03_17c.pdf, cached `_cache/sources/hefce_appraisal_03_17c.pdf`]
- para 5: "the value of an asset should be included even when it is already in the institution's ownership. Such an asset could be used for other purposes or sold if it were not employed in the project … This is known as the opportunity cost. For property, the opportunity cost is normally taken as the market value or the value in alternative use, whichever is higher."
- para 7: "On the other hand the site value may remain constant, or even appreciate. Accordingly the usual assumption is that the value of the site remains unaltered in real terms. Actual increases in land prices should not normally be counted as benefits, as they would have occurred even if the project had not taken place; they would be realised equally by the 'do nothing' option."
- para 46: "The recommended discount rate is 3.5 per cent. It is a real rate".
[SOURCE: HM Treasury, *The Green Book* (2022, links updated 2024), assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6645c709bd01f5ed32793cbc/Green_Book_2022__updated_links_.pdf, cached `_cache/sources/uk_green_book_2022.pdf`]
- p.53 §8.2: "To estimate the social costs of a proposal, practitioners should start from the market prices of the goods and services used as inputs. For an energy generation project, for example, practitioners might start by taking the market price of the necessary land".
- p.58 §8.32: "An asset's residual value or liability at the end of the appraisal period should be included to reflect its opportunity cost. Residual values do not depend on the actual sale of an asset."
- pp.38–39 §6.45: "land has become relatively more expensive over time, and so the price of land has generally seen increases that are 'above inflation'"; §6.47: costs "should be estimated in real prices" with nominal forecasts deflated by the GDP deflator — i.e. the 2022 Green Book would let an expected relative land-price rise enter the residual value.
Algebra [CALCULATION, standard]: land bought at P, residual value P(1+g)^T at T, real discount r: PV land cost = P[1 − ((1+g)/(1+r))^T]; as T→∞ the equivalent annual cost is (r−g)P/(1+g) ≈ (r−g)P. With the "unaltered in real terms" convention (g=0) it is exactly rP — the r1 construct. So CBA practice is the fiscal (r−g)P form, and the long-standing default sets g=0.

## Recommendation [INFERENCE]
Keep **r·P with expected real holding gain = 0** at the case's consumption rate (2.081%) as the central construct. Key reason: the case's object is a *budget* saving, and an unrealised land holding gain never reaches the budget — public road, park and school land is not sold, so never having bought it saves the financing flow r·P on today's market value in perpetuity, with nothing offset by appreciation the government would never cash. This is also exactly the OECD-recommended land user cost (p.159) and the long-standing UK appraisal default ("unaltered in real terms"), and it matches the case's structures rule (r × current-cost stock, no relative-price term) — subtracting g for land alone while ignoring the rising relative price of structures would be asymmetric. The reviewer's premise that r·P with no growth term "matches neither consistent construct" is contradicted by OECD 2009: it is the standard real user cost, i.e. the (r−g)P family with expected *real* g = 0.
Against (r−g)P at a risk-free rate: it books the land's expected risk premium as a free budget gain (with g ≈ 1%, (r−g) = 1.08% < half the measured land rent yield), and the OECD manual rejects extrapolated land gains because they drive user costs toward or below zero (p.158).
Report the **market-rent reading as a beside arm** (resource view): R ≈ 3.0% × P (range 2.5–4.5%), which is above r·P because market land prices discount rents at a risky rate (P = R/(r_m − g)); and (r−g)P at g = 1.0% (≈1.08%) as the low sensitivity. Do not use GDM's 5–9%: its 5% is an unsourced (likely nominal) public-funds rate and its 9% a private investor's return target.
Numbers: **g central 1.0%/yr, range 0.0–2.0%** (CPI-deflated, long-run); **rent yield central 3.0%, range 2.5–4.5%**.
