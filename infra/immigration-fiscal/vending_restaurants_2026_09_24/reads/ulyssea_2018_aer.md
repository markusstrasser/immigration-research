**Verdict:** The standard quantitative model of informal firms competing with formal ones (Brazil): 41.9% of informal firms are "parasites" that could survive formally but stay informal for the cost advantage, 48.8% could survive only informally; nearly eradicating informal firms by enforcement raises formal incumbents' value 7.4% and output 3.2% but lowers welfare 6.7% and low-skill wages 8.5%. Strong method for its purpose (structural equilibrium model estimated on matched data), but every number is a model counterfactual for Brazil, not an estimate for US vending. Grade: B as a model; not direct evidence on Los Angeles.

Source: Gabriel Ulyssea, "Firms, Informality, and Development: Theory and Evidence from Brazil", *American Economic Review* 108(8), August 2018: 2015–2047, doi:10.1257/aer.20141745 (version of record from UCL Discovery).

## Population, period, unit

Brazilian urban firms outside agriculture; ECINF 2003 informal-firm survey and RAIS formal employer-employee records; unit: firm.

## Design

Melitz-type equilibrium model; firms choose formal registration (extensive margin) and off-the-books hiring (intensive margin); formal and informal firms sell a homogeneous good at the same price, so informal firms compete with formal ones through output and labor markets. Estimated by simulated minimum distance; four counterfactual policies.

## What it says about restaurants or formal businesses

The operator's mechanism exists in the model: formal firms do lose from informal competitors, and "low-productivity formal firms benefit the most, which indicates that they are the ones most directly affected by the competition from informal firms" (p. 2042). The same model says removing that competition by enforcement costs more than it gains in welfare, because it also removes the survival-only firms and cuts low-skill wages. [INFERENCE] For Los Angeles the analogue of "formal incumbents" is small, low-margin restaurants near vending; the model's sign carries over, its magnitudes do not (Brazil's informal share of firms is 69%, with payroll taxes and entry costs far above US levels).

## Stated limitations

The enforcement welfare loss "should be seen as an underestimation … as the experiment makes two strong assumptions: (i) all tax revenues are directly rebated to households, with no resources lost; and (ii) there is no cost of implementing greater enforcement" (p. 2044).
