**Verdict:** A careful urban-economics paper on why gourmet food trucks grew (mobile technology relaxing a location-information friction) and how much variety they add in Washington DC; it does not estimate any effect of food trucks on restaurant sales, entry, exit or jobs, so it cannot support either side of the displacement question. Grade: B for its own questions (variety, location choice); not evidence on restaurant displacement.

## Population, period, unit

Gourmet food trucks in Washington DC, 2010–2013 (daily locations from Twitter feeds); national food-truck revenue and Google search series; unit: truck-day locations.

## Design

Model of a restaurateur choosing truck vs fixed store under consumer uncertainty about location; tests using Google Trends timing and conditional-logit location choice (trucks avoid recently visited spots = taste for variety); counterfactual with fixed locations.

## What it says about restaurants or formal businesses

Frames trucks as a differentiated product: their advantage over restaurants is mobility that serves "different consumers each day by switching locations" (p. 2). It acknowledges "opposition from those who are negatively affected by food trucks" (p. 2) but measures no restaurant outcome. Its welfare claim is for consumers (variety), not a net figure including restaurants [INFERENCE]. Its population is licensed gourmet trucks in DC, not unlicensed sidewalk vendors in LA.

## Stated limitations

The location-choice model ignores competition among trucks: "We ignore any possible agglomeration or competition effects in the location choice" (p. 24, fn).
